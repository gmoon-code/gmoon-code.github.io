Ye Eun Moon

Hello, I'm Ye Eun Moon, a science educator in South Korea preparing for doctoral study. I am interested in how science curricula influence what students are invited to investigate and whose knowledge can contribute to that work. When a student questions a familiar model or suggests examining different evidence, what happens next depends partly on how teachers and classmates respond. I hope to study those decisions and explore how students and teachers might develop scientific inquiries together.

My questions about participation are inseparable from concerns about racism, colonial histories, and inequalities involving gender, sexuality, class, and language. These conditions can influence whose knowledge is recognized and which experiences and questions find room in science education. I hope to contribute to anti-racist, anti-colonial, and queer- and trans-inclusive curricula, with biological sex and human variation offering one area for further inquiry. My scoping review of multilingual science education and classroom action research in AP Biology have given me experience examining how scientific reasoning is expressed and interpreted. I have gathered those studies, curriculum work, and learning tools here, alongside some of my interests beyond school.

Research

My scoping review examines how 20 studies interpreted multilingual students' scientific work. In a separate classroom action research inquiry, I followed 28 AP Biology students' explanations across assessments, feedback, and reconstruction. Both inquiries have made me more careful about the conclusions I draw from a single response, a concern that informs the broader questions about participation and curriculum I hope to pursue.

From the research records

Classroom action research · AP Biology · 28 students

What does an explanation after correction establish?

In my classroom inquiry, ten students reconstructed a photosynthesis mechanism after correcting an assessment response. Comparing the original explanations with their later reconstructions revealed different patterns. Some students added missing connections or repaired contradictions, while others left out relationships they had previously expressed. Those differences informed more specific questions about what each student could explain and how I should interpret the available evidence.

One comparison from the study

In S20's original explanation, connections involving the proton gradient, ATP, and NADPH were present. The subsequent reconstruction omitted those relationships.
