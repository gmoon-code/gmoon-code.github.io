The Story Method

I developed The Story Method as an AP Biology learning environment in which students follow scientific questions through fictional places, characters, and events. Each journey introduces ideas that return through recall and application activities. In the Unit 1 example below, an unexpected experimental result gives learners a reason to distinguish what they observed from what their evidence can explain. Whether learners can use that reasoning in an unfamiliar experiment remains a question for future evaluation.

Unit 1 journey example

In Unit 1, The Evidence Lockdown unfolds across nine locations in a fictional laboratory. A reporter-cell experiment has produced an unexpectedly bright signal, and Dr. Rowan Lee begins with the observation itself. As the journey proceeds, the learner has to consider what might have caused the difference and which comparisons would help investigate it.

The Observation Window

Two trays of reporter cells sit behind glass. One glows faintly; the other is unexpectedly bright. Rowan asks the learner to describe what is visible before giving the result a cause.

“First we describe what we can actually see.”

A short line from the first scene of The Evidence Lockdown.

  1. Observation Window

    Recognize a difference in brightness. A difference is an observation, and the cause still needs investigating.

  2. Hypothesis Doors

    Consider predictions for a treatment effect and for no effect, where formal hypothesis testing is appropriate.

  3. Control Benches

    Separate the comparison group from conditions held constant. They play different roles in deciding what an experiment can show.

Observation and experimental inference

The same reporter-cell observation returns across several scenes, giving learners a chance to reconsider it as the scientific problem develops. They first describe the difference in brightness, then compare possible causes and the controls needed to investigate them. As variability and sampling uncertainty enter the story, the question changes from what appears to have happened to what the evidence can justify. The Review and Challenge activities return to that reasoning through further questions.

Design decisions inside the journey

The physical journey advances from one room to the next, although its scientific problem sometimes asks the learner to reconsider an earlier assumption. At the Observation Window, arrows on the evidence board point both forward and back. The narration reinforces the possibility of returning to an earlier question when new evidence warrants it, even within a story arranged as a route through nine locations.

At the Control Benches, learners encounter a treatment group, a comparison group, and positive and negative controls. Moving into the Variable Control Panel, they turn from choosing useful comparisons to identifying the manipulated factor, measured response, and conditions held comparable. Giving these decisions separate places helps organize the scientific vocabulary, although the imagery introduces a possible confusion of its own. A learner might come away expecting every investigation to require all four benches, or confuse a controlled variable with the group used for comparison.

To examine that risk, I could present an unfamiliar experimental design and ask which comparisons it requires, what each control would establish, and which factors must remain comparable. Explaining those decisions would provide evidence of the learner's reasoning beyond recognition of a familiar laboratory location. The released journey record (opens in a new tab) contains the scenes and checkpoints behind these design decisions.

Continuity

One experimental observation remains in view while the learner develops competing predictions and considers what to measure.

Distinct decisions

The Control Benches and Variable Control Panel locate different experimental-design questions in adjacent rooms.

Design tension

Memorable locations can support recall of terminology while also encouraging a simplified account of scientific procedure.

Evaluation

An unfamiliar experiment could reveal whether learners can justify a comparison and revise a claim without the original story cues. That comparison has not been conducted.

Returning to the science through practice

The journey introduces scientific ideas that the application revisits through recall and review questions. Learners can request a hint before seeing an answer, and Quick Recall records the attempt as assisted even after the page is reloaded or another unit is opened. Review also offers a hint before revealing its answer. These records let learners distinguish how they completed a question and decide what to revisit.

A Unit 1 application question

The Unit 1 Challenge Lab also includes the following practice-only question, which moves from a fictional laboratory setting to a numerical result about photosynthesis.

A plant treatment group increases mean oxygen production from 12.0 to 15.0 µmol O₂/m²/s, while a water-control group changes from 12.1 to 12.3. Write one evidence statement supporting the claim that the treatment increased photosynthetic activity, then write a separate reasoning statement connecting the evidence to the biological process.

The treatment group's reported change is 3.0 µmol O₂/m²/s, compared with 0.2 in the control. Students must then explain why oxygen production is relevant to photosynthesis and what the comparison supports. The question appears in the published Unit 1 practice content (opens in a new tab); these are constructed values for learning, with no claim about classroom outcomes.

To investigate how learners use the scientific reasoning introduced in a journey, I could compare their explanations immediately afterward with their responses to an unfamiliar experiment. That comparison would show which relationships they could explain without the story's cues, while practice records could help contextualize the responses. This evaluation has not yet been conducted.

The application repository and release documentation (opens in a new tab) describe how scenes, retrieval prompts, and challenges are packaged and checked across the eight released units. The details below are available for readers interested in the release process.

How the release is checked

The GitHub Pages release is assembled from the student application and curriculum records without development files or source documents. Static checks examine the package across all eight units, including scenes, retrieval prompts, review targets, and application questions. The workflow requires two identical builds and deploys the commit that passed the checks.

These release checks verify the material packaged in the tested build. Whether learners can apply the scientific reasoning developed through the journeys remains a separate question for evaluation.
