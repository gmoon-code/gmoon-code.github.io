Research

Imagine a biology class examining a simplified diagram that traces human sex development from chromosomes to reproductive anatomy. A student asks where differences in hormone responses fit into that pathway. The teacher could explain the missing processes and continue with the lesson, or invite students to examine what the diagram leaves out and decide which relationships a more adequate model should represent.

Teachers make these decisions within curricular and disciplinary expectations, choosing which questions to pursue and whether students' explanations can shape the work that follows. These judgments take place within institutions and histories marked by racism, colonialism, and inequalities connected to gender, sexuality, social class, and language. My interest in justice-oriented science education includes how students can participate in evaluating scientific representations and the grounds on which their contributions are recognized. Scholarship on epistemic agency and rightful presence helps me examine these relationships alongside the scientific reasoning involved.

Research projects

Multilingual science education scoping review

I examined 20 empirical studies retrieved through ERIC to consider how researchers inferred multilingual students’ science understanding from written responses, classroom interaction, and visual work. I also examined the reporting of language options and participation conditions, since these can affect what a student’s contribution allows a researcher to conclude.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I studied students’ explanations across assessments and two adjustments to the feedback routine. For ten students whose Unit 3 explanations did not meet the study's completeness criteria, I compared the original responses with later closed-resource reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions developed fuller causal pathways, while others omitted relationships previously expressed. Comparing these records changed the questions I would ask in subsequent assessment, although those follow-up questions were not administered in the reported inquiry.

A later response can leave important parts of an earlier explanation unseen. In a study included in the scoping review, Luis’s shorter English translation made an ecological distinction less explicit than his bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20’s reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships they had previously explained. The records arose under different task and language conditions, so the cases cannot be treated as equivalent. Each nonetheless complicates an interpretation based on the most recent answer alone.

Reading these records together raised a question my projects had not yet investigated. I could identify ways to assess students' reasoning while having little evidence about whether students could influence the judgments or classroom activities that followed.

Research interests

I read studies of teacher decision-making alongside research on language, cultural knowledge, and scientific modelling. They offer different ways to examine how students' contributions are recognized during particular tasks and interactions, and whether students can influence the criteria by which scientific explanations are judged.

Classroom authority and epistemic agency

How instructional decisions shape students' opportunities to pursue scientific questions.

During a lesson on cell signalling, a student might ask what happens when a receptor remains active after the signal has disappeared. If the classroom diagram shows activation without explaining how signalling ends, the teacher could invite students to examine the processes it leaves out. They might then investigate receptor inactivation or changes further along the signalling pathway. Whether the class pursues that question depends partly on how the teacher interprets the student's observation.

Curricular planning can make room for students to influence the methods of investigation and the models through which a class develops an explanation (Ko & Krist, 2019). Once the lesson begins, those opportunities depend on what teachers notice in students' contributions and how they respond. Studies of teachers' noticing and classroom discourse show how a student's contribution can become a question for collective inquiry or be treated primarily as an answer to evaluate (Krist et al., 2023; Stroupe, 2014). The teacher can acknowledge a contribution while retaining control over which questions the class pursues.

As students pursue questions beyond the planned lesson, teachers also need to consider what disciplinary guidance the investigation requires. Planning inquiry and project-based STEM activities includes considering what makes a problem worth investigating and the support students will need (DeCoito, 2024). In a Grade 6 classroom investigating flight, students' work with physical materials challenged a knowledge-building design centred on writing and discussion (Reeve, 2023). Web-based science inquiry environments raise a related question about how the resources prepared for a lesson shape the explanations students develop (Slotta & Linn, 2009). The planning challenge is to prepare disciplinary resources while leaving room for students' questions to change the course of an investigation.

Relationships and learning settings shape the authority students can exercise. A longitudinal study followed an early-career teacher negotiating an activist commitment alongside the demands of school practice (Barrett, 2013). In a different setting, students and mentors participating in a robotics competition negotiated authority as they developed scientific and technical knowledge together (Puvirajah et al., 2012). Productive disciplinary engagement provides a further account of how student authority can develop alongside responsibility to peers and access to disciplinary resources (Engle & Conant, 2002).

Questions about authority also concern whose contributions are recognized as legitimate. Epistemic agency involves more than performing prescribed scientific practices (Miller et al., 2018). Rightful presence draws attention to how minoritized youth can make classroom inequities visible and contest the norms that determine whose knowledge and participation are recognized (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). A teacher may have strong scientific grounds for correcting an explanation. I want to examine whether students can question the grounds for a correction and how the teacher responds when they do.

A community survey designed during a seventh-grade biology unit on stress illustrates how scientific and ethical judgments can develop together. Students questioned the effects their questions might have on respondents, and adults and youth negotiated care for those respondents as part of deciding what would count as a responsible inquiry (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Recognizing scientific reasoning

How classroom expectations shape which contributions become visible and consequential.

Cultural knowledge and participation

How the form of a task influences the knowledge people make visible.

People can know similar things while drawing on different relationships to organize an explanation. In research with Menominee and European American fish experts, Menominee participants more often mentioned habitat when explaining how they grouped local fish. When the researchers explicitly asked both groups to sort by habitat, strong agreement emerged without a reliable difference between them (Bang et al., 2007). The earlier difference therefore concerned which ecological relationships participants brought forward on their own. It cautions me against interpreting an unmentioned relationship as necessarily unknown. Later scholarship places such differences within cultural practices and examines the assumptions about nature that science education brings into classrooms (Bang & Medin, 2010; Bang et al., 2012).

The fish studies concern particular communities with their own histories. In other settings, researchers have examined how participants' commitments and feelings enter into their accounts of science. Interviews with Muslim secondary science teachers in Canada and Pakistan examined how religious commitments informed their views of evolution and its teaching, while also identifying gaps in some teachers' understanding of evolutionary mechanisms (Asghar, 2013). In a separate study, two groups of physics learners approached radioactivity in ways that the researchers examined in relation to their feelings about the subject (Alsop & Watts, 2000). In my teaching, I would need to consider those experiences while examining the scientific adequacy of students' explanations.

Children attending community science clubs in Canada valued hands-on activities and the relationships they formed. Their accounts also showed how club science and school science could be treated as separate, making it difficult to connect the enjoyment of club activities with learning at school (Burke & Navas Iannini, 2021). I wonder how this distinction affects the scientific work students feel able to pursue when they move between settings.

Recognition also concerns who is considered capable of scientific work. Doctoral physics students described how expectations about gender and competence affected the ways they could position themselves as physicists (Gonsalves, 2014). A longitudinal study followed fifteen women of colour from undergraduate science studies into later careers, finding that recognition by others mattered differently across their science-identity trajectories (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). These accounts make me attend to the judgments through which someone becomes recognized as a participant in science, within a broader understanding of learning as culturally situated (Nasir et al., 2014).

The researcher's interpretation brings its own assumptions into view. Labelling one contribution as everyday knowledge and another as scientific reasoning may overlook how a student connected experience with an emerging explanation. What can be known also depends on which interactions are recorded, how episodes are selected, and whose account informs the interpretation. Guidance for video research examines these practical and ethical decisions (Derry et al., 2010), while the concept of situated knowledge makes the partial perspective of any account an explicit concern (Haraway, 1988). Those decisions affect how confidently I can move from a recorded contribution to a claim about a student's reasoning.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

What becomes visible as ideas move through talk, writing, and shared representations.

In an English-medium science classroom, a student's explanation may develop through discussion before it appears in writing. A student might explain in Korean why water moves across a cell membrane while working with a classmate, then submit a shorter account in English. Without a record of their discussion, I could examine what the student wrote, but I could not know which scientific relationships they had already expressed or how their ideas developed as they worked together. The language and evidence permitted by the assessment would shape what I could conclude about the student's reasoning.

What researchers recognize as evidence depends partly on how they understand language and scientific sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). Access to established school science practices can be pursued alongside efforts to transform which linguistic resources and scientific practices schools value (Grapin et al., 2023). Classmates may question an explanation's claims or ask for further evidence. Analyses of critique during discussions in three middle-school classrooms describe how students could exercise epistemic agency by responding to peers' reasoning (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020).

Language policies can shape which parts of a learner's repertoire become available during a task. In a Malaysian Grade 5 English-language classroom with an English-only policy, students used translanguaging during peer collaboration while encountering constraints from classroom expectations and the attitudes of others (Rajendram, 2023). A Canadian university study compared plurilingual and English-only instruction in an academic English programme and found greater development of plurilingual and pluricultural competence in the plurilingual group (Galante, 2022). Neither study measured secondary science understanding. Their relevance to my work lies in the language opportunities and constraints they document, rather than in evidence of science learning.

Gesture and shared modelling can make scientific relationships visible during an investigation, including relationships that a final written answer may not fully express. In a Swedish elementary science classroom, the language resources bilingual students used differed between teacher-led exchanges and small-group activities (Ünsal et al., 2018). An analytic framework distinguishes changes across representations from the ways learners combine words, gestures, and visual elements within a representation (Tang et al., 2014). In an out-of-school inquiry into electric flow and resistance, emergent bilingual students drew on linguistic and nonlinguistic resources as they developed models with peers and their instructor (Suárez, 2020). The study also describes how the instructor's pedagogical moves signalled when and how students could draw on those resources.

Interpreting students' ideas across talk, writing, and images raises a methodological question about which records to preserve. Research on plurilingual identity texts has examined conversations alongside students' written and visual work (Prasad, 2015). A relational account of multiliteracies considers how meaning develops within social and cultural relationships (Simon, 2011). The literacy settings differ from those of secondary science. The same attention to conversations and classroom relationships may help me interpret written and visual work in science.

Language proficiency scores offer one account of university students' experiences. A longitudinal study examined academic acculturation alongside English proficiency and achievement, attending to factors a language test score might leave unexplained (Neumann et al., 2023). Classroom exchanges present a different interpretive challenge. During spoken-word-poetry activities in an academic English programme, multilingual students discussed racism amid emotional tensions that shaped their interactions (Burton, 2024). Both studies concern university language learning. In secondary science, they lead me to consider how the setting and relationships surrounding a contribution affect what an observer can reasonably conclude from it.

Scientific representations and their limits

The explanatory purposes of models, and the questions that may lead students to reconsider them.

Evaluating and revising scientific models

When the assumptions of a model become part of the investigation.

A simple exponential population-growth model assumes that the per-capita growth rate remains constant. If observations showed a population growing more slowly than the model predicts, students could question the conditions it leaves out, including changes in resources or other influences on birth and death rates. They might then revise the model or specify when its assumptions remain useful. Deciding what would count as an adequate explanation could become part of their investigation.

Students may be able to critique scientific models without having the same influence over the criteria used to judge them. In a fifth-grade inquiry, written reflection on peer critiques and class discussion supported students in revising their class's criteria (Murphy et al., 2023). In a seventh-grade study, students used visual, verbal, and written resources to evaluate and revise models against criteria selected in advance by the researchers and teachers (Cheung & Yang, 2026).

A learning progression for scientific modelling describes how students may come to treat models as tools for explaining and predicting, as well as representations that can change with new understanding (Schwarz et al., 2009). The questions students are trying to resolve also shape how scientific practices and argumentation become meaningful (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015).

Sex determination and biological variation

How scientific categories acquire explanatory and educational authority.

Human sex development offers a more specific context for examining what a biological representation includes. A simplified classroom pathway may show how chromosome-associated gene expression contributes to gonadal determination. Subsequent hormone production, receptor activity, and tissue responses shape aspects of sexual differentiation that the same pathway may leave unexplained. Clinical reviews document variation in these processes, including genetic and endocrine differences that cannot be inferred from chromosomes alone (Arboleda et al., 2014; Witchel, 2018). I draw on these sources for their descriptions of developmental mechanisms, while recognizing that clinical classifications and classroom models serve different purposes.

The choice of what a model includes also affects how students encounter biological variation. Guidance for inclusive postsecondary biology teaching recommends introducing diversity early, examining the historical context of scientific knowledge, and creating a respectful classroom culture (Zemenick et al., 2022). Although these principles were developed for postsecondary education and do not establish the effects of a particular secondary lesson, they help me consider how variation can be taught as an ordinary subject of biological inquiry. Students should be able to examine the scientific claims without being asked to disclose anything about their own bodies or identities.

Historical research demonstrates why the uses of a category deserve scrutiny alongside its biological basis. Earlier attempts to link an XYY chromosome pattern with aggression drew on cultural assumptions about masculinity, an episode examined in the history of sex-chromosome research (Richardson, 2013). A later systematic review found no statistical evidence that an extra Y chromosome, by itself, establishes a predisposition to aggression or criminal behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). One source traces the history of the inference, while the other reviews the studies used to support it.

The history of medical classifications of intersex variation raises questions about who defines a condition and how diagnostic categories influence decisions about care (Fausto-Sterling, 2000; Davis, 2015). Related questions arise when a measured testosterone concentration is used to make claims about a person's capacities or behaviour without accounting for the processes that mediate its effects (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). In a critique of 2011 eligibility policies for elite women athletes, Karkazis and colleagues examined how assumptions about testosterone and athletic advantage entered institutional rules (Karkazis et al., 2012). These settings differ, yet each makes it necessary to ask which conclusion a measurement supports and who bears the consequences when that conclusion is extended.

The conditions under which these questions can be raised also deserve attention. Airton's ethic of hospitality asks educators to prepare to welcome students whose gender identities they cannot know in advance, rather than directing inclusive efforts only toward students already identified as transgender (Airton, 2023). Scholarship on the politics of sex education examines how adult claims to expertise can obscure students' own engagement with a curriculum (Gilbert, 2021). Both prompt me to consider how students can question an explanation without being asked to account for their personal circumstances.

Beyond biology education, scholarship on gender and participation examines how institutional norms can be challenged. Alexander considers trans-exclusionary politics in relation to carceral power and Black trans life (Alexander, 2023). Burkholder and colleagues draw on participatory arts-based projects to explore queer joy as a collective educational practice, often developed beyond formal classrooms (Burkholder et al., 2025). These accounts address different forms of power and participation. Neither evaluates a particular secondary biology lesson, although both encourage me to consider whose terms of participation are taken for granted and which possibilities students can help create.

Across these examples, evaluating a scientific explanation and deciding how a classroom responds to a challenge require different kinds of judgment. A developmental model can be examined for the processes it accurately represents. The classroom exchange also involves deciding who can ask for further reasons, offer evidence, and influence what the group investigates. I would like to study those exchanges as they unfold, particularly when students raise questions that the planned curriculum did not anticipate.

Related writing

These questions also inform my independent essay, What can a biological classification explain? I consider how conclusions drawn from haemoglobin S and malaria studies depend on the outcomes measured, and how historical claims about XYY chromosomes extended beyond the evidence for behaviour. Both cases raise questions about which scientific categories enter biology teaching and whether students can challenge the claims made through them.
