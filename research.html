Research

In a biology lesson, students may examine a simplified diagram tracing human sex development from chromosomes to reproductive anatomy. A student might ask how differences in hormone responses fit into that pathway. The teacher could explain the missing processes and continue with the lesson, or invite students to examine what the diagram leaves out and consider which relationships a more adequate model should represent.

Teachers make these decisions within curricular and disciplinary expectations, choosing which questions to pursue and whether students' explanations can shape the work that follows. These judgments take place within institutions and histories marked by racism, colonialism, and inequalities connected to gender, sexuality, social class, and language. My interest in justice-oriented science education includes how students can participate in evaluating scientific representations and the grounds on which their contributions are recognized. Scholarship on epistemic agency and rightful presence helps me examine these relationships alongside the scientific reasoning involved.

Research projects

Multilingual science education scoping review

I examined 20 empirical studies retrieved through ERIC to consider how researchers inferred multilingual students’ science understanding from written responses, classroom interaction, and visual work. I also examined the reporting of language options and participation conditions, since these can affect what a student’s contribution allows a researcher to conclude.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I studied students’ explanations across assessments and two adjustments to the feedback routine. For ten students whose Unit 3 explanations did not meet the study's completeness criteria, I compared the original responses with later closed-resource reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions developed fuller causal pathways, while others omitted relationships previously expressed. Comparing these records changed the questions I would ask in subsequent assessment, although those follow-up questions were not administered in the reported inquiry.

The record used to assess an explanation can change which scientific relationships are visible. In a study included in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation made an ecological distinction less explicit than his bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's closed-resource reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships expressed in an earlier response. The cases arose under different task and language conditions. Read together, they caution against treating an absent relationship in one response as a complete account of a student's understanding.

My projects examined the grounds for inferring scientific understanding from students' work. They did not establish how students could influence the judgments teachers made from that work. A teacher might identify an incomplete causal pathway and choose what students should revisit, yet the student could have reasons for questioning that interpretation. Studying the interaction would require attention to what each participant considered adequate evidence and to how their judgments shaped the next classroom activity.

Research interests

The questions emerging from my projects extend from the interpretation of a response to the decisions made after it. Research on teacher noticing examines how student reasoning becomes available for consideration. Work on epistemic agency and rightful presence asks who can influence the questions and standards guiding a classroom inquiry. I read these perspectives alongside scholarship on language and scientific modelling, where the evidence students can offer is shaped by the opportunities they have to participate.

Classroom authority and epistemic agency

How instructional decisions shape students' opportunities to pursue scientific questions.

A student's contribution becomes evidence of scientific reasoning through the questions a teacher asks and the interpretation that follows. In an example discussed by Cowie and colleagues, a teacher dismissed an unexpected answer before hearing the students' reasoning, which drew on a different way of comparing the animals in the task (Cowie et al., 2018). Research on teacher noticing examines how teachers attend to students' ideas and respond during science discussions (Krist et al., 2023). These accounts help explain how a contribution may enter or disappear from collective consideration. Whether students can subsequently influence the questions being investigated requires a further examination of epistemic agency (Miller et al., 2018; Stroupe, 2014).

Teachers also establish some of these opportunities before a lesson begins. A curriculum can invite students to shape investigations and the representations used to explain phenomena (Ko & Krist, 2019). Such invitations still depend on the disciplinary resources available when students pursue an unexpected question. Planning inquiry-oriented STEM teaching requires attention to the disciplinary support students may need (DeCoito, 2024). In a Grade 6 flight investigation, students' work with physical materials challenged the activities initially prepared for knowledge building (Reeve, 2023). Planning also involves deciding how students can pursue an unexpected direction with the time and resources available. That decision affects which questions can become part of the class's scientific work.

Students' influence may also differ across the decisions within an inquiry. In a Grade 7 Science and Technology project, students chose to investigate Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls and developed a public Red Dress exhibition. Their sense of ownership coexisted with accounts of peers overlooking some ideas during collaboration (Jao et al., 2026). In another Grade 7 classroom, concerns about how a community survey might affect respondents became part of the criteria students and adults used to judge a responsible investigation (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026). Having a say in the topic of an investigation does not ensure that a student is heard as the work unfolds. The survey case raises another issue about whose concerns influence the criteria used to judge the inquiry. I would examine the moments when those judgments change what the group does next.

Authority is also shaped by what the classroom recognizes as legitimate knowledge and action. Productive disciplinary engagement connects student authority with responsibility to peers and access to resources for investigating a problem (Engle & Conant, 2002). Rightful presence brings a further concern into view, asking how historically unequal relations shape whose ways of knowing are recognized and whose challenges to classroom norms can have consequences (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). Providing students with a choice of activities would not, on its own, show whether they could question the standards by which their explanations were assessed. An analysis of classroom interactions would need to attend to the authority exercised through those standards.

Teachers' interpretations may themselves become objects of inquiry when colleagues work together. In a four-year professional learning community, high school science teachers developed their attention to students' epistemic practices and evidence-based explanations (Shim & Thompson, 2022). A Grade 9 Applied Mathematics initiative offers a different view of collaboration, with teachers reporting learning from the joint development of classroom materials (Jao & McDougall, 2015). One study traces shifts in teachers' attention to students' epistemic practices, while the other draws on teachers' accounts of joint work. To examine how those changes reach students, I would follow discussions of student work into subsequent lessons.

I am interested in moments when students' explanations or questions challenge an anticipated lesson. Scientific guidance remains necessary as teachers assess the mechanisms students propose. I would examine how the grounds for a teacher's judgment become available for discussion and whether students can influence what happens after that judgment. The relationship between disciplinary evaluation and classroom authority is where I hope to develop the next phase of my research.

During a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned whether a proposed community survey might upset respondents. As students and adults considered how to proceed, care for respondents became part of what counted as a responsible way to gather information. Their discussions show how students could help establish criteria for the investigation through negotiations of classroom authority (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Recognizing scientific reasoning

How classroom expectations shape which contributions become visible and consequential.

Cultural knowledge and participation

How the form of a task influences the knowledge people make visible.

What someone brings forward in an explanation depends partly on what the task invites them to consider. When Menominee and European American fish experts grouped familiar fish, Menominee participants more often referred to habitat. Asked explicitly to sort by habitat, the groups showed substantial agreement (Bang et al., 2007). The distinction between a relationship offered spontaneously and one expressed under a particular prompt is important for interpreting classroom work. An unmentioned relationship may still be available to a learner.

A student's experience can inform a scientific explanation in ways a classroom task has not anticipated. Scholarship on cultural practices of ecological reasoning examines how knowledge develops through relationships with the natural world and how school science may privilege particular ways of describing those relationships (Bang & Medin, 2010; Bang et al., 2012). A teacher still has to consider whether the student's explanation accounts adequately for the phenomenon at hand. The difficulty lies in evaluating the scientific relationships expressed without assuming that the language or organization of a familiar school answer exhausts what the student can explain.

Recognition gained through scientific activity may not carry the same meaning across settings. Children attending community science clubs valued practical activities and relationships with others, yet some treated club science and school science as separate experiences (Burke & Navas Iannini, 2021). In an after-school conversation club, girls used relationships and shared interests to position themselves as knowledgeable about science. The recognition they gained held value within the group, while their accounts of school science remained more distant (Gonsalves & Rahm, 2023). I would examine how students' questions and practices from outside school are received when they enter a classroom investigation.

Expectations of competence introduce another dimension of recognition. Doctoral physics students described how gendered expectations shaped their efforts to become recognized as physicists (Gonsalves, 2014). Across longer science-identity trajectories, recognition by others took different forms for women of colour pursuing scientific work (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Recognition emerges through relationships and judgments about competence, a concern also developed in accounts of learning as a cultural process (Nasir et al., 2014). In a classroom study, I would need to examine how that judgment appears in responses to students' questions.

The researcher's own decisions also shape what is recognized in a record. Calling a contribution everyday knowledge or scientific reasoning may obscure how the student connected experience with an emerging explanation. Video research requires choices about which interactions to preserve and how to interpret them (Derry et al., 2010). Situated knowledge further reminds me that an analysis is developed from a particular, incomplete perspective (Haraway, 1988). I would read each recorded contribution alongside the task and surrounding interaction, examining which claims about the student's reasoning the evidence can support.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

What becomes visible as ideas move through talk, writing, and shared representations.

A written explanation records what a student expressed under the conditions of a particular task. It does not show every relationship the student may have considered while speaking or working with others. In my scoping review, researchers inferred science understanding from task responses and from the ways ideas developed in interaction or across representations. These routes support different kinds of claims. When the language available for discussion differs from that required in a final response, the records need to be examined together before an omission can be interpreted as a gap in scientific understanding.

The way researchers conceptualize language also affects what they study. A synthesis of science education research distinguished linguistic and more expansive views of language and documented differences in the scientific practices examined under those views (González-Howard et al., 2023). The figure below shows the distribution of research attention, not differences in students' understanding. Related scholarship considers how access to established school science practices can be pursued while questioning which linguistic resources schools recognize (Grapin et al., 2023). For my research, this distinction raises a practical issue about whether an assessment examines the scientific relationships a student can explain or primarily the forms of expression the task permits.

Classroom language expectations can affect which resources become available during an interaction. In a Malaysian Grade 5 English-language classroom with an English-only policy, students nevertheless used translanguaging in peer work while encountering constraints from classroom norms and others' responses (Rajendram, 2023). The difference between an official language policy and the resources students use in collaboration offers a useful distinction for my classroom research. I would examine when students draw on their linguistic resources and how others respond to their contributions.

Participation in discussion introduces another kind of evidence. Students may challenge an explanation or ask a peer to justify a claim, with the exchange making the grounds for an idea available to others. An analysis of critique in middle-school science discussions examines how such responses can support epistemic agency (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020). To assess the scientific reasoning involved, I would examine the claims students made and how peers' responses shaped what the group pursued next.

Shared models and gestures may make further relationships available for examination. In elementary science, bilingual students' use of language resources differed between teacher-led exchanges and small-group work (Ünsal et al., 2018). In an out-of-school inquiry into electrical phenomena, emergent bilingual students used linguistic and nonlinguistic resources while constructing models with peers and an instructor (Suárez, 2020). An analytic framework for multimodal representations helps distinguish how resources work together within a representation from changes between representations (Tang et al., 2014). These distinctions would help me trace whether a gesture clarifies a scientific relationship, or whether a revised diagram expresses a relationship absent from an earlier version.

The teacher's interpretation remains part of the evidence problem. During high school science assessment co-design, teachers' discussions included both asset- and deficit-oriented descriptions of students' ideas and linguistic resources. Those descriptions shifted towards more asset-oriented framings during particular phases of practice and reflection (Fine & Furtak, 2024). To examine when those interpretations become consequential in my classroom, I would link students' contributions with teachers' responses and subsequent instructional decisions.

Scientific representations and their limits

The explanatory purposes of models, and the questions that may lead students to reconsider them.

Evaluating and revising scientific models

When the assumptions of a model become part of the investigation.

A model's adequacy depends on what it is intended to explain. An omitted relationship may be appropriate for one purpose and prevent a model from addressing another. A learning progression for scientific modelling examines how students come to use models for explanation and prediction, including their capacity for revision (Schwarz et al., 2009). Accounts of argumentation emerging through scientific activity add attention to the questions that give a model or claim its purpose (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015). In classroom assessment, the adequacy of a representation therefore needs to be considered in relation to the phenomenon students were investigating and the explanatory work the model was expected to do.

Who determines those expectations is a related question. In a fifth-grade inquiry, students contributed to revising their class's criteria for evaluating models through reflection and discussion (Murphy et al., 2023). In a seventh-grade study, students evaluated and revised models using criteria established by researchers and teachers (Cheung & Yang, 2026). Both studies examine students' evaluative work, yet their accounts place the authority to determine criteria differently. A student may have room to improve a model within given standards while having little opportunity to examine those standards themselves. That distinction would be important when deciding whether classroom modelling supports epistemic agency.

The development of a model also involves relationships that its final form may not make visible. In a seventh-grade investigation of stress, four students gave their models names and identities that developed through their interactions with one another and with adults. Their work with physical and digital materials formed part of that process (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2023). This relational analysis raises questions about what is lost when a finished model is treated as a complete record of the activity. The scientific relationships represented in the model still require evaluation, while the interaction through which the model developed may illuminate who could influence its direction.

My classroom action research compared students' written explanations and later reconstructions of a photosynthesis mechanism. It showed how relationships could be expressed in one record and absent from another, but those records did not establish how students negotiated the criteria for an adequate explanation. Studying that process would require recording how teachers and students discuss proposed revisions. I would then be able to examine the disciplinary grounds for a judgment alongside the interactions that make particular judgments consequential.

Sex determination and biological variation

How scientific categories acquire explanatory and educational authority.

A classroom diagram tracing sex development from chromosomes to reproductive anatomy can be useful within a stated explanatory scope. It may leave the processes connecting those features largely invisible. Gene-regulatory pathways contribute to gonadal determination, while hormone production and tissue responses shape subsequent development. Clinical reviews describe variation in these processes that cannot be inferred from chromosomes alone (Arboleda et al., 2014; Witchel, 2018). When a student asks about a pathway the diagram leaves out, the scientific task is to examine whether that omission affects what the diagram claims to explain. The question can lead to a more adequate account of the mechanism while retaining the value of a simplified representation for other purposes.

A biological finding can acquire claims beyond the evidence on which it rests. Historical attempts to associate an XYY chromosome pattern with aggression drew on assumptions about masculinity (Richardson, 2013). A subsequent systematic review found no statistical evidence that an additional Y chromosome, on its own, established a predisposition to aggression or criminal behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). The historical inference crossed from a chromosomal observation to a claim about behaviour without establishing the proposed connection. In teaching, that distinction offers a way to ask students which causal claims a classification supports and what further evidence would be required.

Scientific categories also acquire consequences through the institutions that use them. Histories and critiques of intersex classification examine how diagnostic categories are defined and how they can influence decisions about care (Fausto-Sterling, 2000; Davis, 2015). These questions differ from those surrounding the use of testosterone measurements in sport. Research questioning straightforward inferences from testosterone to individual capacities offers one way to examine the biological assumptions involved (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). A critique of women's athletic eligibility policies in 2011 also shows how such assumptions entered regulatory decisions (Karkazis et al., 2012). Clinical decisions and athletic eligibility rules serve different purposes and require scrutiny of the inferences drawn from biological measurements.

These distinctions place demands on how biology is taught. Guidance developed for postsecondary biology recommends presenting human variation within the ordinary study of biological mechanisms (Zemenick et al., 2022). Other educational scholarship asks teachers to prepare for students whose gender identities cannot be known in advance (Airton, 2023) and examines how youth may question adults' claims to curricular expertise (Gilbert, 2021). For my teaching, they raise a responsibility to examine scientific claims carefully without asking students to explain or disclose their own bodies and identities.

Evaluating a model and deciding what to do when a student challenges it remain distinct judgments. The biological question concerns the mechanisms the representation can support. The classroom question concerns whether a student's observation can influence which explanatory relationships the class investigates next. I would like to examine how those decisions are made during discussion, attending to the disciplinary reasons teachers offer and to the ways students' questions are taken up.

Related writing

In What can a biological classification explain?, I examine two cases in which a scientific claim depends on what the available evidence can establish. A study of haemoglobin S and malaria reports different estimates of protection across clinical outcomes. The history of XYY chromosome research traces a different inference, from identifying a chromosomal pattern to treating it as evidence of behaviour. The essay considers what students might question when such interpretations become part of a biology lesson.
