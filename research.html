Research

In a biology lesson, students may examine a simplified diagram tracing human sex development from chromosomes to reproductive anatomy. A student might ask how differences in hormone responses fit into that pathway. The teacher could explain the missing processes and continue with the lesson, or invite students to examine what the diagram leaves out and consider which relationships a more adequate model should represent.

Teachers make these decisions within curricular and disciplinary expectations, choosing which questions to pursue and whether students' explanations can shape the work that follows. These judgments take place within institutions and histories marked by racism, colonialism, and inequalities connected to gender, sexuality, social class, and language. My interest in justice-oriented science education includes how students can participate in evaluating scientific representations and the grounds on which their contributions are recognized. Scholarship on epistemic agency and rightful presence helps me examine these relationships alongside the scientific reasoning involved.

Research projects

Multilingual science education scoping review

I examined 20 empirical studies retrieved through ERIC to consider how researchers inferred multilingual students’ science understanding from written responses, classroom interaction, and visual work. I also examined the reporting of language options and participation conditions, since these can affect what a student’s contribution allows a researcher to conclude.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I studied students’ explanations across assessments and two adjustments to the feedback routine. For ten students whose Unit 3 explanations did not meet the study's completeness criteria, I compared the original responses with later closed-resource reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions developed fuller causal pathways, while others omitted relationships previously expressed. The comparisons suggested different questions for subsequent assessment, which were not administered during the inquiry.

The record used to assess an explanation can change which scientific relationships are visible. In a study included in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation made an ecological distinction less explicit than his bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's closed-resource reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships expressed in an earlier response. The cases arose under different task and language conditions. Read together, they caution against treating an absent relationship in one response as a complete account of a student's understanding.

My projects examined the grounds for inferring scientific understanding from students' work. A teacher may judge an explanation incomplete on scientific grounds and still encounter a student who questions that interpretation. What remains less visible in an assessment record is how such a judgment is explained and whether the student can influence what happens next.

Research interests

The judgment made about a student's response can shape what the class investigates next. Teacher noticing offers a way to examine how reasoning becomes available for consideration; epistemic agency and rightful presence raise further questions about who can influence the direction and standards of inquiry. The linguistic and representational forms of students' contributions shape both the evidence available and the judgments made from it.

Classroom authority and epistemic agency

How instructional decisions shape students' opportunities to pursue scientific questions.

A student's contribution enters classroom inquiry through judgments about what the response means and whether it deserves further attention. In an example discussed by Cowie and colleagues, a teacher dismissed an unexpected answer before hearing the students' reasoning about how animals could be compared (Cowie et al., 2018). Such moments bring into focus what teachers notice and how they respond as students develop scientific ideas (Krist et al., 2023). The question of students' epistemic agency extends beyond recognition of an idea to their opportunities to influence the problem being investigated and the work undertaken in response (Miller et al., 2018; Stroupe, 2014).

Those opportunities are shaped by planning as well as by decisions made during inquiry. Opening curricula to students' contributions creates possibilities for epistemic agency, while sustaining an investigation requires disciplinary support and resources (Ko & Krist, 2019; DeCoito, 2024). The tension becomes apparent when students pursue a phenomenon in ways that unsettle an anticipated sequence. In a Grade 6 flight investigation, their work with materials challenged activities the teacher had prepared for knowledge building (Reeve, 2023). A responsive curriculum therefore depends on how teachers make room for emerging questions while maintaining opportunities to develop scientifically adequate explanations.

Influence over a project's topic can coexist with unequal influence over what happens within it. In a Grade 7 Science and Technology project, students chose to investigate Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls and prepared a public Red Dress exhibition, while some reported that peers overlooked their ideas during collaboration (Jao et al., 2026). Questions about participation emerge at another point in an inquiry when students and adults negotiate the standards guiding a decision. During a seventh-grade biology investigation, students' concerns about upsetting community survey respondents contributed to deliberations about responsible evidence gathering (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026). Taken together, the cases distinguish opportunities to choose an inquiry direction from opportunities to influence its conduct and criteria.

The criteria themselves are a form of classroom authority. Productive disciplinary engagement links students' authority to their responsibilities within a shared problem and the resources available to investigate it (Engle & Conant, 2002). Rightful presence raises questions about the historically unequal conditions under which particular contributions, relationships, and challenges to established practices can be recognized (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). These perspectives extend the distinction emerging from the classroom cases. Participation concerns the intellectual work students can undertake, including whether the grounds for judging that work are themselves open to discussion.

What teachers attend to together also shapes the judgments they bring back to classrooms. Sustained professional learning has supported science teachers' attention to students' epistemic practices, while collaborative development of instructional materials offers another setting for examining practice (Shim & Thompson, 2022; Jao & McDougall, 2015). Yet collaboration does not make every aspect of teaching equally available for scrutiny. In rehearsals of mathematical defining, discussion concentrated on pedagogical approaches, with relatively few opportunities to consider students' reasoning about definitions (Kobiela et al., 2023). The focus of professional dialogue matters when considering how teachers learn to interpret students' ideas and how those interpretations enter subsequent decisions.

An unexpected student question brings these concerns into a single classroom moment. The teacher must consider the explanation's disciplinary adequacy and decide what the class will investigate next. How that judgment is articulated, and whether the student can question its grounds, reveals the authority exercised through ordinary decisions about inquiry.

During a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned whether a proposed community survey might upset respondents. As students and adults considered how to proceed, care for respondents became part of what counted as a responsible way to gather information. Their discussions show how students could help establish criteria for the investigation through negotiations of classroom authority (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Recognizing scientific reasoning

How classroom expectations shape which contributions become visible and consequential.

Cultural knowledge and participation

How the form of a task influences the knowledge people make visible.

The scientific relationships made visible in an explanation depend partly on the task that elicits them. Research on cultural practices of ecological reasoning shows how understandings of the natural world develop through particular relationships and activities, some of which are less readily recognized in school science (Bang & Medin, 2010; Bang et al., 2012). The distinction becomes concrete in a comparison of Menominee and European American fish experts. Menominee participants more often invoked habitat when grouping fish spontaneously, whereas an explicit request to sort by habitat produced substantial agreement across groups (Bang et al., 2007). A relationship absent from an initial answer may thus reflect what the question invited participants to express. Evaluating that answer still requires attention to the scientific account it offers.

Whether a contribution becomes valued also depends on the setting in which it is offered. Studies of community science activities describe relationships and practical engagement that participants found meaningful, alongside persistent distinctions between experiences of science outside school and school science (Burke & Navas Iannini, 2021; Gonsalves & Rahm, 2023). In the conversation club studied by Gonsalves and Rahm, girls drew on shared interests to position themselves as knowledgeable about science, yet that recognition remained closely tied to the club. Participation may therefore carry different possibilities for being seen as knowledgeable across settings, even when the activities involve science.

Recognition also depends on the expectations attached to the person offering an idea. Gendered judgments about scientific competence shaped doctoral physics students' efforts to be recognized (Gonsalves, 2014), while accounts of women of colour pursuing science traced varied forms of recognition across their trajectories (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Work on learning as a cultural process likewise locates participation within relationships and practices that make particular forms of knowing recognizable (Nasir et al., 2014). These perspectives bring a further question to the interpretation of classroom contributions. The judgment that an explanation is credible can also involve judgments about who is understood to be capable of offering it.

Researchers participate in these processes of recognition when they select and interpret evidence. Decisions about which classroom interactions are captured, retained, and analysed shape the record from which claims about learning are made (Derry et al., 2010). Accounts of situated knowledge make the researcher's position within that interpretive work consequential (Haraway, 1988). Labelling a contribution as everyday knowledge or scientific reasoning can obscure the connections a student made between experience and explanation. Interpreting such contributions requires attention to the task, the surrounding interaction, and the grounds on which particular meanings were assigned.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

What becomes visible as ideas move through talk, writing, and shared representations.

A student's written explanation preserves relationships expressed under the conditions of a particular task. Its interpretation becomes more complex when other records make additional reasoning visible. In my scoping review, researchers inferred science understanding from written responses, classroom interaction, and students' work with representations. Each route made different aspects of reasoning available, with different grounds for the claims researchers could draw. A relationship omitted from a final response may have appeared in an earlier conversation or representation, especially when the language permitted during discussion differs from that required in the submitted work.

What researchers recognize as evidence also depends on how language is conceptualized. A synthesis of science education studies found that linguistic and more expansive views of language were associated with attention to different scientific practices (González-Howard et al., 2023). The figure below records that distribution of research attention rather than differences in students' understanding. Scholarship on language and disciplinary practice raises a connected challenge, considering students' access to established ways of participating in school science alongside the linguistic resources schools recognize (Grapin et al., 2023). The conceptual framing of an assessment therefore affects both the contributions it invites and the grounds on which scientific understanding is inferred.

Classroom interactions show how those opportunities are negotiated in practice. Students working under an English-only policy in a Malaysian Grade 5 English-language classroom used translanguaging with peers, while classroom expectations and responses also constrained its use (Rajendram, 2023). Access to linguistic resources is one condition of participation; another concerns what happens when ideas are questioned. Research on critique in middle-school science discussions examines how students' challenges and requests for justification can support epistemic agency (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020). Taken together, these concerns direct attention to the interaction itself, where the resources available for expression and the responses to a contribution shape what becomes possible to explain.

Scientific relationships can also take shape through gestures and shared representations. Bilingual students' linguistic resources have been observed to vary across teacher-led and small-group elementary science activities (Ünsal et al., 2018), while emergent bilingual learners investigating electricity outside school combined linguistic and nonlinguistic resources as they developed models with others (Suárez, 2020). A multimodal framework helps distinguish the resources working together within a representation from relationships that become visible as students move between representations (Tang et al., 2014). A finished diagram offers evidence of an expressed explanation; the interaction surrounding its development can reveal how that explanation emerged.

The teacher's interpretation remains consequential across these forms of evidence. During high school science assessment co-design, teachers' descriptions of students' ideas and linguistic resources included asset- and deficit-oriented framings, with more asset-oriented descriptions emerging during particular phases of practice and reflection (Fine & Furtak, 2024). Such changes show how teachers' interpretations can develop through professional activity. Understanding their implications for students also requires attention to the explanations students offer and the responses those explanations receive in subsequent classroom work.

Scientific representations and their limits

The explanatory purposes of models, and the questions that may lead students to reconsider them.

Evaluating and revising scientific models

When the assumptions of a model become part of the investigation.

Assessing a scientific model begins with the explanatory work it is expected to perform. An omitted relationship may be consistent with one purpose yet prevent the same representation from addressing another. A learning progression for modelling describes students' growing capacity to use and revise representations for explanation and prediction (Schwarz et al., 2009). Research on argumentation further locates the grounds for a model or claim within the activity and questions that give it a purpose (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015). The adequacy of a representation therefore has to be considered alongside the phenomenon under investigation and the claims students are trying to explain.

The authority to determine those purposes and standards can be distributed differently even when students actively evaluate models. In a fifth-grade inquiry, students helped revise the criteria by which their class assessed models (Murphy et al., 2023). In a seventh-grade study, students evaluated and improved models using criteria set by researchers and teachers (Cheung & Yang, 2026). Both accounts involve evaluative work, but they describe different opportunities to influence the basis of evaluation. Students may contribute sophisticated revisions while the standards governing those revisions remain established elsewhere. That distinction is consequential for claims about epistemic agency.

The process of constructing a model can also make relationships visible that a finished representation does not preserve. During a seventh-grade inquiry into stress, students developed names and identities for models through interactions with peers and adults as they worked with physical and digital materials (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2023). Their activity draws attention to the social and material conditions through which a representation acquires its meaning. The scientific relationships expressed in the finished model still require evaluation, alongside consideration of how participants influenced its development.

My classroom action research examined a related evidentiary problem through individual written explanations and later reconstructions of a photosynthesis mechanism. Relationships present in one record were sometimes absent from another, showing the value of comparing responses before drawing conclusions about what students expressed. These records did not document students' participation in determining the criteria for an adequate explanation. Tracing the interactions through which a model or explanation is judged would bring that additional dimension of classroom authority into view.

Sex determination and biological variation

How scientific categories acquire explanatory and educational authority.

The explanatory value of a biological classification depends on the processes it represents and the claims it is asked to support. A diagram linking chromosomes directly to reproductive anatomy may introduce a general pathway while leaving the mechanisms of gonadal determination, hormone production, and tissue response largely invisible. Clinical accounts of variation in sex development show why chromosomal information alone does not determine the full developmental account (Arboleda et al., 2014; Witchel, 2018). A student's question about an omitted pathway can therefore become an inquiry into the model's scope and which additional relationships a scientifically adequate explanation would require.

Problems arise when a biological observation is treated as evidence for claims that extend beyond its demonstrated relationships. Historical interpretations of the XYY chromosome pattern associated it with aggression through assumptions about masculinity (Richardson, 2013). A later systematic review found no statistical evidence that an additional Y chromosome, by itself, established a predisposition to aggression or criminal behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). The contrast shows how a category can acquire an explanatory force that its evidence does not support. It also offers a concrete way to examine the additional observations and causal reasoning needed before extending a biological classification into a claim about behaviour.

The consequences of classification also depend on the institutions that use it. Histories and critiques of intersex classification examine how diagnostic categories are constituted and how they bear on decisions about care (Fausto-Sterling, 2000; Davis, 2015). Debates over testosterone and eligibility in women's sport involve a different decision, with scholarship questioning direct inferences from hormone measurements to individual capacities and examining how such assumptions informed regulatory policies (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019; Karkazis et al., 2012). Clinical and athletic settings require evidence appropriate to their particular purposes. Across them, the movement from biological measurement to institutional judgment calls for scrutiny of what the evidence can justify.

Teaching these distinctions calls for attention to the scientific representation and to the people participating in its interpretation. Guidance for postsecondary biology recommends studying human variation as part of ordinary biological mechanisms (Zemenick et al., 2022). Scholarship on gender-inclusive education and youth engagement with curricular authority also raises questions about assumptions teachers make concerning students and about whose questions can influence what is taught (Airton, 2023; Gilbert, 2021). For my teaching, they raise a responsibility to examine scientific claims carefully without asking students to explain or disclose their own bodies and identities.

When a student challenges an established biological model, the question may concern the evidence supporting a particular relationship or the scope assigned to a classification. How the teacher responds determines whether that question remains a brief clarification or becomes part of the class's examination of the science. The same decision brings the adequacy of an explanation and the authority to shape inquiry into view.

Related writing

In What can a biological classification explain?, I examine two cases in which a scientific claim depends on what the available evidence can establish. A study of haemoglobin S and malaria reports different estimates of protection across clinical outcomes. The history of XYY chromosome research traces a different inference, from identifying a chromosomal pattern to treating it as evidence of behaviour. The essay considers what students might question when such interpretations become part of a biology lesson.
