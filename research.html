Research

A diagram of sex determination may help students understand a common developmental pathway. During a biology lesson, however, a student might ask how the diagram accounts for a case that develops differently. The teacher then has to consider whether the question points to a misunderstanding, a limitation of the representation, or a possibility worth investigating. That judgment can shape whether the student’s question becomes part of the scientific explanation the class develops.

I am interested in such decisions within justice-oriented science education. Students encounter explanations chosen through curricula, and they may participate in discussion without having much influence over the criteria used to judge their contributions. My proposed doctoral inquiry would examine how teachers and students negotiate the adequacy of scientific representations and whether students can take part in reconsidering the expectations through which reasoning is recognised as credible. I am particularly interested in a bounded sequence of secondary biology lessons on sex determination and variation in sex characteristics, while remaining attentive to the histories of science and inequalities involving gender, sexuality, race, class, and language.

Research projects

Multilingual science education scoping review

I examined 20 ERIC-indexed empirical studies to understand how researchers connected multilingual students' writing, classroom interaction, and visual representations with claims about scientific understanding. The review also considered what the reports disclosed about the language options and participation conditions surrounding those contributions.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I examined two adjustments to assessment correction. Comparing ten students’ original explanations with reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism revealed different questions for subsequent assessment, even among responses with the same later classification. Practitioner notes helped me distinguish concerns recorded while teaching from questions developed during retrospective analysis. The evidence informed the next inquiry without establishing why the explanations differed.

Read the classroom action research account →

The limitations of a single response become clearer when it can be read alongside another record. In a study examined in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation left an ecological distinction less explicit than his earlier bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's later reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships present in the original explanation. These episodes occurred under different language demands, prompts, and feedback conditions. Each nonetheless shows how an earlier account can complicate a judgment drawn from the later response alone.

Reading an earlier explanation alongside a later response may alter the questions a teacher needs to ask. In my action research, I could specify those questions through retrospective analysis, though I did not administer them in the reported inquiry. Neither this inquiry nor the scoping review examined how students might subsequently influence the assessment or curriculum decisions themselves. I hope to follow those decisions through classroom interaction.

Epistemic agency and instructional decisions

How students' questions enter the investigation, and how teachers decide what counts as progress.

Teachers ordinarily select the phenomena, materials, and explanations around which lessons are organised. These decisions help students build scientific understanding, while also establishing which contributions seem relevant. In an inquiry about biological classification, a student's question may require a teacher to decide whether to clarify a mechanism, reconsider a model, or change what evidence the class examines. Each response can open some possibilities for students' reasoning while narrowing others.

Ko and Krist (2019) describe curriculum adaptation as a means of redistributing epistemic agency. Their framework distinguishes opportunities for students to influence methods of investigation, anchoring phenomena, and the explanatory models they construct. Looking more closely at moment-to-moment instruction, Krist and colleagues (2023) used teachers' point-of-view video accounts to examine what teachers noticed when deciding whether to open or close space for students' epistemic agency. Their findings direct my attention to the substance and epistemic orientation of students' contributions, alongside a teacher's interpretation of those contributions. I want to examine how such judgments develop when the adequacy of the supplied representation itself becomes a question.

The teacher's responsibility to guide disciplinary learning remains part of this problem. DeCoito's (2024) discussion of STEM curriculum and pedagogy helps me consider the planning needed to support student-directed investigations. In Reeve's (2023) study of a Grade 6 knowledge-building classroom, students' engagement with materials challenged the intended design for exploring flight. Slotta and Linn's (2009) account of web-based inquiry offers another view of how curricular tools organise opportunities to develop explanations. These examples invite a closer examination of the relationship between a planned scientific sequence and students' influence over the work that follows.

The possibilities for changing a lesson also depend on a teacher's role within the school. Barrett (2013) followed one science teacher's developing activist commitments over time, while Puvirajah, Verma, and Webb (2012) examined how students and mentors negotiated knowledge and power during an informal robotics competition. The settings differ from my proposed inquiry, yet they offer questions about the conditions under which teachers and students can share responsibility for making and evaluating knowledge. An invitation to contribute does not, on its own, establish that students can influence the inquiry.

Calabrese Barton and Tan's (2020) framework of rightful presence moves this concern beyond the provision of opportunities to speak. It asks how historical and institutional injustices become present in local practices and how participants can challenge the practices that define legitimate knowledge and participation. I would use this framework to examine whether students can question evaluative expectations as well as the explanations to which those expectations are applied.

In a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned how a community survey might affect its respondents. Krishnamoorthy, Duncan, and Tan (2026) analysed how youth and adults negotiated care for respondents as part of deciding how the survey should produce knowledge.

The example raises a question about whose concerns can become criteria for a scientifically and ethically responsible investigation.

Cultural knowledge and biological observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognizes.

One way to examine the relationship between knowledge and the questions people are asked is to look at what they volunteer when explaining an observation. In research with Menominee and European American fish experts, participants sorted local species and explained their groupings. The Menominee experts more often mentioned habitat spontaneously, although both groups showed strong agreement when explicitly asked to sort fish by habitat (Bang et al., 2007). The comparison invites careful attention to the knowledge participants expressed and the expectations of the task that elicited it.

Those patterns emerged within the histories and practices of particular communities, so I cannot assume that comparable differences would appear in my classroom. Bang and colleagues (2012) also ask science educators to reconsider expectations that separate knowledge about nature from the cultural relationships through which people encounter it. They do give me reason to examine how students draw on their experiences when making biological observations and how a task invites them to share those observations. Learning also develops through shared activities, tools, and relationships (Nasir et al., 2014), which complicates a judgment based solely on what a student writes in response to one question.

Disciplinary participation also depends on the people and settings in which science is taught. In interviews with Muslim secondary science teachers in Canada and Pakistan, Asghar (2013) examined how teachers understood evolution in relation to their religious commitments. Alsop and Watts (2000) investigated how learners' feelings about radioactivity related to their approaches to physics. The topics, participants, and methods differ, yet both studies complicate an account of scientific learning that treats prior experiences as irrelevant to the question at hand.

Recognition introduces another difficulty. Children participating in Canadian community science clubs described engaging experiences while often separating club activities from what they considered school science (Burke and Navas Iannini, 2021). In a physics department, Gonsalves (2014) examined how doctoral students positioned themselves within gendered expectations of disciplinary competence. The distance between those settings is substantial. For my purposes, each makes it important to investigate how a particular community establishes which activities and people count as participating in science.

I would also need to question the categories I use in my analysis. Labelling an observation “everyday,” “cultural,” or “scientific” could obscure how students connect experiences beyond school with the biological explanation under discussion. I would want to establish what those distinctions help explain in the actual records before using them to characterize a student's contribution.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Language use, representation, and the interpretation of scientific explanations.

The distinction between what someone knows and what a task invites them to express also arises in multilingual classrooms. Students developing an explanation together may move among conversation, gesture, diagrams, and writing, with different relationships made explicit in each. If a later individual response leaves out a connection shown in the group's model, I would need to examine how that idea developed, how classmates responded, and what the written task required before interpreting the omission.

Language practices also affect which contributions are recognized as scientific sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). A question first expressed in Korean might be translated, developed through a diagram, or set aside as a group prepares an English explanation. Following what happened to the question would help establish whether it influenced the group's scientific work. This concern extends to who can influence the practices a classroom values, as discussed in scholarship on equity with multilingual learners (Grapin et al., 2023).

Whether learners can use their linguistic resources depends on classroom practices as well as stated language rules. Rajendram (2023) documented the agency and constraints surrounding translanguaging in Malaysian Grade 5 classrooms, including an English-only setting. In a Canadian university English programme, Galante (2022) compared plurilingual instruction with English-only instruction and measured plurilingual and pluricultural competence. These are different populations and outcomes from secondary science understanding, so neither study establishes what would happen in my classroom. They help specify the language conditions a science-education inquiry needs to document rather than merely identify students as multilingual.

Research on bilingual classroom interaction and multimodal representations provides ways to examine how an explanation develops across languages and forms of expression (Ünsal et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2014). I would compare the interactions, evolving representations, and later accounts, attending to the language options and feedback available at each point. That history may help explain why a relationship appears in one record and remains unstated in another. The science practices researchers choose to examine also vary with how they conceptualise language, as the published synthesis below documents.

Other work shows why the record should not end with the most recent written answer. In English and French classrooms, Prasad (2015) examined students' plurilingual identity texts alongside conversations about how they were made. Simon (2011) argues for attention to the relational and culturally situated dimensions of multiliteracies pedagogy. Those studies help me think about students' representations and the assumptions through which a teacher interprets them. A longitudinal study of university adjustment by Neumann, Kozak, and Gil (2023) likewise examined language proficiency alongside academic acculturation, reminding me not to interpret one language measure as a complete account of participation or success. Its population and outcomes differ from those of my proposed inquiry.

There are further questions about what a teacher does when students' contributions unsettle classroom expectations. In multilingual language teaching, Burton (2024) analysed two spoken-word-poetry activities to examine emotional tensions during antiracist teaching. This was not a secondary science study. It nonetheless invites me to document how teachers respond to disagreement and discomfort rather than assume that making room for multilingual expression resolves inequalities in whose ideas are pursued.

When a scientific model is questioned

How students evaluate representations and participate in defining the criteria.

A scientific representation can explain some aspects of a phenomenon while leaving others unexplored. When a student identifies a possible limitation, the class must examine the biological reasoning and decide what evidence is needed. That inquiry raises a further question about who has a voice in deciding what counts as an adequate explanation.

In a fifth-grade modelling inquiry, Murphy and colleagues (2023) examined students’ participation in revising a shared list of criteria for evaluating scientific models. They described how written reflection on peer criticism and subsequent classroom discussion supported this work. Cheung and Yang (2026) studied two seventh-grade groups constructing and revising models through visual, written, and verbal representations. In that study, criteria were provided for the evaluation task. The distinction between applying given standards and participating in reconsidering them helps make my research question more precise.

These studies involve different science topics and school levels from my proposed doctoral inquiry. I would examine whether, and under what circumstances, students can influence the evaluative criteria as they question an established representation.

Sex determination, variation, and explanatory limits

How students might investigate the adequacy of a biological representation.

In a lesson on sex determination, a diagram may help explain one familiar developmental pathway while leaving other pathways or outcomes unexplained. Biological sex-related characteristics can be studied through chromosomes, genetic regulation, gonadal differentiation, endocrine processes, reproductive anatomy, or reproductive function, according to the question under investigation. These features do not invariably align. I am interested in how students and teachers decide when a representation provides an adequate explanation and when its limits warrant further scientific inquiry.

The teaching principles discussed by Zemenick and colleagues (2022) encourage attention to biological diversity and the historical development of scientific explanations. Their article concerns postsecondary biology, so the implications for this proposed secondary classroom inquiry remain to be examined. The history of sex-chromosome research offers a concrete example. Claims connecting an XYY chromosome pattern with aggression circulated during the 1960s and 1970s, until larger genetic epidemiology studies undermined the proposed association. Historical analysis examines how assumptions about masculinity entered those claims (Richardson, 2013). Accounts of medicine and intersex variation also document the consequences of decisions about bodily categories (Fausto-Sterling, 2000). These histories show how classifications can acquire wider social meanings, including claims whose biological justification has not been established.

Similar care is needed when measured physiological differences are used to explain human behaviour. Claims about testosterone depend on how the hormone and behaviour were measured, who was studied, and the circumstances surrounding those measurements (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). The interactions and regulatory processes studied in endocrinology are therefore part of what I would want students to examine before using a hormone measurement to explain a difference in behaviour. Attending to those processes could help them distinguish what an experiment establishes from what remains open.

This history becomes relevant to curriculum when lessons on inheritance, development, and reproduction use the word “sex” to describe different biological features or processes. I would want to examine which features the materials identify, what evidence supports the distinction, and whether a classification is being used to explain something beyond its evidential basis. Questions students raise about those connections could then contribute to the biological investigation itself.

Questions about representation also involve the conditions of welcome and whose account can influence a curriculum. Airton (2023) argues that an ethical welcome for gender-diverse students cannot depend on teachers identifying in advance which students need particular accommodations. Reflecting on expert testimony in a dispute over an Ontario sex-education curriculum, Gilbert (2021) asks what happens when curricular advocacy reduces sexuality to apparently neutral health outcomes and misses how youth themselves remake the curriculum. I read these as challenges to approaches that treat greater representation in teaching materials as sufficient evidence of a more just classroom.

The political meanings of educational categories also deserve scrutiny. Alexander (2023) connects trans-exclusionary ideas with carceral logics in a critical commentary that is not an empirical study of science classrooms. In participatory arts-based research, Burkholder, Wright, and Keehn (2025) investigate queer joy as a collective pedagogical practice, drawing substantially on work outside formal schooling. Their different approaches help me consider the limits of an inquiry centred only on correcting biological facts. Any attempt to explore these questions in secondary biology must also attend to students' agency, privacy, and the institutional conditions that shape what can safely be discussed.

Following such a question through classroom discussion would require attention to the biological claim and to how teachers and classmates responded to it. Students must also retain control over what they disclose about themselves, since questioning a classification provides no grounds for assuming someone's identity or medical history. I would want the scientific question to be examined without asking for personal disclosure.

Proposed doctoral study

A qualitative inquiry into scientific representations, classroom judgments, and students' epistemic participation.

An organising question for the proposed study is how students’ questions about the adequacy of scientific representations are interpreted during secondary biology lessons and under what conditions those questions influence the evaluation and development of classroom explanations.

I hope to follow a bounded sequence of secondary biology lessons addressing sex determination and variation in sex characteristics. These topics bring questions about the explanatory purposes and limitations of biological representations into view. A diagram that clarifies a familiar developmental pathway may provide a useful starting point while leaving other pathways unexplained. Students might ask which developmental processes are represented, what evidence supports the explanation, and whether a different diagram or source is needed. I want to understand how teachers and students work through such questions and how the criteria for evaluating the representation are established.

The proposed inquiry would attend to distinctions that are easy to collapse in a discussion of student voice. A student might raise a scientifically warranted criticism of a model, make an incorrect biological claim, or ask a question that the present evidence cannot resolve. A teacher who rejects a claim after examining its evidence is making a different decision from one who dismisses a contribution without considering its reasoning. Both the disciplinary substance and the conditions of participation would need to be examined before interpreting what took place.

My interest is in what happens beyond the initial exchange. If a question leads the class to compare new evidence, qualify an explanation, or revise a later task, those changes need to be traced through the participants' subsequent work. A teacher may also decide to return to the existing lesson objective, and students may disagree about whether their contribution was taken seriously. Following such episodes would help me examine both the possibilities and limitations of student participation in scientific knowledge-making.

A related proposed AP Biology sequence on haemoglobin variation explores how students might question population categories when making genetic and evolutionary claims. It is a curriculum proposal rather than an implemented intervention or a second doctoral study.

Following classroom decisions across lessons

I anticipate using video-recorded classroom interaction to follow when a question emerges, how participants respond, and whether the idea returns as the lesson sequence develops. The instructional diagrams, students' written and visual representations, and relevant curricular materials would help me compare what was initially available with what participants subsequently changed or retained. The study would examine a bounded sequence of lessons rather than attempting to attribute broad learning outcomes to a single discussion.

Conversations with participating teachers and students could clarify how they interpreted selected episodes. I would consider those retrospective accounts alongside the interactional record, particularly where participants disagree about whether a suggestion influenced the lesson. Language choices, gestures, drawings, and other representational resources would also matter when tracing how an idea becomes recognisable as a scientific contribution.

Interpretation would require considering alternative explanations. A revised diagram might follow a student's proposal, reflect an existing teaching plan, or result from several people deciding together. I would look for the interaction and subsequent evidence that supports a particular account, including episodes in which a teacher's correction enabled further inquiry and others in which an invitation to question the model had little apparent influence.

Justice and accountability in interpretation

Whose contributions are recognised, and how the researcher's own judgments shape the record.

Rightful presence would inform how I examine the relation between scientific decisions and the authority to make them. A question about sex determination can be evaluated by examining developmental mechanisms and evidence. It can also be received through assumptions about which bodies or experiences are typical, whose interpretations seem credible, and what is considered appropriate to discuss. The study would need to distinguish a scientifically justified evaluation from a pattern in which a student's contribution receives little consideration for reasons bound up with classroom hierarchy or inequitable expectations.

Neither participation in the research nor discussion of a biological representation should require students to disclose their sex characteristics, gender identity, medical history, or family circumstances. Published scientific cases and instructional representations can support inquiry without making anyone's body or identity an object of classroom investigation. The research procedures would also require consent, secure handling of recordings, and attention to how classroom power affects participants' ability to disagree or decline.

My account would remain partial. Camera placement, transcription, episode selection, and the labels I apply to students' reasoning all shape what readers can see, a concern also developed in Haraway's (1988) writing on situated knowledge. I would document these choices and attend to moments that complicate my expectations. Where participants offer different accounts of an exchange, the differences should remain visible rather than being treated as errors to be reconciled into one interpretation.

Scholarly writing

In a separate essay on biological classification, I examine how evidence about haemoglobin variation and sex chromosomes has been interpreted beyond the relationships it can establish. The discussion develops questions about the scientific authority of categories and what students might be able to change when those categories are examined in class. This is a critical reading of published scholarship, independent of my two empirical inquiries.
