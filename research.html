Justice-oriented and anti-colonial science education

Research

The models and explanations students encounter in school science carry histories of how people have investigated and classified the world. Curriculum decisions bring some of those accounts into the classroom and leave others unexplored. If a student notices that a biological classification cannot account for a particular form of variation, the teacher and class face a decision about whether to pursue that question and what evidence to consider. I want to understand how these decisions unfold and how much influence students have over the inquiry that follows.

Whose questions receive attention is connected to broader histories of scientific and educational authority. Racism, colonial histories, and inequalities involving gender, sexuality, class, and language can influence which knowledge schools present as authoritative and whose contributions are pursued. I am drawn to anti-racist, anti-colonial, and queer- and trans-inclusive science education through these questions about knowledge and participation. My existing research has examined how teachers and researchers interpret scientific explanations produced under different conditions. That work has also led me to consider an earlier set of decisions about who helps choose the questions, representations, and evidence through which a classroom investigation develops.

Classroom action research · 2026 · 28 AP Biology students

Post-assessment reflection and mechanistic reasoning

In a class of 28 AP Biology students, I followed explanations across three unit assessments and examined ten pairs of original responses and later independent reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions extended or repaired earlier causal pathways, while others omitted relationships that had appeared before. The differences helped me identify what I still needed to ask students about their reasoning.

Read the paired-record inquiry →

The limitations of a single response become clearer when it can be read alongside another record. In a study examined in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation left an ecological distinction less explicit than his earlier bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's later reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships present in the original explanation. These episodes occurred under different language demands, prompts, and feedback conditions. Each nonetheless shows how an earlier account can complicate a judgment drawn from the later response alone.

When an earlier record changes how a later response can be interpreted, it also changes the grounds on which a teacher might decide what to ask next. A missing scientific relationship may invite clarification or a further representation, depending on what the student has previously expressed. Although my inquiries examined the evidence available for interpreting student explanations, they did not follow how students might influence the questions and tasks that produced those explanations. I hope to investigate those decisions through classroom interaction.

Curriculum decisions and student participation

Teachers often begin an investigation by selecting the phenomenon, model, and sources. When students propose different evidence or raise concerns about the investigation itself, those initial choices become open to reconsideration. Whether the proposal changes the work then depends on how teachers and classmates assess it and who can participate in the decision. I want to follow that process through to the questions the class pursues and the explanations it eventually develops.

Decisions about which questions and sources a class pursues are also shaped by histories of whose knowledge school science has recognized. Expectations about nature and culture can restrict participation by students from historically nondominant communities (Bang et al., 2012). The concept of rightful presence extends this concern to whether participants can influence a learning community's practices and authority (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). For my own research, these questions would require learning about the particular histories and relationships of the communities involved, examining my assumptions, and considering how participants could help determine what deserves investigation.

A classroom case from the literature

During a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned how a community survey might affect those asked to respond. Their negotiations with adults brought care for respondents into decisions about the research instrument (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

The students helped determine what counted as a responsible way to collect evidence.

Cultural knowledge and biological observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognizes.

One way to examine the relationship between knowledge and the questions people are asked is to look at what they volunteer when explaining an observation. In research with Menominee and European American fish experts, participants sorted local species and explained their groupings. The Menominee experts more often mentioned habitat spontaneously, although both groups showed strong agreement when explicitly asked to sort fish by habitat (Bang et al., 2007). The comparison invites careful attention to the knowledge participants expressed and the expectations of the task that elicited it.

Those patterns emerged within the histories and practices of particular communities, so I cannot assume that comparable differences would appear in my classroom. They do give me reason to examine how students draw on their experiences when making biological observations and how a task invites them to share those observations. Learning also develops through shared activities, tools, and relationships (Nasir et al., 2014), which complicates a judgment based solely on what a student writes in response to one question.

I would also need to question the categories I use in my analysis. Labelling an observation “everyday,” “cultural,” or “scientific” could obscure how students connect experiences beyond school with the biological explanation under discussion. I would want to establish what those distinctions help explain in the actual records before using them to characterize a student's contribution.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Language use, representation, and the interpretation of scientific explanations.

The distinction between what someone knows and what a task invites them to express also arises in multilingual classrooms. Students developing an explanation together may move among conversation, gesture, diagrams, and writing, with different relationships made explicit in each. If a later individual response leaves out a connection shown in the group's model, I would need to examine how that idea developed, how classmates responded, and what the written task required before interpreting the omission.

Language practices also affect which contributions are recognized as scientific sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). A question first expressed in Korean might be translated, developed through a diagram, or set aside as a group prepares an English explanation. Following what happened to the question would help establish whether it influenced the group's scientific work. This concern extends to who can influence the practices a classroom values, as discussed in scholarship on equity with multilingual learners (Grapin et al., 2023).

Research on bilingual classroom interaction and multimodal representations provides ways to examine how an explanation develops across languages and forms of expression (Ünsal et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2014). I would compare the interactions, evolving representations, and later accounts, attending to the language options and feedback available at each point. That history may help explain why a relationship appears in one record and remains unstated in another.

Biological sex and scientific classification

Histories of genetics, decisions about measurement, and the explanatory limits of a category.

Questions about scientific authority become especially consequential when classifications are used to describe human bodies and variation. In biological research, sex may be operationalized through chromosomes, gonadal development, reproductive anatomy, endocrine processes, or reproductive function, depending on the problem under investigation. These features do not always vary together, so the measurements and purposes behind a classification need to be considered before extending it to broader claims about people's bodies and lives.

The history of sex-chromosome research offers a concrete example. Claims connecting an XYY chromosome pattern with aggression circulated during the 1960s and 1970s, until larger genetic epidemiology studies undermined the proposed association. Historical analysis examines how assumptions about masculinity entered those claims (Richardson, 2013). Accounts of medicine and intersex variation also document the consequences of decisions about bodily categories (Fausto-Sterling, 2000). These histories show how classifications can acquire wider social meanings, including claims whose biological justification has not been established.

Similar care is needed when measured physiological differences are used to explain human behaviour. Claims about testosterone depend on how the hormone and behaviour were measured, who was studied, and the circumstances surrounding those measurements (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). The interactions and regulatory processes studied in endocrinology are therefore part of what I would want students to examine before using a hormone measurement to explain a difference in behaviour. Attending to those processes could help them distinguish what an experiment establishes from what remains open.

This history becomes relevant to curriculum when lessons on inheritance, development, and reproduction use the word “sex” to describe different biological features or processes. I would want to examine which features the materials identify, what evidence supports the distinction, and whether a classification is being used to explain something beyond its evidential basis. Questions students raise about those connections could then contribute to the biological investigation itself.

Following such a question through classroom discussion would require attention to the biological claim and to how teachers and classmates responded to it. Students must also retain control over what they disclose about themselves, since questioning a classification provides no grounds for assuming someone's identity or medical history. I would want the scientific question to be examined without asking for personal disclosure.

A possible direction for doctoral research

Biological classification, classroom inquiry, and students' influence on scientific work.

I would like to follow a secondary biology class as students examine a model of biological sex or human variation. A classification may be appropriate for a particular scientific question while leaving developmental or physiological relationships unexplained in another. If students raise such a concern, the class could compare measurements, revisit the model's intended use, or consult a different source. I want to understand how teachers and students decide which questions to pursue and whether students' contributions influence what happens next.

That inquiry would also involve examining whose bodies and experiences the curriculum takes as typical, how particular classifications acquired authority, and how students' ethical concerns are treated alongside their scientific questions. Following these relationships would require attention to the biological reasoning and to the decisions that shape participation. Collaborative curriculum development with students and teachers is a longer-term interest whose form would depend on the particular setting and the priorities of those involved.

Related questions about classification also arise in population genetics. In a proposed AP Biology sequence on haemoglobin variation, I have considered how students might question the categories used to explain allele distributions and propose further evidence. What students and teachers would subsequently do with those proposals remains a question for investigation.

Tracing changes in classroom inquiry

To examine whether a student's question influenced a classroom investigation, I would need to follow the work beyond that exchange. A revised diagram or a new source of evidence might reflect the student's proposal, a teacher's existing plan, or a decision reached by several participants. Establishing the relationship would require tracing who suggested the change, how alternatives were assessed, and what the class subsequently did.

The original task, classroom recordings, subsequent materials, and participants' accounts could support that comparison. They could also reveal disagreements about why a change occurred or why an alternative was set aside. These disagreements would be part of the inquiry, particularly when a teacher's authority shaped which account became accepted.

Accountability in recording and interpretation

Researcher decisions and participants' opportunities to question an account.

Following a classroom decision would also require examining how I produced the research record. Camera placement, transcription, and episode selection each affect which parts of an interaction remain available for interpretation. Accounts of situated knowledge ask researchers to consider how their positions shape these choices (Haraway, 1988). I would document them so that participants and readers could examine the grounds for my conclusions, including details that complicate my interpretation.

I could return to a recorded interaction with participants and ask how they understood a question that I had initially interpreted as uncertainty, disagreement, or resistance to a category. Their accounts might differ from mine or from one another's, particularly about why a proposal was pursued or set aside. Documenting those differences, along with any changes to my interpretation, would make the grounds for my conclusions more open to examination.

Scholarly writing

In a separate essay on biological classification, I examine how evidence about haemoglobin variation and sex chromosomes has been interpreted beyond the relationships it can establish. The discussion develops questions about the scientific authority of categories and what students might be able to change when those categories are examined in class. This is a critical reading of published scholarship, independent of my two empirical inquiries.
