Research

In a biology lesson, students may examine a simplified diagram tracing human sex development from chromosomes to reproductive anatomy. A student might ask how differences in hormone responses fit into that pathway. The teacher could explain the missing processes and continue with the lesson, or invite students to examine what the diagram leaves out and consider which relationships a more adequate model should represent.

Teachers make these decisions within curricular and disciplinary expectations, choosing which questions to pursue and whether students' explanations can shape the work that follows. These judgments take place within institutions and histories marked by racism, colonialism, and inequalities connected to gender, sexuality, social class, and language. My interest in justice-oriented science education includes how students can participate in evaluating scientific representations and the grounds on which their contributions are recognized. Scholarship on epistemic agency and rightful presence helps me examine these relationships alongside the scientific reasoning involved.

Research projects

Multilingual science education scoping review

I examined 20 empirical studies retrieved through ERIC to consider how researchers inferred multilingual students’ science understanding from written responses, classroom interaction, and visual work. I also examined the reporting of language options and participation conditions, since these can affect what a student’s contribution allows a researcher to conclude.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I studied students’ explanations across assessments and two adjustments to the feedback routine. For ten students whose Unit 3 explanations did not meet the study's completeness criteria, I compared the original responses with later closed-resource reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions developed fuller causal pathways, while others omitted relationships previously expressed. The comparisons suggested different questions for subsequent assessment, which were not administered during the inquiry.

The record used to assess an explanation can change which scientific relationships are visible. In a study included in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation made an ecological distinction less explicit than his bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's closed-resource reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships expressed in an earlier response. The cases arose under different task and language conditions. Read together, they caution against treating an absent relationship in one response as a complete account of a student's understanding.

My projects examined the grounds for inferring scientific understanding from students' work. A teacher may judge an explanation incomplete on scientific grounds and still encounter a student who questions that interpretation. What remains less visible in an assessment record is how such a judgment is explained and whether the student can influence what happens next.

Research interests

The judgment made about a student's response can shape what the class investigates next. Teacher noticing offers a way to examine how reasoning becomes available for consideration; epistemic agency and rightful presence raise further questions about who can influence the direction and standards of inquiry. The linguistic and representational forms of students' contributions shape both the evidence available and the judgments made from it.

Classroom authority and epistemic agency

How instructional decisions shape students' opportunities to pursue scientific questions.

A teacher can close off a line of inquiry before hearing the reasoning that prompted it. In an episode discussed by Cowie and colleagues, a teacher dismissed an unexpected answer without first exploring the students' reasoning about how animals could be compared (Cowie et al., 2018). The exchange illustrates a difficulty that arises when teachers interpret students' developing ideas and decide which contributions warrant further attention (Krist et al., 2023). Students' epistemic agency is implicated in these judgments. Their opportunities to participate in scientific knowledge-making depend partly on whether their ideas can influence the questions being pursued and the work undertaken to investigate them (Miller et al., 2018; Stroupe, 2014).

Such decisions occur within lessons whose purposes and resources have often been established in advance. Teachers can create opportunities for students to contribute to the direction of inquiry, yet sustaining that inquiry requires attention to disciplinary goals and the support students need to develop explanations (Ko & Krist, 2019; DeCoito, 2024). A Grade 6 investigation of flight illustrates how these responsibilities can come into tension. As students worked with materials, their investigations challenged the sequence of knowledge-building activities the teacher had prepared (Reeve, 2023). The difficulty extends beyond accommodating an unexpected question. Teachers must decide how the question might be investigated, what scientific relationships require attention, and how the available time and materials can support that work.

Students may also have considerable freedom to select an inquiry topic while exercising less influence over the decisions that follow. In a Grade 7 Science and Technology project, students chose to investigate Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls and prepared a public Red Dress exhibition. Some nevertheless reported that peers overlooked their contributions during collaboration (Jao et al., 2026). Another dimension of participation emerges when students help determine how an investigation should proceed. During a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned whether a proposed community survey might upset respondents. Their concerns became part of discussions with adults about responsible evidence gathering (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026). These accounts locate student influence at different points in inquiry, from choosing a direction to negotiating the practices and criteria through which the work is carried out.

The distinction raises questions about who is authorized to judge scientific work. Productive disciplinary engagement connects students' authority with their responsibilities toward a shared problem and the resources available for investigating it (Engle & Conant, 2002). Rightful presence brings attention to the historically unequal conditions through which particular contributions and challenges to established practices become recognizable and legitimate (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). A classroom may invite students to develop explanations while leaving the standards for evaluating those explanations entirely with the teacher. Examining participation therefore requires attention to what students can contribute and to the opportunities they have to question the grounds on which their contributions are judged.

Teachers' professional conversations offer another setting in which these judgments can be examined. Sustained professional learning has supported science teachers in attending more closely to students' epistemic practices, and collaborative instructional-material development creates opportunities to reconsider classroom approaches (Shim & Thompson, 2022; Jao & McDougall, 2015). Yet the focus of collaboration can shape which aspects of student thinking receive scrutiny. In rehearsals of mathematical defining, participants discussed pedagogical approaches more extensively than students' reasoning about definitions (Kobiela et al., 2023). Although the disciplinary context differs, the finding raises a relevant question for science teacher learning. When teachers examine a lesson together, how often do they investigate the reasoning behind students' contributions, including contributions that seem inconsistent with the lesson's anticipated direction?

An unexpected student question brings these concerns into an ordinary instructional decision. A teacher may need to evaluate the scientific adequacy of a proposed explanation while deciding whether the question warrants further investigation. Students' opportunities to influence the inquiry depend partly on how that judgment is reached, explained, and opened to further discussion. I am interested in understanding the considerations that shape these responses and what happens to students' questions afterward, including whether they return to the classroom's work in a different form.

During a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned whether a proposed community survey might upset respondents. As students and adults considered how to proceed, care for respondents became part of what counted as a responsible way to gather information. Their discussions show how students could help establish criteria for the investigation through negotiations of classroom authority (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Recognizing scientific reasoning

How classroom expectations shape which contributions become visible and consequential.

Cultural knowledge and participation

How the form of a task influences the knowledge people make visible.

The questions researchers and teachers ask can affect which relationships participants express in their explanations. Studies of ecological reasoning have examined how knowledge of the natural world develops through cultural practices and relationships that may be represented differently in school science (Bang & Medin, 2010; Bang et al., 2012). In a comparison of Menominee and European American fish experts, Menominee participants more frequently invoked habitat when spontaneously grouping fish. When the task explicitly requested grouping by habitat, the groups showed substantial agreement (Bang et al., 2007). The change in responses complicates an interpretation based solely on what participants mentioned initially. A task can make particular relationships easier to express, leaving the evaluator to consider how the question shaped the evidence available for judging an explanation.

The setting in which scientific knowledge is expressed introduces a related problem. Community science activities can support meaningful relationships and practical engagement, although participants' experiences outside school may remain separated from the forms of science recognized within it (Burke & Navas Iannini, 2021; Gonsalves & Rahm, 2023). In the conversation club examined by Gonsalves and Rahm, girls drew on shared interests and relationships to position themselves as knowledgeable about science. The recognition available through those interactions remained closely associated with the club. Their experiences invite examination of how opportunities to be recognized as knowledgeable travel across settings. Familiarity with scientific ideas may support participation in one community without producing comparable opportunities to contribute in another.

Even within a setting, the interpretation of a contribution can be shaped by expectations concerning the person offering it. Gendered judgments of scientific competence affected how doctoral physics students navigated recognition (Gonsalves, 2014). Accounts of women of colour pursuing science likewise show how recognition develops through different experiences and social relationships (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Learning, understood as participation in cultural practices, involves the relationships through which particular ways of knowing become meaningful and recognizable (Nasir et al., 2014). These perspectives complicate the apparent simplicity of assessing whether an explanation is scientifically credible. An evaluator's judgment may involve assumptions about whose knowledge is credible, even when the immediate task concerns a particular scientific claim.

Researchers encounter a comparable responsibility when deciding which contributions will count as evidence of learning. Choices about recording, selecting, and analysing classroom interactions shape what remains available for interpretation (Derry et al., 2010). Haraway's account of situated knowledge further directs attention to the researcher's position within the production and interpretation of evidence (Haraway, 1988). A student's reference to familiar experience, for instance, may express a scientific relationship that becomes less visible when the contribution is classified under a broad category such as everyday knowledge. Understanding what the student has expressed requires examining the question that elicited the response and the surrounding interaction. It also requires making the researcher's interpretive decisions open to scrutiny.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

What becomes visible as ideas move through talk, writing, and shared representations.

Written explanations provide a record of the scientific relationships students expressed under particular task conditions. Other records may complicate an interpretation based on that response alone. In my scoping review of research on multilingual learners' science understanding, researchers drew inferences from students' written work, classroom interactions, and representations. These forms of evidence supported different routes to claims about understanding. A relationship omitted from a submitted explanation might have been expressed during earlier discussion or through work with a representation. Comparing such records requires attention to what each task invited students to do and to the linguistic resources available at different points in the activity.

The way researchers conceptualize language can shape the scientific practices they examine. A synthesis by González-Howard and colleagues found differences in research attention associated with linguistic and more expansive views of language (González-Howard et al., 2023). Grapin and colleagues' discussion of language and disciplinary practice raises further questions about how students gain access to established forms of participation and which linguistic resources schools recognize (Grapin et al., 2023). Together, these concerns suggest that the interpretation of scientific understanding is shaped by the practices researchers choose to examine and by the language practices considered relevant to those activities.

Classroom language expectations can influence what students are able to contribute during an activity. In a Malaysian Grade 5 English-language classroom operating under an English-only policy, students used translanguaging with peers, although classroom expectations and responses constrained those practices (Rajendram, 2023). The example concerns an English-language classroom, but it illustrates how institutional expectations can shape access to linguistic resources. In science discussions, students also need opportunities to question claims and request justification. Research on critique in middle-school science has examined how these interactions can support epistemic agency (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020). The two lines of work address different aspects of participation. One concerns the resources students can draw upon to express ideas; the other concerns how those ideas can be examined and challenged through interaction. Both become relevant when considering the conditions under which students' scientific reasoning can develop publicly.

Students may develop explanations across forms of expression that are difficult to separate in the finished work. Observations of bilingual students in elementary science have shown differences in linguistic resource use between teacher-led and small-group activities (Ünsal et al., 2018). Outside school, emergent bilingual learners investigating electricity combined linguistic and nonlinguistic resources while developing models with others (Suárez, 2020). These activities invite attention to the relationships expressed through gestures, materials, and talk as representations take shape. A multimodal framework can help distinguish resources combined within a representation from relationships made visible as students move between forms of representation (Tang et al., 2014). A finished diagram may preserve particular explanatory relationships while leaving the interaction through which they were developed inaccessible to someone examining the diagram alone.

The evidence available to teachers does not determine how they will interpret it. During high school science assessment co-design, teachers described students' ideas and linguistic resources through both asset- and deficit-oriented framings, with more asset-oriented descriptions emerging during particular phases of practice and reflection (Fine & Furtak, 2024). Such variation suggests that teachers' interpretations can change through professional activity, although the implications for subsequent classroom decisions require further examination. I am particularly interested in following the relationship between the explanations students express, the interpretations teachers develop, and the opportunities students encounter in later classroom interactions.

Scientific representations and their limits

The explanatory purposes of models, and the questions that may lead students to reconsider them.

Evaluating and revising scientific models

When the assumptions of a model become part of the investigation.

A scientific model may be adequate for one explanatory purpose while leaving relationships unresolved that become important for another. Its usefulness must therefore be considered in relation to the phenomenon under investigation and the questions the representation is intended to address. A learning progression for scientific modelling describes students' developing capacity to construct and revise representations for explanation and prediction (Schwarz et al., 2009). Work on argumentation likewise situates the evaluation of claims within the problems and activities that give them meaning (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015). These perspectives invite attention to why a particular relationship is included in a model and what additional evidence or mechanisms would be needed if its explanatory purpose changed.

Students can participate in evaluating models without necessarily influencing the criteria used to judge them. In a fifth-grade inquiry, students helped revise their class's model-evaluation criteria (Murphy et al., 2023). Seventh-grade students in another investigation evaluated and improved models using criteria established by researchers and teachers (Cheung & Yang, 2026). Both studies concern students' evaluative activity, yet the authority to establish the basis of evaluation was distributed differently. This difference is important when interpreting evidence of epistemic agency. Revising a model in response to established standards provides one opportunity for disciplinary participation. Deliberating about the standards themselves introduces a further opportunity to shape how scientific work is judged.

The meaning of a representation also develops through the interactions surrounding its construction. During a seventh-grade inquiry into stress, students worked with physical and digital materials while developing names and identities for models through exchanges with peers and adults (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2023). The work draws attention to the relationships and material practices through which representations acquire meaning within an investigation. Examining the finished model remains necessary for evaluating the scientific relationships it expresses. Following its development can provide additional evidence about how participants understood the representation and influenced the decisions that shaped it.

My classroom action research encountered a related problem when I compared individual written explanations with later reconstructions of a photosynthesis mechanism. Some scientific relationships present in one record were absent from another. Comparing successive responses helped me interpret what students had expressed, while the records also placed limits on what I could conclude about their reasoning. They did not document how students participated in determining the criteria for an adequate explanation. That limitation has shaped a further question about the interactions through which scientific representations are judged. When a student identifies a missing relationship or questions an established model, how do teachers and students decide whether the concern should change the model, the criteria used to evaluate it, or the inquiry being pursued?

Sex determination and biological variation

How scientific categories acquire explanatory and educational authority.

The explanatory value of a biological classification depends on the processes it represents and the claims it is asked to support. A diagram linking chromosomes directly to reproductive anatomy may introduce a general pathway while leaving the mechanisms of gonadal determination, hormone production, and tissue response largely invisible. Clinical accounts of variation in sex development show why chromosomal information alone does not determine the full developmental account (Arboleda et al., 2014; Witchel, 2018). Therefore, a student's question about an omitted pathway can become an inquiry into the model's scope and which additional relationships a scientifically adequate explanation would require.

Problems arise when a biological observation is treated as evidence for claims that extend beyond its demonstrated relationships. Historical interpretations of the XYY chromosome pattern associated it with aggression through assumptions about masculinity (Richardson, 2013). A later systematic review found no statistical evidence that an additional Y chromosome, by itself, established a predisposition to aggression or criminal behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). The contrast shows how a category can acquire an explanatory force that its evidence does not support. It also offers a concrete way to examine the additional observations and causal reasoning needed before extending a biological classification into a claim about behaviour.

The consequences of classification also depend on the institutions that use it. Histories and critiques of intersex classification examine how diagnostic categories are constituted and how they bear on decisions about care (Fausto-Sterling, 2000; Davis, 2015). Debates over testosterone and eligibility in women's sport involve a different decision, with scholarship questioning direct inferences from hormone measurements to individual capacities and examining how such assumptions informed regulatory policies (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019; Karkazis et al., 2012). Clinical and athletic settings require evidence appropriate to their particular purposes. Across them, the movement from biological measurement to institutional judgment calls for scrutiny of what the evidence can justify.

Teaching these distinctions calls for attention to the scientific representation and to the people participating in its interpretation. Guidance for postsecondary biology recommends studying human variation as part of ordinary biological mechanisms (Zemenick et al., 2022). Scholarship on gender-inclusive education and youth engagement with curricular authority also raises questions about assumptions teachers make concerning students and about whose questions can influence what is taught (Airton, 2023; Gilbert, 2021). For my teaching, they raise a responsibility to examine scientific claims carefully without asking students to explain or disclose their own bodies and identities.

When a student challenges an established biological model, the question may concern the evidence supporting a particular relationship or the scope assigned to a classification. How the teacher responds determines whether that question remains a brief clarification or becomes part of the class's examination of the science. The same decision brings the adequacy of an explanation and the authority to shape inquiry into view.

Related writing

In What can a biological classification explain?, I examine two cases in which a scientific claim depends on what the available evidence can establish. A study of haemoglobin S and malaria reports different estimates of protection across clinical outcomes. The history of XYY chromosome research traces a different inference, from identifying a chromosomal pattern to treating it as evidence of behaviour. The essay considers what students might question when such interpretations become part of a biology lesson.
