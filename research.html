Justice-oriented science education

Research

In a science classroom, an explanation is produced under particular conditions. A written response, a model, and a discussion may make different parts of a student's reasoning visible, and those differences affect what a teacher can reasonably conclude. My scoping review examined how 20 empirical studies interpreted multilingual students' scientific work. In a classroom action research inquiry, I followed AP Biology students' explanations across assessments, feedback, and a later reconstruction.

These inquiries have also made me attentive to what happens after students contribute an idea. A question about a model's assumptions may lead a class to seek different evidence; another question may be discussed without changing the investigation. I am interested in how teachers and students reach those decisions, particularly when language, prior experiences, and biological classifications influence what is taken seriously in a lesson. Scholarship on participation and the histories of biological knowledge offers ways to examine those decisions and their consequences.

Classroom action research · 2026 · 28 AP Biology students

Post-assessment reflection and mechanistic reasoning

In a class of 28 AP Biology students, I followed explanations across three unit assessments and examined ten pairs of original responses and later independent reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions extended or repaired earlier causal pathways, while others omitted relationships that had appeared before. The differences helped me identify what I still needed to ask students about their reasoning.

Read the paired-record inquiry →

Two differences in the records have stayed with me. In a study examined in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation made an ecological distinction less explicit than in his earlier bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's later reconstruction omitted photosynthetic connections present in the original explanation. The language demands, prompts, and feedback differed between these cases, so they cannot be treated as equivalent. Yet both show how a judgment based on one response changes when earlier work is considered.

Neither inquiry followed a student's challenge to a scientific model or classification into the decisions made by the class afterward. I am interested in how such questions affect the evidence students seek, the explanations they revise, and the investigations they pursue. Studying those decisions would require following an idea beyond the exchange in which it first appeared and examining who was able to influence what happened next.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Language use, representation, and the interpretation of scientific explanations.

In English-medium science classrooms, students may develop an explanation through conversation, gesture, diagrams, numerical relationships, and writing. Consider a scientific relationship that students work through in peer discussion or an annotated model and later explain individually in English. A connection expressed during the discussion may be abbreviated or left implicit in the written response. Interpreting that difference requires knowing what each task asked students to do and which language resources and representations were available.

To follow such an idea across activities, I would examine the original discussion, the developing representation, the later explanation, and any feedback between them. The comparison could identify relationships students retained, revised, or omitted under different conditions. A fuller account in one setting would still need to be interpreted in relation to the demands of that task.

Those judgments also affect participation. Assumptions about language influence which contributions teachers and researchers recognize as scientific sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). Questions of equity extend to whether students can influence what a classroom values as scientific work, alongside their access to established expectations (Grapin et al., 2023). The teacher's response to an explanation can therefore affect what enters the discussion and whose questions the class pursues.

Bilingual classroom research examines how patterns of interaction create opportunities for meaning-making (Ünsal et al., 2018). Multimodal analysis, meanwhile, attends both to how an idea develops across activities and to relationships among words, images, and symbols within a contribution (Tang et al., 2014). These approaches would help me examine how scientific relationships travel between forms of expression while documenting the conditions in which students produced them.

Curriculum decisions and student participation

A teacher's choice of phenomenon, source, or model establishes where a class investigation begins. That direction can change when a student proposes another explanation or questions what the example leaves out. I am interested in the decisions that follow, especially whether the student's contribution influences which evidence is sought and which scientific questions the class continues to pursue.

These decisions are connected to the histories and forms of knowledge a curriculum recognizes. Expectations about nature and culture can restrict how students from historically nondominant communities participate in science (Bang et al., 2012). The concept of rightful presence further asks whose contributions can influence the practices and authority of a learning community (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). I would want to trace what happens after a concern is raised, including who can question a proposed response and whose account of the situation is taken seriously. Developing this work as a decolonial inquiry would also require learning about the particular histories and relationships of the communities involved.

A classroom case from the literature

During a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students questioned how a community survey might affect those asked to respond. Their negotiations with adults brought care for respondents into decisions about the research instrument (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

The students helped determine what counted as a responsible way to collect evidence.

Cultural knowledge and biological observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognizes.

What students bring into a scientific discussion depends partly on what the task has invited them to notice. In research with Menominee and European American fish experts, participants sorted local fish species and explained their groupings. Both groups shared substantial knowledge, while Menominee participants more frequently attended to habitat in their spontaneous explanations. When researchers explicitly asked participants to sort by habitat, the groups showed strong agreement (Bang et al., 2007). The contrast raises a question about which relationships a task makes salient and which may remain unexpressed.

The communities and expertise examined in that study have their own histories. In my classroom, I would need to learn how students developed their observations and what a biology question allowed them to express before interpreting a difference as a limit of understanding. Accounts of learning as a cultural process also draw attention to how knowledge develops through activities, tools, and relationships (Nasir et al., 2014). This becomes especially relevant when students recognize ecological relationships that were absent from a worksheet or model.

My own categories would require examination as well. Labelling an observation “everyday,” “cultural,” or “scientific” could obscure how students connect experiences beyond school with the biological explanation under discussion. I would want to establish what those distinctions help explain in the actual records before using them to characterize a student's contribution.

Biological sex and scientific classification

Histories of genetics, decisions about measurement, and the explanatory limits of a category.

Biological sex is examined through related features such as chromosomes, gonadal development, reproductive anatomy, endocrine processes, and reproductive function. These features do not always vary together, and different investigations may have reasons to measure different aspects of them. I am interested in how scientists justify a classification for a particular question and what happens when a category developed for that purpose is treated as though it provides a broader explanation of people's bodies.

The history of sex-chromosome research offers a concrete example. Claims that people with an XYY chromosome pattern were predisposed to aggression circulated during the 1960s and 1970s, before larger genetic epidemiology studies undermined the proposed association. Historical analysis traces how assumptions about masculinity entered those claims, alongside scientific debates over the evolution of the Y chromosome (Richardson, 2013). Accounts of medicine and intersex variation further examine the consequences of how bodies have been classified (Fausto-Sterling, 2000). These histories have led me to examine more carefully which biological features a study measures and which broader claims its evidence permits.

Questions about interpretation also arise when a measured physiological difference is used to explain human behaviour. Claims about testosterone, for example, need to be examined in relation to the populations, measurements, and circumstances on which they rest (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). I enjoy the detail of endocrinology and want that detail to remain present in the explanation. Findings from a particular experiment may illuminate a biological process while leaving broader claims about people unresolved.

Secondary biology materials provide another setting in which these questions can be examined. Lessons on inheritance, development, and reproduction may use the word “sex” while referring to different features or mechanisms. Comparing the features named, the evidence provided, and the claims drawn from them could help identify where a classification explains the biology and where its explanatory reach is left unspecified.

These questions also arise when students challenge a classification or offer a biological counterexample during a lesson. Examining their contributions would involve following the scientific claim and the responses of teachers and classmates, with students retaining control over what they disclose. No one should have to explain their identity or medical history to participate in such a discussion.

Tracing changes in classroom inquiry

To examine whether a student's question influenced a classroom investigation, I would need to follow the work beyond that exchange. A revised diagram or a new source of evidence might reflect the student's proposal, a teacher's existing plan, or a decision reached by several participants. Establishing the relationship would require tracing who suggested the change, how alternatives were assessed, and what the class subsequently did.

The original task, classroom recordings, subsequent materials, and participants' accounts could support that comparison. They could also reveal disagreements about why a change occurred or why an alternative was set aside. These disagreements would be part of the inquiry, particularly when a teacher's authority shaped which account became accepted.

Interpreting classroom evidence responsibly

Recording choices, participants' accounts, and the position from which a claim is made.

The records used to follow a classroom decision have limits of their own. A camera, a transcript, and a coding scheme preserve different parts of an interaction, and my decisions about camera placement, transcription, and episode selection would affect the account I could produce. Situated accounts of knowledge ask researchers to examine how their position shapes those choices (Haraway, 1988). Documenting the decisions would allow readers and participants to question the basis of an interpretation, especially when they understood the exchange differently.

If I described a student's question as disagreement, uncertainty, or resistance to a category, I would need to identify what in the interaction supported that reading. Returning to the recording with the student and other participants might lead me to reconsider the account. Their perspectives would form part of the analysis, although agreement alone would not establish that one interpretation was correct.

I would also keep records that complicate my initial interpretation and document how analytic categories were applied. Participants should have opportunities to question my account, especially given the authority a teacher or researcher holds within a school. Such opportunities cannot remove that imbalance, but they can make its effects more open to scrutiny.
