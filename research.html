Justice-oriented science education

Research

Teaching biology and chemistry has made me more attentive to the choices involved in a science lesson. The questions I prepare, the examples I use, and the responses I assess each affect what I can learn about students' scientific thinking.

Students bring experiences and questions that may challenge what I have prepared. I want to understand how teachers respond to those contributions and how students can participate in decisions about the scientific work of a class. My interests in justice-oriented science education include the racialized, gendered, and linguistic conditions of participation. Attending to those conditions also requires careful attention to the biology and chemistry being taught.

Curriculum studies, multilingual science education, and histories of scientific classification have given me different ways to examine these questions. I am especially interested in where an interpretation of student reasoning becomes more certain than the available evidence permits. My completed classroom inquiry and scoping review are described later on this page.

Curriculum decisions and student participation

When teachers select a phenomenon or an investigation, they also make decisions about which explanations and questions students will encounter. I want to consider what happens when students raise questions that fall outside those initial choices. A lesson can remain scientifically accurate while leaving little room to examine why particular examples, methods, or historical accounts were selected.

Bang and colleagues (2012) examine how expectations about nature and culture can limit the knowledge recognized in school science and the roles available to students from historically nondominant communities. Calabrese Barton and Tan's account of rightful presence (2020) similarly considers participation in relation to power and authority within learning communities. These arguments lead me to ask which curriculum decisions students can question or influence. I would also need to learn from the particular histories and relationships of the communities involved before describing a classroom inquiry as decolonial work.

A question for curriculum work

Can a student's concern lead to a change in the evidence collected, the explanation developed, or the question the class is pursuing?

I would want to know what happened after the concern was raised, and who had a say in what followed.

Cultural knowledge and biological observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognizes.

Students can attend to different relationships among organisms when they work with the same biological setting. A task concerned with taxonomic similarity may direct their observations differently from one examining ecological interactions. Before interpreting a difference in their explanations, I would want to understand what each student was trying to account for and what experience they brought to the task.

Bang, Medin, and Atran (2007) examine relationships between cultural experience and models of nature, questioning whether the categories through which researchers interpret reasoning are themselves culturally neutral. Nasir and colleagues (2014) define cultural practices as historically developed and continually reshaped through communities' activities, tools, relationships, and ways of making meaning. Together these accounts make the conditions of a task consequential. I would need to understand how students learned to notice particular biological relationships before treating differences between their explanations as differences in scientific capability.

I am interested in whether school investigations make room for ecological relationships that students have learned to notice in other settings. An analysis could trace which organisms, processes, and connections students identify when engaging with local ecological knowledge and with disciplinary models. The interpretive challenge would include documenting the source and use of these ideas without treating “everyday,” “cultural,” and “scientific” as self-evident or mutually exclusive kinds of knowledge.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Language use, representation, and the interpretation of scientific explanations.

In English-medium science classrooms, students may use a wider range of linguistic and representational resources than an assessment makes available. Their explanations can develop through conversation, gesture, diagrams, numerical relationships, and writing. I want to understand how teachers and researchers recognize scientific reasoning when students develop explanations together or move between speech, writing, diagrams, and other forms of expression. Each setting presents different evidence, and those differences affect whose contributions enter the scientific discussion.

González-Howard and colleagues (2023) examine how researchers' conceptions of language influence the scientific practices they study and the positions available to multilingual students. Grapin and colleagues (2023) distinguish equity as access to established disciplinary knowledge, practices, and language from equity as transformation of what schools recognize and value. They examine this distinction across science learning, assessment, and teacher education, and show why an approach may expand participation while leaving existing standards of acceptable expression largely untouched. Read alongside González-Howard and colleagues, this work leads me to consider how students' contributions enter the explanations a class develops and who has authority to evaluate those contributions.

The conditions of each activity affect how I would interpret a student's contribution. Ünsal and colleagues (2018) examine bilingual pupils' use of their languages in elementary science activities, including the demands that different forms of classroom interaction place on scientific meaning-making. Tang, Delgado, and Moje (2014) distinguish analyses of how students move between representations over time from analyses of how words, symbols, and visual elements work together within a representation. Their framework separates timescale from the level of detail being studied. For my proposed inquiry, these distinctions affect what I would compare. A student's diagram, the explanation they build with peers, and a subsequent written answer may differ partly because each activity makes different resources and relationships available.

I would like to examine what happens when an idea first appears in peer discussion or a visual representation and is later evaluated in an individual English-language response. I would trace which scientific relationships remain visible, which are reorganized or left implicit, and how feedback responds to what students have expressed. A comparison would need to document the actual task conditions and avoid assuming that a more elaborate contribution in one setting proves stable understanding across all settings.

This proposed classroom inquiry remains distinct from my completed scoping review, which analyzed the claims and evidence reported in published studies.

Biological sex and scientific classification

Histories of genetics, decisions about measurement, and the explanatory limits of a category.

Biological sex is studied through several features, including chromosomes, gonadal development, reproductive anatomy, endocrine processes, and reproductive function. These features are related, although they do not always vary together or answer the same scientific question. A category defined for one kind of investigation may become misleading when carried into another without examining its operational meaning. The research interest here concerns how scientists decide what to classify, how they justify that decision, and what follows when the resulting categories circulate beyond the original investigation.

Richardson (2013) traces how X and Y came to be called sex chromosomes, including early scientists’ concerns that the term itself might distort what these chromosomes were understood to do. She also examines claims about the XYY 'supermale' and debates over Y-chromosome degeneration. These cases show how gendered assumptions can enter scientific terminology, models, and explanations as research develops. They leave me asking what is actually being measured when a study reports a sex difference and how its chosen classification relates to the biological mechanism under investigation. Fausto-Sterling (2000) examines the scientific and medical histories through which bodies have been categorized, including the treatment of intersex variation. Her account invites scrutiny of the decisions that turn variation into a classification used in research and medicine.

Jordan-Young and Karkazis (2019) scrutinize popular and scientific claims that treat testosterone as an overarching explanation for differences in human behaviour. Their analysis encourages close attention to what was measured, in which populations and conditions, and how findings from a particular study came to support general claims about people. I value the physiological detail in endocrinology, and I want interpretations of testosterone research to remain answerable to what investigators measured and how the relevant processes work.

A possible document study could compare how secondary biology materials operationalize sex in chapters on inheritance, developmental biology, and reproduction. I would identify the biological feature being classified, the explanation for which it is used, and any move from that limited claim to a generalization about people. I would then compare how the materials handle variation and counterexamples. Such an analysis would need to preserve the scientific differences among the topics while examining the authority given to particular categories. I would need a clearly bounded collection of materials and a consistent way to compare the tasks, explanatory claims, and omissions before drawing conclusions.

In a discussion of biological sex or human variation, students may question a classification for different reasons. I would want to examine the scientific content of their questions and how others respond, while protecting their control over any personal information. A student's question should not create an expectation of disclosing identity or medical history.

Research interpretation and accountability

Situated knowledge, interpretation, and the position from which claims are made.

When I interpret classroom evidence, I need to account for the decisions involved in collecting and analyzing it. Where an observation begins, what a recording captures, and how I classify a contribution all affect the account I can offer. Those decisions also take place within relationships between the researcher, the institution, and the people participating.

In Situated Knowledges, Haraway (1988) questions the authority claimed by an observer who appears to see from nowhere. Her argument for partial, embodied perspectives still demands accountable knowledge about a shared material world. I take that challenge seriously when considering classroom research. A video recording preserves a selected view of an interaction, and the researcher's decisions about where to position the camera, how to transcribe speech, and which episodes to analyze shape the account available to readers.

That creates a methodological obligation. If I describe a student's question as disagreement, uncertainty, or resistance to a scientific category, I need evidence for that interpretation. Other participants may understand the moment differently, and a participant's later explanation may complicate what appeared evident in the recording. Returning to the material with participants could help examine those differences, although agreement would not by itself settle the analysis.

I would need to retain evidence that complicates an initial interpretation, document how I applied analytic categories, and give participants opportunities to question my account. The power imbalance between researcher and participant remains relevant, and these practices would make some of its consequences available for examination.

Tracing changes in classroom inquiry

I would want to follow a student's question beyond the moment it was raised. The subsequent discussion and any revisions to sources, diagrams, or investigations would help me examine whether the question influenced the class's scientific work. I would also need to know who proposed a change and who decided whether it was adequate. An encouraging teacher response alone would offer limited evidence of a change in curricular authority.

For a classroom-based inquiry, I would want to examine the original task, the interaction surrounding the question, any revisions to the material, and participants' accounts of those decisions. Returning to a moment with the people involved might reveal interpretations that my reading of the recording missed. I would also need to ask who could disagree with the researcher's account and what it meant for students to take part when the researcher or teacher held institutional authority.

I am still learning how to study these interactions responsibly. Calabrese Barton and Tan's account of rightful presence (2020) raises questions about whose participation can alter the practices of a learning community. In a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, Krishnamoorthy, Duncan, and Tan (2026) examine how students and adults designed a community survey and negotiated care for the people who might answer it. Students' concerns about possible harm became part of deciding what counted as responsible inquiry. That example helps specify the kind of decision I would need to follow across classroom interactions and revised materials. I am still learning how to develop the relationships and methods such a study would require.

Completed research

My scoping review examined how published researchers interpreted multilingual students' scientific work. In a separate classroom action research project, I followed linked AP Biology assessment records to examine mechanistic explanations and revise my correction practice. Each inquiry provides a different kind of evidence, and I have described their methods and limitations separately.

Scoping review · 2026 · 20 empirical studies

Multilingual science education scoping review

Across 20 ERIC-indexed empirical studies, I examined the scientific relationships researchers attributed to students’ writing, classroom interaction, and visual representations, and the evidence supporting those interpretations. The review also examined how studies reported the language and participation conditions under which students produced their work. It describes reporting practices within this bounded corpus; it cannot establish how students in my classroom would respond to different language arrangements.

Classroom action research · 2026 · 28 AP Biology students

Post-assessment reflection and mechanistic reasoning

With 28 AP Biology students, I compared explanations across three non-equivalent unit assessments and examined ten pairs of original explanations and later independent reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions extended earlier reasoning, while others omitted relationships previously articulated. The different task conditions limited what I could infer about students’ understanding or the effects of the correction routine.

Questions for further inquiry

The scoping review examined published interpretations of multilingual students’ work, without observing my own classroom. The action research followed actual classroom records but did not investigate how language practices or student questions influenced decisions about what counted as scientific knowledge. Read together, the projects sharpen my attention to the circumstances under which an explanation is produced and assessed. They leave the questions of curricular authority, identity-related classification, and consequential participation open for future inquiry. Those questions would require different evidence and research relationships.
