Justice-oriented science education

Research

I want to understand how young people can influence the scientific work of their classrooms, especially when their questions call an explanation, representation, or established practice into question.

When students notice something a scientific model leaves unresolved, question how a category is used, or raise concerns about the consequences of an investigation, their contributions may invite the group to reconsider how it proceeds. The teacher still has responsibilities for scientific accuracy and for the people involved. I am interested in how those responsibilities are negotiated, and in what gives students' contributions a meaningful role in the decisions that follow.

This is the focus that draws me toward justice-oriented science education. My classroom research on explanations and my review of multilingual science learning have helped me approach it. I hope to continue learning how young people and teachers might examine the conditions of their science learning and help develop curricula that respond to the questions they consider consequential.

When students' concerns change the scientific work

Science lessons ask students to work with evidence and develop explanations. They also involve decisions about which problems are worth pursuing, whose accounts are considered credible, and what responsibilities accompany an investigation. Teachers, curriculum materials, and disciplinary conventions often determine those decisions in advance. I want to study what happens when young people have an opportunity to question them and participate in determining how the work proceeds.

Calabrese Barton and Tan's account of rightful presence (2020) has helped me think about this question. It attends to the injustices through which schooling and disciplinary practices have positioned some young people as guests within spaces of knowledge-making. A related study by Calabrese Barton, Kim, and Tan (2020) examines collaborative work through which youth and educators challenged these arrangements in informal science learning.

A particularly instructive episode appears in Krishnamoorthy, Duncan, and Tan's study of a middle-school biology unit on stress (2026). During the design of a community survey, young people raised concerns about how taking part could affect the people being surveyed. The researchers examined how care for those people became part of the group's negotiations about scientific practice. The episode interests me because the students' concerns entered the work of deciding what would count as a responsible way to conduct the inquiry.

The question I keep returning to

When young people identify a concern in a scientific representation or practice, under what conditions can it influence how the group decides to proceed?

Answering this would require close attention to the interaction, the scientific task, the relationships involved, and what changed afterward.

What was questioned?

A claim, a diagram, an investigative method, or an assumption about whose knowledge belongs in the work.

Who had influence?

How students, teachers, and other participants negotiated the concern, including the authority each person could exercise.

What changed?

Whether the group revised an explanation, altered a procedure, reconsidered a representation, or pursued a different question.

How I arrived at these questions

My route into this work has been through teaching Advanced Placement Biology and Chemistry in English-medium classrooms in South Korea. I have seen how scientific ideas develop through speech, equations, diagrams, and movement between languages. The same student can express different parts of an explanation under different conditions. Reading students' work has made me attentive to what a teacher can recognize and to the limits of an interpretation made from one response.

In my classroom action research, I followed students' explanations after assessment feedback. Comparing original answers, reflections, preparation reports, and subsequent reconstructions showed that a later response could develop a causal relationship, repair a contradiction, or leave out something the student had articulated before. My scoping review extended this inquiry into published research on multilingual learners. It examined the different forms of work researchers use as evidence and the conditions that make their interpretations more or less accountable to what students actually expressed.

Those inquiries have helped me ask more carefully whose reasoning becomes recognizable in a classroom. Teaching topics involving biological sex and human variation brought another concern into view. Students may question the limits of a familiar classification, notice variation that a diagram does not explain, or ask how a scientific term is being used. Their questions can prompt further biological investigation while also carrying social and institutional significance. The consequences of how teachers respond extend beyond the completeness of an assessment answer.

That movement from interpretation toward participation and curricular authority has drawn me to justice-oriented science education. I remain an emerging researcher. My existing studies provide methodological preparation and evidence about particular problems. They leave open the larger question of how students' contributions can influence what a classroom comes to regard as worthwhile, rigorous, and responsible scientific work.

Questions I hope to investigate

The concerns below belong to a shared inquiry about justice, science, and curricular authority. The specific settings and questions would need to be developed with the people involved.

Youth participation and curricular authority

Whose questions influence what the class comes to know?

I am interested in classroom moments when students question an accepted explanation, the ethics of a scientific procedure, or an assumption embedded in an activity. How does the teacher respond? Which ideas enter the subsequent investigation? When a concern prompts a change, who has the opportunity to shape that change and judge whether it addresses the problem?

There is a difference between inviting contributions and sharing authority over the direction of scientific work. I would want to examine that distinction carefully through actual episodes and participants' accounts. The scholarship on rightful presence and youth participation offers ways of asking how classroom relationships, disciplinary authority, and wider social conditions shape these opportunities (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020; Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Biological categories and scientific representations

What happens when a model leaves something important unresolved?

Biological sex and human variation offer one meaningful context. Categories can help students understand patterns, although any particular diagram or classification has a history, a purpose, and a limited range of application. Questions about chromosomes, development, reproduction, and variation can make those limits visible. I want to know what happens when young people examine them with teachers and whether their questions can influence how the biology is represented and taught.

Queer and trans scholarship has helped me consider the scientific and human consequences of essentialist explanations. Rende Mendoza and Johnson (2024) examine gender-inclusive teaching through the experiences of transgender science teachers; Zemenick and colleagues (2022) discuss ways biology teaching can represent variation accurately and respectfully. This is one possible setting for the inquiry. Environmental relationships, colonial histories of science, and questions that matter to particular communities may lead to other curricular contexts.

Knowledge, language, and the histories of science

Which forms of knowledge are treated as authoritative?

Students bring different linguistic, cultural, and disciplinary resources to scientific problems. My earlier work has led me to look at what changes when an explanation moves between discussion, writing, diagrams, and models. I want to connect that attentiveness to broader questions about the forms of knowledge curriculum makes visible and the conditions under which contributions can become consequential for a group.

Bang, Warren, Rosebery, and Medin (2012) examine how settled expectations in science education can restrict what is valued as scientific knowledge and how young people are positioned as knowers. Decolonial scholarship also raises questions about the histories through which some accounts of land, living systems, and communities came to be treated as authoritative. These concerns ask something of the research process itself, including who defines the problem and whose interpretation has weight.

Learning to inquire with young people and teachers

I am drawn to community-engaged inquiry and collaborative curriculum development because they create possibilities for participants to help determine which problems are worth examining and how a response might be developed. Collaborative work would still require attention to the relationships through which decisions are made. Who can disagree with the researcher's framing? Whose priorities guide the curriculum? What happens when participants wish to take the inquiry in a direction the original proposal did not anticipate?

I hope to develop the theoretical and methodological preparation needed to approach these questions responsibly. Interaction analysis could help me attend to how an idea enters a discussion and what happens to it afterward. Returning to selected moments with participants may help me examine differences between my interpretation and their own. Critical, sociocultural, decolonial, and queer scholarship would inform how I approach knowledge, power, identity, and the responsibilities of inquiry.

These are directions for future study. I have experience with classroom action research and literature synthesis, and I would need further preparation, ethical approval, and genuine partnerships before undertaking collaborative or community-engaged research of this kind.

Responsibilities I want to keep visible

Teaching and research involve unequal authority. Students' willingness to ask a question does not establish consent to have their experiences interpreted or shared. A question about biological sex, belonging, or a community concern gives me no grounds to presume anyone's identity or reasons for asking.

I want research decisions to account for privacy, participants' control over personal disclosure, the consequences of using classroom materials, and the right to challenge the researcher's interpretation. The scientific work also deserves care, with evidence and uncertainty represented faithfully.
