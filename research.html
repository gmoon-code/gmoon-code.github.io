Justice-oriented science education

Research

In my research, I have examined what teachers and researchers can reasonably conclude from students' scientific work. A scoping review led me to consider how studies interpret multilingual students' explanations across writing, classroom interaction, and visual representations. In a classroom action research inquiry, I followed students' accounts of biological mechanisms across assessments and a subsequent reconstruction task.

These inquiries have also led me to consider what happens when students question a model, challenge a classification, or raise a concern about the evidence a class is using. I am interested in how their contributions influence the scientific work that follows and how teachers and students make those decisions together. My current questions bring classroom inquiry into conversation with scholarship on curriculum, justice-oriented science education, and the histories of biological knowledge.

Classroom action research · 2026 · 28 AP Biology students

Post-assessment reflection and mechanistic reasoning

In a class of 28 AP Biology students, I followed explanations across three unit assessments and examined ten pairs of original responses and later independent reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions extended or repaired earlier causal pathways, while others omitted relationships that had appeared before. The differences helped me identify what I still needed to ask students about their reasoning.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Language use, representation, and the interpretation of scientific explanations.

In English-medium science classrooms, students sometimes develop explanations through conversation, gesture, diagrams, numerical relationships, and writing. Each form allows different parts of an idea to become visible. I am interested in how teachers interpret these contributions, particularly when reasoning develops across several forms and an individual written response captures only part of the earlier discussion.

A teacher's interpretation can affect whether a contribution enters the class's developing explanation. Assumptions about language and participation influence what researchers and educators recognize as scientific sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). Debates about equity in multilingual science education also distinguish access to established expectations from opportunities to influence what is valued in science learning (Grapin et al., 2023). I want to examine how those questions arise in the ordinary work of discussing a diagram, responding to an explanation, or deciding what belongs in a shared account.

Tracing contributions across these settings would require attention to what each activity asks students to do. Bilingual science classroom research examines how forms of interaction affect opportunities to develop meaning (Ünsal et al., 2018), while multimodal analysis distinguishes the development of an idea over time from the relationships among words, images, and symbols in a single contribution (Tang et al., 2014). Together, these distinctions could guide a comparison of peer discussion, annotated drawings, and later written explanations while keeping their different conditions visible.

For example, I would like to follow an idea that emerges in peer discussion or an annotated model and is subsequently assessed through an individual English-language explanation. The comparison could show which scientific relationships were retained, revised, or left implicit, alongside the feedback students received. I would need to document each task's conditions before interpreting those differences, since greater detail in one setting cannot by itself establish understanding across every other setting.

My scoping review examined how published researchers supported interpretations of multilingual students' scientific work. Following contributions across activities in my own classroom would be a separate inquiry, requiring records of the interactions and the decisions made in response.

Curriculum decisions and student participation

A teacher's choice of phenomenon, source, or model establishes where a class investigation begins. That direction can change when a student proposes another explanation or questions what the example leaves out. I am interested in the decisions that follow, especially whether the student's contribution influences which evidence is sought and which scientific questions the class continues to pursue.

These decisions are connected to the histories and forms of knowledge a curriculum recognizes. Expectations about nature and culture can restrict how students from historically nondominant communities participate in science (Bang et al., 2012). The concept of rightful presence further asks whose contributions can influence the practices and authority of a learning community (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). I would want to trace what happens after a concern is raised, including who can question a proposed response and whose account of the situation is taken seriously. Developing this work as a decolonial inquiry would also require learning about the particular histories and relationships of the communities involved.

A possible classroom decision

If a student questioned why a model treated two habitats as equivalent, the class could examine what ecological relationships it leaves out and whether another source of evidence is needed.

The question is who helps decide what the class examines next.

Cultural knowledge and biological observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognizes.

Students examining the same biological setting may notice different relationships. An activity organized around taxonomic similarities, for example, directs attention differently from one concerned with ecological interactions. To understand a student's explanation, I would need to examine the question they were addressing and what the task had encouraged them to observe.

What a student recognizes as relevant in a biological setting may also reflect experiences beyond school. Cultural models of nature can shape which observations and relationships are recognized as scientific evidence (Bang et al., 2007), and learning develops through the activities, tools, and relationships people share over time (Nasir et al., 2014). These concerns lead me to ask how students have learned to notice particular ecological relationships before interpreting differences in their explanations as differences in capability.

One possible study would follow the ecological relationships students identify in school and other settings, examining how they connect those observations with disciplinary models. It would be necessary to document where an idea originated, how it was used, and what happened when others responded. I would also need to examine the labels used in the analysis, including what a researcher is treating as “everyday,” “cultural,” or “scientific” knowledge.

Biological sex and scientific classification

Histories of genetics, decisions about measurement, and the explanatory limits of a category.

Biological sex is examined through related features such as chromosomes, gonadal development, reproductive anatomy, endocrine processes, and reproductive function. These features do not always vary together, and different investigations may have reasons to measure different aspects of them. I am interested in how scientists justify a classification for a particular question and what happens when a category developed for that purpose is treated as though it provides a broader explanation of people's bodies.

The history of sex classification makes those questions especially worth examining. Debates over the designation of X and Y as sex chromosomes, and later interpretations of XYY and Y-chromosome degeneration, show how assumptions about gender have entered scientific explanations (Richardson, 2013). Histories of medicine and intersex variation also document the consequences of decisions about bodily categories (Fausto-Sterling, 2000). Reading these accounts has led me to ask what biological features a contemporary study actually measures when it reports a sex difference and how far its evidence can support that claim.

Questions about interpretation also arise when a measured physiological difference is used to explain human behaviour. Claims about testosterone, for example, need to be examined in relation to the populations, measurements, and circumstances on which they rest (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). I enjoy the detail of endocrinology and want that detail to remain present in the explanation. Findings from a particular experiment may illuminate a biological process while leaving broader claims about people unresolved.

One possible study would compare how secondary biology materials define sex in lessons on inheritance, development, and reproduction. I would examine which features a category refers to, how the classification relates to the mechanism being taught, and whether the evidence supports any wider claims made about people. Using a bounded collection of materials and consistent criteria could help preserve differences among these biological topics while making their treatment of variation open to examination.

The classroom discussion presents another question. Students may challenge a classification, ask about a counterexample, or question the consequences of using a category. I would want to examine the biology in those questions and how teachers and classmates respond, while keeping students in control of what they disclose. A contribution to scientific discussion should never require a student to explain their identity or medical history.

Tracing changes in classroom inquiry

To examine whether a student's question influenced a classroom investigation, I would follow the discussion beyond the moment when it was raised. Changes to sources, diagrams, questions, or tasks could show how the class responded. I would need to know who proposed a change, how it was evaluated, and whether students were able to affect the eventual decision. A teacher's encouraging reply, on its own, would leave much of that history unknown.

That inquiry could draw on the original task, recordings of the interaction, subsequent materials, and participants' accounts of the decisions involved. Comparing those records would help me see where an idea entered the work and what became of it. It would also allow me to examine disagreements about what occurred, including situations in which institutional authority shaped whose interpretation prevailed.

The question of influence brings me back to rightful presence and the relationship between participation and authority (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). In a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students and adults negotiated the design of a community survey and considered the possible harm questions might cause respondents (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026). That account offers a concrete example of participants revising scientific work together. In my own setting, investigating comparable decisions would require sustained relationships and records that could show what each participant contributed and what followed.

Interpreting classroom evidence responsibly

Recording choices, participants' accounts, and the position from which a claim is made.

Following a classroom decision over time also requires care with what the record makes visible. A camera, a transcript, and a coding scheme preserve different parts of an interaction. How I select an episode or identify a contribution would therefore need to remain open to examination, particularly when participants understood the exchange differently.

Those decisions are also shaped by the researcher's position. Situated accounts of knowledge call for attention to how a claim is produced and to the limits of the perspective from which it is made (Haraway, 1988). In video-based inquiry, I would document camera placement and transcription choices so that readers could examine how the record was constructed.

If I described a student's question as disagreement, uncertainty, or resistance to a category, I would need to identify what in the interaction supported that reading. Returning to the recording with the student and other participants might lead me to reconsider the account. Their perspectives would form part of the analysis, although agreement alone would not establish that one interpretation was correct.

I would also keep records that complicate my initial interpretation and document how analytic categories were applied. Participants should have opportunities to question my account, especially given the authority a teacher or researcher holds within a school. Such opportunities cannot remove that imbalance, but they can make its effects more open to scrutiny.
