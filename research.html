Research

A food web traces energy from one organism to another, although its arrows say little about how much energy is transferred. If a student asks where the rest goes, the teacher might clarify the diagram’s purpose or investigate the energy transfers it leaves unexplained. How that question is taken up can influence the explanation the class develops.

This is one way I have come to think about justice in science education. Curriculum materials help teachers organize scientific knowledge, while classroom decisions shape which questions become part of an inquiry and whose explanations receive sustained attention. I am especially interested in how students participate in evaluating the representations they encounter and in the judgments that establish what counts as a credible scientific contribution. Scholarship on epistemic agency and rightful presence helps me examine these questions without separating disciplinary reasoning from the relationships through which it is recognized.

Multilingual science education scoping review

I examined 20 empirical studies retrieved through ERIC to consider how researchers inferred multilingual students’ science understanding from written responses, classroom interaction, and visual work. I also examined the reporting of language options and participation conditions, since these can affect what a student’s contribution allows a researcher to conclude.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I studied students’ explanations across assessments and two adjustments to the feedback routine. Ten originally below-complete Unit 3 explanations could be compared with later, closed-resource reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions developed fuller causal pathways, while others omitted relationships previously expressed. Comparing these records changed the questions I would ask in subsequent assessment, although those follow-up questions were not administered in the reported inquiry.

A later response can leave important parts of an earlier explanation unseen. In a study included in the scoping review, Luis’s shorter English translation made an ecological distinction less explicit than his bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20’s reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships they had previously explained. Different tasks and language conditions shaped these records, so the cases cannot be treated as equivalent. Each nonetheless complicates an interpretation based on the most recent answer alone.

The comparison also left me with questions that my completed projects did not address. I could identify different ways to assess students’ reasoning, yet I had not examined how students themselves might influence the judgments or activities that followed. That interest connects the empirical work with the scholarship on epistemic agency discussed below.

Research interests

The studies I return to approach classroom participation from different positions. Some examine who can influence the questions and practices through which a class develops knowledge. Others show how a student’s contribution becomes more or less visible through language, tasks, and the judgments of others. Reading across these settings has helped me consider how epistemic authority operates in ordinary classroom decisions and what a more just science education might require.

Classroom authority and epistemic agency

How instructional decisions shape students' opportunities to pursue scientific questions.

During a lesson on cell signalling, a student might ask what happens when a receptor remains active after the signal has disappeared. The illustration before the class may make activation clear while leaving regulation beyond its scope. The teacher then has to decide whether the question reveals a misunderstanding, calls for a further explanation, or warrants an investigation. That judgment shapes what the class can examine next and how far the student's concern enters its scientific work.

Curricular planning can make room for students to influence the methods of investigation and the models through which a class develops an explanation (Ko & Krist, 2019). Those opportunities are also negotiated during instruction. Studies of teachers' noticing and classroom discourse show how a student's contribution can become a question for collective inquiry or be treated primarily as an answer to evaluate (Krist et al., 2023; Stroupe, 2014). These distinctions have made me more attentive to what happens after a teacher acknowledges an idea.

Supporting student-directed inquiry involves decisions about disciplinary guidance. A discussion of STEM curriculum considers inquiry and project-based approaches among the pedagogies teachers can use to develop meaningful problems (DeCoito, 2024). The work students undertake may then lead beyond the anticipated sequence. In a Grade 6 classroom investigating flight, physical materials complicated a knowledge-building design centred on discussion and writing (Reeve, 2023). Web-based science inquiry environments offer another perspective on how the resources prepared for a lesson can structure the explanations students develop (Slotta & Linn, 2009). These accounts prompt questions about how teachers respond to emerging ideas while sustaining attention to the science.

That responsibility is exercised within institutional and relational conditions. A longitudinal study followed an early-career teacher negotiating an activist commitment alongside the demands of school practice (Barrett, 2013). In a different setting, students and mentors participating in a robotics competition negotiated authority as they developed scientific and technical knowledge together (Puvirajah et al., 2012). Productive disciplinary engagement provides a further account of how student authority can develop alongside responsibility to peers and access to disciplinary resources (Engle & Conant, 2002). The differences across these settings make the conditions for participation worth examining in their own right.

The justice-oriented concern extends to the criteria through which contributions become legitimate. Epistemic agency involves more than performing prescribed scientific practices (Miller et al., 2018). Rightful presence draws attention to how participants might challenge the norms and power relations that determine whose contributions receive consideration (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). A teacher may have strong scientific grounds for correcting an explanation. I am also interested in whether students can examine those grounds, ask for further evidence, and influence the direction of the discussion.

A community survey designed during a seventh-grade biology unit on stress illustrates how scientific and ethical judgments can develop together. Students questioned the effects their questions might have on respondents, and adults and youth negotiated care for those respondents as part of deciding what would count as a responsible inquiry (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Here, students' concern became part of what the group needed to consider in evaluating its research practice.

Recognizing scientific reasoning

How classroom expectations shape which contributions become visible and consequential.

Cultural knowledge and participation

How the form of a task influences the knowledge people make visible.

People may draw on similar knowledge while organizing their explanations differently. In research with Menominee and European American fish experts, Menominee participants more often mentioned habitat when explaining their own groupings of local species. Once explicitly asked to sort fish by habitat, both groups showed strong agreement (Bang et al., 2007). The difference concerned the prominence of ecological relationships within the task, and it cautions against treating an unmentioned relationship as necessarily unknown. Later scholarship connects such differences to cultural practices and to the assumptions about nature that science education brings into classrooms (Bang & Medin, 2010; Bang et al., 2012).

These findings belong to particular communities, with histories that matter to their interpretation. Other research illustrates different ways that experience enters scientific learning. Interviews with Muslim science teachers in Canada and Pakistan explored how professional and religious commitments informed their understanding and teaching of evolution (Asghar, 2013). A study involving two groups of physics learners examined how feelings about radioactivity related to their approaches to the topic (Alsop & Watts, 2000). Their findings cannot be applied directly to my students, but they help me think about what a single assessment response may leave unexplored.

Whether an activity is recognized as science also depends on its setting. Children attending community science clubs in Canada encountered hands-on work and supportive relationships, although participants sometimes reinforced a division between club activities and school science (Burke & Navas Iannini, 2021). The contrast raises questions about what students come to expect from the scientific work available to them in different educational spaces.

Recognition also concerns who is seen as capable of doing that work. Doctoral physics students described how disciplinary competence was entangled with gendered expectations (Gonsalves, 2014). A longitudinal study of women of colour entering scientific careers likewise examined how recognition informed their developing science identities (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Their different experiences give substance to the broader view of learning as a cultural process shaped through relationships and participation (Nasir et al., 2014).

The researcher's interpretation brings its own assumptions into view. Labelling one contribution as everyday knowledge and another as scientific reasoning may overlook how a student connected experience with an emerging explanation. What can be known also depends on which interactions are recorded, how episodes are selected, and whose account informs the interpretation. Guidance for video research examines these practical and ethical decisions (Derry et al., 2010), while the concept of situated knowledge makes the partial perspective of any account an explicit concern (Haraway, 1988). These readings have made me more careful about the distance between a student's contribution and the conclusion I draw from it.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

What becomes visible as ideas move through talk, writing, and shared representations.

In an English-medium science classroom, an explanation can develop across languages and forms of expression. A student might describe water movement in Korean while working with a classmate, then record a shorter account in English. The written response alone would provide limited evidence of the relationships they had already discussed. The difficulty is familiar from my work with students' explanations, and it has encouraged me to read multilingual science research with attention to how an idea develops across successive interactions.

Researchers' understandings of language influence what they look for when studying scientific sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). Within science education, the distinction between providing access to established practices and transforming whose resources are valued raises further questions about equity (Grapin et al., 2023). Students can also influence an inquiry through critique of their classmates' reasoning. Analyses of discussion in three middle-school classrooms describe different ways that such critique became an expression of epistemic agency (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020). These studies help me consider what happens to a contribution once other participants respond to it.

Classroom language expectations create additional conditions for participation. In a Malaysian Grade 5 classroom with an English-only policy, learners used translanguaging in peer collaboration while encountering restrictions from classroom and wider social expectations (Rajendram, 2023). A Canadian university study compared plurilingual and English-only instruction in an academic English programme, finding differences in plurilingual and pluricultural competence (Galante, 2022). The populations and outcomes differ from secondary science learning. Both studies nonetheless show why the language options available during a task need to be understood before interpreting what learners express.

Some scientific ideas become available through gestures or jointly developed models before they appear in a conventional written answer. Studies of bilingual science talk and multimodal representation offer approaches to examining these relationships (Ünsal et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2014). The value of following these processes is visible in an out-of-school electrical-circuits inquiry, where emergent bilingual children drew on several communicative resources to construct models together (Suárez, 2020).

Work beyond science classrooms extends these interpretive questions. Students' creation of plurilingual identity texts has been examined through conversations accompanying their written and visual work (Prasad, 2015). A relational account of multiliteracies similarly asks educators to consider how texts acquire meaning within a community (Simon, 2011). Both perspectives help me think about the interactions through which a student's expression comes to be understood by others.

The relationship between language and participation also extends beyond individual tasks. A longitudinal study of international students considered academic acculturation alongside English proficiency when examining university experiences (Neumann et al., 2023). In an academic English classroom, spoken-word-poetry activities exposed emotional tensions as participants engaged with antiracist questions (Burton, 2024). These studies concern different educational settings, yet both make visible conditions that a language score or completed text could leave unexplored. I am interested in how comparable questions of recognition and response arise within the distinctive disciplinary work of secondary biology.

Scientific representations and their limits

The explanatory purposes of models, and the questions that may lead students to reconsider them.

Evaluating and revising scientific models

When the assumptions of a model become part of the investigation.

A population-growth model can describe increasing numbers under particular assumptions about resources and reproduction. Its predictions become less useful when those assumptions no longer hold. Examining the model therefore involves questions about the evidence it represents, the processes it leaves aside, and the conditions under which a different explanation is needed. Classroom inquiry can make those judgments open to discussion, including the standards students use to decide whether a model is adequate.

Students have sometimes participated directly in establishing such standards. In one fifth-grade inquiry, they revised a shared list of criteria for scientific models following peer critique and classroom discussion (Murphy et al., 2023). Seventh-grade groups in a different study reviewed and revised models using criteria already supplied to them, drawing on speech, writing, and visual representations (Cheung & Yang, 2026). Their different responsibilities clarify an important distinction between applying evaluative criteria and helping decide what those criteria should be.

Learning progressions for scientific modelling also distinguish constructing a representation from understanding how it is used and revised (Schwarz et al., 2009). Research on epistemologies in practice and scientific argumentation places these activities within the problems participants are trying to resolve (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015). I find this connection useful when considering what students can contribute beyond producing a scientifically correct drawing. How their suggestions shape an inquiry remains an empirical question, particularly when a representation is already presented as part of an established curriculum.

Sex determination and biological variation

How scientific categories acquire explanatory and educational authority.

Sex determination offers a more specific context for thinking about the scope of a biological representation. A simplified pathway may connect chromosomal and genetic signals with gonadal differentiation, while leaving other developmental routes unexplained. Clinical reviews describe how variation in genetic regulation and endocrine processes can produce outcomes beyond the patterns commonly introduced in textbooks (Arboleda et al., 2014; Witchel, 2018). These are sources on developmental biology and clinical practice. Their relevance to teaching lies in helping distinguish what a given model can explain from what cannot be inferred from it.

There are also responsibilities in how this content is taught. Recommendations for inclusive postsecondary biology education encourage early attention to biological diversity, historical perspectives on scientific knowledge, and a classroom culture of respect (Zemenick et al., 2022). Those recommendations have not established the effects of a particular secondary school lesson. They do offer a useful basis for considering how variation can be investigated as an ordinary biological subject without treating students' bodies or identities as examples to be disclosed.

Historical research demonstrates why the uses of a category deserve scrutiny alongside its biological basis. Earlier attempts to link an XYY chromosome pattern with aggression drew on cultural assumptions about masculinity, an episode examined in the history of sex-chromosome research (Richardson, 2013). A later systematic review found no statistical evidence that the chromosomal pattern alone predisposes someone to aggressive or deviant behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). The two sources contribute different kinds of evidence, one concerning the history of a claim and the other its empirical support.

Medical classifications of intersex variation have another history, including decisions about diagnosis, treatment, and who has authority to define a condition (Fausto-Sterling, 2000; Davis, 2015). A related issue emerges when claims about testosterone extend beyond the circumstances in which a hormone was measured (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). Debates over eligibility rules for elite athletes show how such measurements can acquire institutional significance (Karkazis et al., 2012). These examples concern different biological processes and social settings. Together, they have made me more attentive to the movement from a scientific measurement to a broader judgment about a person.

The conditions under which these questions can be raised also deserve attention. An ethic of hospitality toward gender diversity asks educators to make room for students without first presuming knowledge of their identities (Airton, 2023). Scholarship on the politics of sex education examines how adult claims to expertise can obscure students' own engagement with a curriculum (Gilbert, 2021). Both prompt me to consider how students can question an explanation without being asked to account for their personal circumstances.

Wider scholarship raises further questions about educational authority. Critical writing on trans-exclusionary politics examines practices of exclusion (Alexander, 2023), while participatory research on queer joy explores what collective educational work can make possible (Burkholder et al., 2025). These works offer political and participatory perspectives; neither establishes the effectiveness of teaching a particular biology model. Their relevance here lies in how they illuminate the relationships through which a classroom makes space for participation and disagreement.

Scientific adequacy and classroom justice meet when a student's question leads others to examine the evidence for an explanation. A teacher may reasonably correct a claim about developmental biology, while the way that correction is handled still influences whose questions can be pursued. I am interested in the judgments involved in both parts of that exchange and in the possibilities for students to contribute to them.

Related writing

These questions about the reach of scientific explanations also shape my essay on biological classification. There, I consider how findings about haemoglobin variation and sex chromosomes have been interpreted beyond what the evidence can establish, and what those histories might mean for examining classifications in biology education.
