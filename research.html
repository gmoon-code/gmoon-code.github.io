Justice-oriented science education

Research

Teaching biology and chemistry has made me more attentive to the choices involved in a science lesson. The questions I prepare, the examples I use, and the responses I assess each affect what I can learn about students' scientific thinking.

Students bring experiences and questions that may challenge what I have prepared. I want to understand how teachers respond to those contributions and how students can participate in decisions about the scientific work of a class. My interests in justice-oriented science education include the racialized, gendered, and linguistic conditions of participation. Attending to those conditions also requires careful attention to the biology and chemistry being taught.

Curriculum studies, multilingual science education, and histories of scientific classification have given me different ways to examine these questions. I am especially interested in where an interpretation of student reasoning becomes more certain than the available evidence permits. My completed classroom inquiry and scoping review are described later on this page.

Curriculum decisions and student participation

Selecting a phenomenon, source, or model gives an investigation its initial direction. When students question an example or propose another explanation, teachers must decide how to respond and what evidence the class should consider. I am interested in how students can participate in those decisions and whether their contributions affect the subsequent scientific work.

Questions about students' influence over curriculum also require attention to whose knowledge a lesson treats as relevant. Expectations about nature and culture can restrict the participation of students from historically nondominant communities (Bang et al., 2012). The idea of rightful presence extends this concern to the authority students have within a learning community, including opportunities to influence its practices (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). Together, these arguments encourage me to examine decisions made after a student raises a concern. I would need to understand the particular histories and relationships of the communities involved before developing such work as a decolonial inquiry.

A question for curriculum work

Can a student's concern lead to a change in the evidence collected, the explanation developed, or the question the class is pursuing?

I would want to know what happened after the concern was raised, and who had a say in what followed.

Cultural knowledge and biological observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognizes.

Students can attend to different relationships among organisms when they work with the same biological setting. A task concerned with taxonomic similarity may direct their observations differently from one examining ecological interactions. Before interpreting a difference in their explanations, I would want to understand what each student was trying to account for and what experience they brought to the task.

Differences in what students notice need to be considered alongside the experiences through which they have learned to observe. Research on cultural models of nature questions the apparent neutrality of the classifications used to interpret scientific reasoning (Bang et al., 2007). Cultural practices also develop through activities, tools, and relationships shared over time (Nasir et al., 2014). Together, these accounts encourage me to examine how students came to recognize particular biological relationships before interpreting differences between their explanations as differences in capability.

One possible inquiry would examine whether school investigations make room for ecological relationships that students have learned to notice in other settings. Following the organisms, processes, and connections they identify across local knowledge and disciplinary models could help establish what each contribution adds. The analysis would also need to document where those ideas came from and how they were used, while treating labels such as “everyday,” “cultural,” and “scientific” as categories that themselves require examination.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Language use, representation, and the interpretation of scientific explanations.

In English-medium science classrooms, students may use a wider range of linguistic and representational resources than an assessment makes available. Their explanations can develop through conversation, gesture, diagrams, numerical relationships, and writing. I want to understand how teachers and researchers recognize scientific reasoning when students develop explanations together or move between speech, writing, diagrams, and other forms of expression. Each setting presents different evidence, and those differences affect whose contributions enter the scientific discussion.

These differences between classroom expression and assessment also raise questions about who can contribute to the scientific account. Researchers' conceptions of language influence which practices and forms of participation they recognize (González-Howard et al., 2023). An associated distinction concerns whether equity provides access to established disciplinary expectations or enables students to question and revise what schools value (Grapin et al., 2023). Additional opportunities to express an idea may expand participation, yet the authority to assess and develop that idea still needs examination.

To investigate that question, I would need to consider how each classroom activity makes reasoning available. Research with bilingual pupils has examined the different demands that forms of interaction place on language use in science (Ünsal et al., 2018). Analyses of representations also distinguish changes across time from relationships among words, symbols, and images within a single contribution (Tang et al., 2014). These distinctions would guide comparisons of peer discussion, drawings, and later written explanations, particularly when a causal relationship becomes explicit in one setting and remains unstated in another.

I would like to examine what happens when an idea first appears in peer discussion or a visual representation and is later evaluated in an individual English-language response. I would trace which scientific relationships remain visible, which are reorganized or left implicit, and how feedback responds to what students have expressed. A comparison would need to document the actual task conditions and avoid assuming that a more elaborate contribution in one setting proves stable understanding across all settings.

My completed scoping review examined how published studies supported their interpretations of student work. Investigating these classroom interactions would require a separate study.

Biological sex and scientific classification

Histories of genetics, decisions about measurement, and the explanatory limits of a category.

Biological sex is studied through several features, including chromosomes, gonadal development, reproductive anatomy, endocrine processes, and reproductive function. These features are related, although they do not always vary together or answer the same scientific question. A category defined for one kind of investigation may become misleading when carried into another without examining its operational meaning. The research interest here concerns how scientists decide what to classify, how they justify that decision, and what follows when the resulting categories circulate beyond the original investigation.

The history of biological sex classification offers examples of how terminology and explanatory claims develop together. Early debates over the designation of X and Y as sex chromosomes, along with later interpretations of XYY and Y-chromosome degeneration, reveal how assumptions about gender can enter scientific accounts (Richardson, 2013). Medical and scientific approaches to intersex variation also demonstrate the consequences of decisions about how bodies are categorized (Fausto-Sterling, 2000). These histories make me attentive to what a contemporary study measures when it reports a sex difference and whether its classifications are appropriate to the biological process under investigation.

Claims that attribute broad differences in human behaviour to testosterone deserve careful examination of the measurements, populations, and conditions on which they rest (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). I value the physiological detail involved in endocrinology, which is why I want explanations to remain closely connected to the processes a study actually examines. A result from one setting may have a narrower interpretation than the general claim subsequently made about people.

A document study of secondary biology materials could compare how sex is operationalized in topics such as inheritance, development, and reproduction. I would record which biological features are classified, how the categories relate to the mechanisms under discussion, and where limited findings become general claims about people. A bounded collection of materials and consistent criteria would allow comparison of the treatment of variation and counterexamples while preserving the scientific differences among these topics.

In a discussion of biological sex or human variation, students may question a classification for different reasons. I would want to examine the scientific content of their questions and how others respond, while protecting their control over any personal information. A student's question should not create an expectation of disclosing identity or medical history.

Research interpretation and accountability

Situated knowledge, interpretation, and the position from which claims are made.

When I interpret classroom evidence, I need to account for the decisions involved in collecting and analyzing it. Where an observation begins, what a recording captures, and how I classify a contribution all affect the account I can offer. Those decisions also take place within relationships between the researcher, the institution, and the people participating.

These choices are connected to the researcher's position and the responsibility to make knowledge claims open to scrutiny (Haraway, 1988). In classroom research, even a video recording preserves a selected view of an interaction. Documenting camera placement, transcription decisions, and the episodes chosen for analysis would therefore be necessary, especially where a participant might understand an exchange differently from my interpretation.

If I interpret a student's question as disagreement, uncertainty, or resistance to a scientific category, I would need to show what in the interaction supports that reading. The student and other participants may understand the moment differently. Reviewing the recording with them could complicate my initial account, although agreement would not settle the analysis on its own.

I would need to retain evidence that complicates an initial interpretation, document how I applied analytic categories, and give participants opportunities to question my account. The power imbalance between researcher and participant remains relevant, and these practices would make some of its consequences available for examination.

Tracing changes in classroom inquiry

To examine whether a student's question influenced an investigation, I would follow the subsequent discussion and any changes to sources, diagrams, or tasks. The record would need to show who proposed a change and who evaluated it. An encouraging response from the teacher might be meaningful, although it would offer limited evidence of a change in curricular authority.

A classroom inquiry could bring together the original task, records of the interaction, revised materials, and participants' accounts of the decisions involved. Returning to a recorded moment with participants might complicate my initial interpretation. The analysis would also need to consider who could disagree with my account when the teacher or researcher held institutional authority.

For students' participation to influence scientific inquiry, their concerns must have some bearing on the decisions that follow. Rightful presence offers a way to examine that relationship between participation and authority (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). In a seventh-grade biology unit on stress, students and adults negotiated the design of a community survey, including concerns about possible harm to respondents (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026). Their decisions provide a concrete example of the interactions and revisions I would want to examine. Research of this kind in my own setting would require sustained relationships with participants and careful documentation of how their contributions influenced the work.

Completed research

My scoping review examined how published researchers interpreted multilingual students' scientific work. In a separate classroom action research project, I followed linked AP Biology assessment records to examine mechanistic explanations and revise my correction practice. Each inquiry provides a different kind of evidence, and I have described their methods and limitations separately.

Scoping review · 2026 · 20 empirical studies

Multilingual science education scoping review

Across 20 ERIC-indexed empirical studies, I examined the scientific relationships researchers attributed to students’ writing, classroom interaction, and visual representations, and the evidence supporting those interpretations. The review also examined how studies reported the language and participation conditions under which students produced their work. It describes reporting practices within this bounded corpus; it cannot establish how students in my classroom would respond to different language arrangements.

Classroom action research · 2026 · 28 AP Biology students

Post-assessment reflection and mechanistic reasoning

With 28 AP Biology students, I compared explanations across three non-equivalent unit assessments and examined ten pairs of original explanations and later independent reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism. Some reconstructions extended earlier reasoning, while others omitted relationships previously articulated. The different task conditions limited what I could infer about students’ understanding or the effects of the correction routine.

Questions for further inquiry

The scoping review examined how published researchers interpreted multilingual students' scientific work. My classroom inquiry followed students' explanations across different assessment conditions in AP Biology. Those records leave open how students' questions affect curriculum decisions and how participants respond when scientific classifications are questioned. Investigating these issues would require records of classroom interactions, participants' accounts, and evidence of the decisions that followed.
