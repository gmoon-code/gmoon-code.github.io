Justice-oriented science education

Research

A science lesson is full of decisions that students rarely see. Someone chooses the question, selects the evidence, draws the diagram, and decides what will count as a satisfactory explanation.

I am interested in how those decisions shape whose knowledge and experiences have a place in science learning. My work as a biology and chemistry teacher has led me toward justice-oriented approaches that examine racialized, gendered, linguistic, and other inequalities within the practices of science education. I want to understand how teachers and students might question those arrangements together, while attending carefully to the disciplinary science.

This page is a place for working through those ideas. It includes readings that have challenged my assumptions, examples of curriculum questions I would like to investigate, and the studies I have undertaken so far.

What a curriculum makes possible

One question I keep returning to concerns the authority built into ordinary school science. A curriculum can offer accurate content and still present its choice of examples, investigative questions, and historical accounts as though these decisions required no explanation. Students learn the science through that selection. They also encounter assumptions about which questions are legitimate and who is allowed to ask them.

In Desettling Expectations in Science Education, Bang and colleagues (2012) examine how established expectations restrict the knowledge valued in school science and students' positions within it. Calabrese Barton and Tan's account of rightful presence (2020) presses further on the relationships of authority and injustice that can persist even when students are welcomed into a learning activity. These readings leave me considering what teachers can examine in the curriculum itself, alongside what students learn from it.

For me, this connects anti-racist, decolonial, and queer-inclusive science education with questions of scientific practice. How have particular categories been developed? What histories of scientific knowledge are made visible? Whose observations or concerns could cause the class to change a procedure or revisit an explanation? Answering those questions calls for substantial disciplinary knowledge and a willingness to examine the institutions and relationships in which that knowledge is taught.

A question for curriculum work

Can a student's concern lead to a change in the evidence collected, the explanation developed, or the question the class is pursuing?

I would want to know what happened after the concern was raised, and who had a say in what followed.

Reading a chemistry problem more closely

A prospective curriculum example. This is an analysis of possible instructional choices, not a report of a lesson or an evaluated intervention.

Advanced Placement Chemistry · Equilibrium and nitrogen compounds

What does an equation allow us to ask?

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)

In a conventional equilibrium task, students might predict how increasing pressure at constant temperature affects the equilibrium composition. They need to compare the numbers of gaseous particles on each side and distinguish an equilibrium shift from a change in the equilibrium constant. Those are worthwhile chemical questions.

Ammonia production also connects directly to nitrogen fertilisers, agriculture, industrial energy demands, and the consequences of nitrogen entering surrounding environments. The chemistry invites further investigation of what is produced, who benefits from its use, and where environmental costs may be experienced.

How the inquiry could widen

Students could begin with the equilibrium model and then examine a question about nitrogen use and water quality. That would require work with chemical species, concentrations, transport, sampling, and the limits of environmental measurements. It would also involve deciding which waterways to study, whose concerns should inform the investigation, and what kinds of evidence could support a responsible claim.

Such a sequence needs care. Environmental inequality cannot be established simply by adding a community's name to a laboratory problem. Students would need credible local evidence, an appropriate research question, and permission where community engagement is involved. The people potentially affected should have a meaningful say in how their experiences are represented.

Morales-Doyle (2017) documents a justice-centred advanced chemistry class organized around an environmental racism issue identified by the community. His study gives a much more developed example of how disciplinary learning and community concerns can inform one another. I am interested in examining what such work demands of curriculum design and teacher preparation.

What a genetics representation teaches

A second prospective example examining scientific accuracy and the consequences of classification.

From an allele to a claim about a population

In genetics, an explanatory diagram often begins with a simplified cross or a carefully chosen trait. Such models help students reason about inheritance. Difficulties arise when the explanation moves from an allele's inheritance to claims about human groups without examining population history, environmental conditions, and variation within groups.

Consider an example involving haemoglobin variants and malaria. Explaining allele frequencies requires attention to evolutionary history and the relationship between a variant and particular environmental pressures. Racial labels alone cannot provide that explanation. A population-genetic account also needs to distinguish a biological pattern from the social categories used to describe people.

Donovan (2022) reviews evidence that genetics instruction can influence students' beliefs about racial essentialism. The implications are demanding for biology teaching. A teacher needs to preserve the scientific usefulness of genetic models while examining the claims those models do and do not support.

Questions I would put beside the diagram

  1. What exactly is being represented, and which assumptions make this simplified model useful?
  2. What information about population variation, selection, or environment would be needed to support a broader claim?
  3. Which histories and experiences are absent from the chosen example, and how could students help identify what needs revisiting?

These questions also connect with my interest in how biological sex, reproduction, and human variation are taught. The categories and representations change with the topic, but the responsibility to examine their explanatory boundaries remains.

How I would know that a question made a difference

A student raising an important question is a beginning. To understand whether it influenced the scientific work, I would need to follow what happened next. Did the class examine a different source? Did the teacher revise a diagram or change an investigation? Who proposed the revision, and who decided whether it was adequate? An encouraging reply from a teacher may be meaningful, although it offers limited evidence of a change in curricular authority.

For a classroom-based inquiry, I would want to examine the original task, the interaction surrounding the question, any revisions to the material, and participants' accounts of those decisions. Returning to a moment with the people involved might reveal interpretations that my reading of the recording missed. I would also need to ask who could disagree with the researcher's account and what it meant for students to take part when the researcher or teacher held institutional authority.

This is a methodological question I am still learning to approach. Work on rightful presence by Calabrese Barton and Tan (2020) and Krishnamoorthy, Duncan, and Tan (2026) helps me consider participation through consequential decisions and the relationships in which scientific knowledge is produced. I am interested in developing the preparation and partnerships that such research requires.
