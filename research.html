Research

A diagram of sex determination may help students understand a common developmental pathway. During a biology lesson, however, a student might ask how the diagram accounts for a case that develops differently. The teacher then has to consider whether the question points to a misunderstanding, a limitation of the representation, or a possibility worth investigating. That judgment can shape whether the student’s question becomes part of the scientific explanation the class develops.

I am interested in such decisions within justice-oriented science education. Students encounter explanations chosen through curricula, and they may participate in discussion without having much influence over the criteria used to judge their contributions. My proposed doctoral inquiry would examine how teachers and students negotiate the adequacy of scientific representations and whether students can take part in reconsidering the expectations through which reasoning is recognized as credible. I am particularly interested in a bounded sequence of secondary biology lessons on sex determination and variation in sex characteristics, while remaining attentive to the histories of science and inequalities involving gender, sexuality, race, class, and language.

Reconsidering assessment evidence in biology

In classroom action research with 28 AP Biology students, I examined two adjustments to assessment correction. Comparing ten students’ original explanations with reconstructions of the same photosynthesis mechanism revealed different questions for subsequent assessment, even among responses with the same later classification. Practitioner notes helped me distinguish concerns recorded while teaching from questions developed during retrospective analysis. The evidence informed the next inquiry without establishing why the explanations differed.

The limitations of a single response become clearer when it can be read alongside another record. In a study examined in the scoping review, Luis's shorter English translation left an ecological distinction less explicit than his earlier bilingual account. In my classroom inquiry, S20's later reconstruction omitted photosynthetic relationships present in the original explanation. These episodes occurred under different language demands, prompts, and feedback conditions. Each nonetheless shows how an earlier account can complicate a judgment drawn from the later response alone.

Reading an earlier explanation alongside a later response may alter the questions a teacher needs to ask. In my action research, I could specify those questions through retrospective analysis, though I did not administer them in the reported inquiry. Neither this inquiry nor the scoping review examined how students might subsequently influence the assessment or curriculum decisions themselves. I hope to follow those decisions through classroom interaction.

Epistemic agency and instructional decisions

How teachers respond when students question the explanations they are learning.

A teacher may have selected a diagram for its clarity, only to discover that the pathway it depicts cannot answer a question a student has raised. The next decision involves both the scientific substance of the question and the learning students have already undertaken. A correction may be appropriate when an important mechanism has been misunderstood. In other cases, the class may need to compare the representation with additional evidence or reconsider its explanatory scope. I want to follow how teachers make these judgments and what opportunities students have to influence them.

Curricular planning can create openings for students to make epistemic decisions. These may concern the methods of investigation, the phenomena around which the class organizes its work, or the models it develops, as Ko and Krist (2019) illustrate through a framework for adapting science curricula. During discussion, however, the possibilities also depend on what a teacher notices in students' contributions. Point-of-view accounts of teaching show how teachers attend to disciplinary substance, students' orientations toward knowledge-making, and their engagement when deciding whether to extend an inquiry (Krist et al., 2023). This relationship between instructional judgment and student influence is central to the study I hope to undertake. A multicase study of five beginning teachers likewise found that classroom discourse could invite students to work on an idea as epistemic agents or position the idea as a matter of correctness (Stroupe, 2014).

Planning for student-directed work also requires disciplinary guidance. A discussion of STEM curriculum and pedagogy considers how teachers prepare investigations that students can help direct (DeCoito, 2024). Classroom materials can then complicate that design in ways a teacher did not anticipate. In a Grade 6 knowledge-building classroom studying flight, for example, students' work with physical materials changed the conditions for a lesson initially organized around discourse and writing (Reeve, 2023). Web-based inquiry environments raise related questions about how curricular tools structure scientific explanations over time (Slotta & Linn, 2009). In each case, the interesting decision concerns how to respond to what students are actually doing while keeping the scientific work intellectually demanding.

Teachers make these decisions within professional and institutional relationships. A longitudinal case of a teacher's developing activist commitments traces the difficulty of sustaining them in everyday science teaching (Barrett, 2013). Students and mentors in an informal robotics competition also negotiated responsibility for knowledge through the relationships they established during their work (Puvirajah et al., 2012). The differences between these settings matter. They nevertheless invite me to examine how established responsibilities shape whose proposals can influence an inquiry. In a study of productive disciplinary engagement, student authority was sustained through shared disciplinary norms, accountability to peers, and access to relevant resources (Engle & Conant, 2002).

The question of influence also concerns justice. A conceptual analysis of science standards distinguishes performing practices selected by others from exercising agency over the knowledge a community develops (Miller et al., 2018). Within a framework of rightful presence, participation includes opportunities to challenge the practices through which some contributions are recognized and others are set aside (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020). I would need to examine the scientific grounds for a teacher's evaluation alongside the classroom relationships that make some judgments possible. Students' ideas may receive careful consideration even when they are ultimately rejected; an apparently open invitation to contribute may, in contrast, leave existing criteria entirely untouched.

A classroom inquiry about stress and community health offers a useful instance of this difficulty. Seventh-grade students questioned how a proposed survey could affect the people completing it. During their exchanges with adults, care for respondents became part of the discussion about what would make the survey a responsible way to produce knowledge (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2026).

Their concern changed what participants needed to consider when making an epistemic decision.

Cultural knowledge and the recognition of scientific reasoning

What a task makes visible, and what its interpretation may overlook.

What people include in an explanation depends partly on the question they are asked. When Menominee and European American fish experts sorted local species, Menominee participants more often mentioned habitat without being prompted. Both groups nevertheless showed strong agreement when asked explicitly to sort fish by habitat (Bang et al., 2007). That difference makes me attentive to the relationship between the form of an assessment and the knowledge participants choose to express. It also sits within a broader challenge to expectations that separate knowledge of nature from the cultural histories and practices through which it develops (Bang & Medin, 2010; Bang et al., 2012).

These findings arose in particular communities, and I would need to understand a student's circumstances before drawing an analogy with my own classroom. Other studies make that caution important in different ways. Muslim secondary science teachers in Canada and Pakistan discussed evolution through their professional and religious commitments (Asghar, 2013), while research on the learning of radioactivity examined relationships between learners' feelings and their engagement with physics (Alsop & Watts, 2000). Their topics and methods differ. Both prompt me to look beyond the correctness of a single response when considering what someone brings to a scientific explanation.

Learning also develops through shared tools and relationships, which can influence what participants have opportunities to explain (Nasir et al., 2014). Recognition is also shaped by the setting in which scientific activity takes place. Children attending community science clubs in Canada described engaging experiences while often maintaining a distinction between what happened in clubs and what counted as school science (Burke & Navas Iannini, 2021). Among doctoral students in physics, judgments about disciplinary competence were entangled with gendered expectations (Gonsalves, 2014). Recognizing someone as a science participant also has a history beyond the immediate setting. A longitudinal study of women of colour pursuing science degrees and careers traced how recognition by others contributed to different science identity trajectories (Carlone & Johnson, 2007). Taken together, the studies make me interested in the people and practices that determine whose work becomes recognizable as science.

The same concern applies to my analysis. If I label one contribution as cultural or everyday knowledge and another as scientific reasoning, the distinction may conceal the connections a student was making. I would want to see how a contribution developed, what evidence it drew upon, and how others responded before deciding what those categories can explain.

Multilingual participation and scientific reasoning

Tracing explanations through speech, writing, and shared representations.

In an English-medium biology lesson, a student may begin an explanation in Korean, sketch a process for a classmate, and later submit an answer in English. Each exchange can make different relationships visible. If the written response leaves out a mechanism previously discussed or drawn, I would need to examine how the idea developed before interpreting the omission as evidence of what the student understood.

Language also affects which ideas others recognize as scientific contributions. A student’s question may be translated and pursued, altered as the group develops its explanation, or set aside as the class prepares a written response. A synthesis of research on multilingual students’ science practices shows why researchers' views of language influence what they attend to as sensemaking (González-Howard et al., 2023). Related scholarship distinguishes access to existing practices from possibilities for changing which practices and resources a learning community values (Grapin et al., 2023). For my proposed inquiry, the question is what happened to the student's contribution as the scientific work continued. Critique among students is another relevant form of participation. In three middle-school classrooms, discourse and social-network analyses showed different patterns through which students critiqued peers' ideas while exercising epistemic agency (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020).

Those possibilities depend on the language conditions of the classroom. Research in Malaysian primary classrooms documents both teachers' and learners' agency and the restrictions that can surround translanguaging (Rajendram, 2023). In a Canadian university English programme, plurilingual instruction was compared with English-only instruction through measures of plurilingual and pluricultural competence (Galante, 2022). Neither population or outcome corresponds directly to my proposed secondary biology setting. Their different designs remind me to document how language is actually used rather than infer participation from a school's stated policy.

Students' reasoning may also be carried by the representations they revise with others. Analyses of bilingual science talk and multimodal scientific explanation provide ways to examine how meanings develop across languages and diagrams (Ünsal et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2014). Work with students' plurilingual identity texts likewise draws attention to the conversations involved in creating a representation (Prasad, 2015). Alongside this, a relational perspective on multiliteracies asks teachers to consider the people and contexts in which texts acquire meaning (Simon, 2011). I would want to follow the explanation across these encounters, keeping each representational form connected to what participants were trying to establish. An out-of-school investigation of electrical circuits makes the significance of that approach visible. Emergent bilingual children coordinated language, gestures, and drawings while constructing explanations, with the instructor's responses helping signal when those resources could be used (Suárez, 2020).

Interpretation still requires caution when the record appears complete. A longitudinal study of university adjustment considered language proficiency alongside academic acculturation, revealing the limitations of reducing students' experiences to a single measure (Neumann et al., 2023). The study concerns a different educational level, although its attention to the conditions surrounding language use is relevant to my methodological decisions. A further concern is how teachers respond when students' contributions introduce disagreement. Spoken-word poetry activities with multilingual university learners exposed tensions involved in antiracist language teaching (Burton, 2024). I would examine similar moments of hesitation or disagreement within their particular biology context rather than assume that opportunities for expression necessarily lead to influence.

When a scientific model is questioned

What counts as an adequate explanation, and who can help decide.

A diagram helps students identify relationships within a phenomenon, although any representation leaves some details beyond its immediate scope. When a student notices something the diagram does not explain, the class can reconsider the proposed mechanism or seek evidence that would establish whether the concern is warranted. The decision about what to examine introduces another question. Who is able to influence the standards by which the model is judged?

Some model-based inquiries have made those evaluative standards part of students' work. Fifth-grade students revised a shared list of criteria for scientific models following peer critique and classroom discussion (Murphy et al., 2023). In another study, two groups of seventh-grade students coordinated drawings, talk, and writing to evaluate and revise models using criteria supplied for the task (Cheung & Yang, 2026). Both studies involve model evaluation, although students participated differently in establishing what would count as a good model. That difference makes me interested in how criteria are proposed, maintained, or reconsidered when a student questions a representation already introduced as authoritative. A learning progression for scientific modelling offers a further distinction between making a model and understanding the reasons for its construction, use, and revision (Schwarz et al., 2009).

These inquiries took place at different levels and with different scientific topics from my proposed study. Their findings provide concepts and possible approaches to examining classroom interaction, while the role of evaluative criteria in lessons on sex determination remains a question for investigation. I am also interested in whether modelling and argumentation serve a question participants are trying to resolve, a concern raised in analyses of scientific practices as purposeful knowledge construction (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015).

Sex determination, variation, and explanatory limits

Why the purposes and assumptions of a biological representation deserve attention.

In a lesson on sex determination, a diagram showing a familiar developmental pathway may help students connect genetic regulation with subsequent biological changes. Its explanatory purpose also sets limits on what can be inferred from the illustration. Chromosomal patterns, gonadal differentiation, endocrine processes, and anatomical characteristics involve relationships that need to be considered in the context of a particular biological question. Clinical reviews of variations in sex development document how genetic and developmental processes can yield outcomes not captured by a single familiar pathway (Arboleda et al., 2014; Witchel, 2018). These reviews concern clinical science rather than classroom learning, so they provide biological context for the questions students might raise. If a student asks about a developmental pathway absent from the diagram, I would want to examine how the teacher and class decide whether the representation needs clarification or further evidence.

Teaching these topics also carries responsibilities beyond selecting a biologically accurate diagram. Principles developed for inclusive postsecondary biology education include introducing variation early, attending to the historical context of science, and presenting scientific understanding as revisable (Zemenick et al., 2022). The article does not test an intervention in secondary classrooms, though it helps me consider the care required when students explore sex-related variation. A question about a diagram should be possible to investigate without asking a student to disclose personal information.

Historical scholarship offers reasons to examine the purposes for which scientific classifications have been used. Claims that linked an XYY chromosome pattern with aggression depended partly on cultural assumptions about masculinity and were undermined by later research. A historical analysis traces the construction of this claim (Richardson, 2013), and a subsequent systematic review found no statistical evidence that XYY itself predisposes someone to aggressive or deviant behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). Accounts of medical decisions concerning intersex variation raise further questions about how classifications acquire authority beyond their immediate evidential scope (Fausto-Sterling, 2000; Davis, 2015). Research and writing on testosterone provide another example of the need to attend to measurement and interpretation before drawing claims about human behaviour (Jordan-Young & Karkazis, 2019). In elite athletics, policy controversies have likewise exposed difficulties in treating a hormone measurement as sufficient justification for institutional classification and eligibility rules (Karkazis et al., 2012). Each concerns a distinct set of scientific and historical claims. I would draw on them to examine the evidence behind particular representations rather than assume their conclusions apply interchangeably.

Classroom participation also depends on how teachers make space for questions without anticipating which students will raise them. An ethic of hospitality toward gender diversity cautions against approaches built on knowing students' identities in advance (Airton, 2023). In a different curricular debate, analysis of expertise in sex education draws attention to the ways students' own engagement with the curriculum may escape the terms established by adult advocates (Gilbert, 2021). These works encourage me to consider the relationship between the scientific question and the classroom conditions in which it can be pursued.

There are wider political and participatory questions here. A critical account of trans-exclusionary ideas examines their relationship with carceral ways of exercising authority (Alexander, 2023), while participatory arts research develops an account of queer joy through collective educational practices (Burkholder et al., 2025). Neither investigates how secondary students evaluate biological diagrams. Their relevance to my interests lies in the questions they raise about authority, participation, and the possibilities of students contributing to educational work. Within the proposed biology study, I would still need to establish what scientific judgments were warranted and how participants arrived at them.

Proposed doctoral study

A qualitative inquiry into scientific representations, classroom judgments, and students' epistemic participation.

An organizing question for the proposed study is how students’ questions about the adequacy of scientific representations are interpreted during secondary biology lessons and under what conditions those questions influence the evaluation and development of classroom explanations.

I hope to follow a bounded sequence of secondary biology lessons addressing sex determination and variation in sex characteristics. These topics bring questions about the explanatory purposes and limitations of biological representations into view. A diagram that clarifies a familiar developmental pathway may provide a useful starting point while leaving other pathways unexplained. Students might ask which developmental processes are represented, what evidence supports the explanation, and whether a different diagram or source is needed. I want to understand how teachers and students work through such questions and how the criteria for evaluating the representation are established.

The proposed inquiry would attend to distinctions that are easy to collapse in a discussion of student voice. A student might raise a scientifically warranted criticism of a model, make an incorrect biological claim, or ask a question that the present evidence cannot resolve. A teacher who rejects a claim after examining its evidence is making a different decision from one who dismisses a contribution without considering its reasoning. Both the disciplinary substance and the conditions of participation would need to be examined before interpreting what took place.

My interest is in what happens beyond the initial exchange. If a question leads the class to compare new evidence, qualify an explanation, or revise a later task, those changes need to be traced through the participants' subsequent work. A teacher may also decide to return to the existing lesson objective, and students may disagree about whether their contribution was taken seriously. Following such episodes would help me examine both the possibilities and limitations of student participation in scientific knowledge-making.

A related proposed AP Biology sequence on haemoglobin variation explores how students might question population categories when making genetic and evolutionary claims. It is a curriculum proposal rather than an implemented intervention or a second doctoral study.

Following classroom decisions across lessons

I anticipate using video-recorded classroom interaction to follow when a question emerges, how participants respond, and whether the idea returns as the lesson sequence develops. The instructional diagrams, students' written and visual representations, and relevant curricular materials would help me compare what was initially available with what participants subsequently changed or retained. Guidance on video research in the learning sciences identifies the selection of episodes, analytical approach, technologies, and participant protection as consequential decisions rather than merely technical steps (Derry et al., 2010). The study would examine a bounded sequence of lessons rather than attempting to attribute broad learning outcomes to a single discussion.

Conversations with participating teachers and students could clarify how they interpreted selected episodes. I would consider those retrospective accounts alongside the interactional record, particularly where participants disagree about whether a suggestion influenced the lesson. Language choices, gestures, drawings, and other representational resources would also matter when tracing how an idea becomes recognisable as a scientific contribution.

Interpretation would require considering alternative explanations. A revised diagram might follow a student's proposal, reflect an existing teaching plan, or result from several people deciding together. I would look for the interaction and subsequent evidence that supports a particular account, including episodes in which a teacher's correction enabled further inquiry and others in which an invitation to question the model had little apparent influence.

Justice and accountability in interpretation

Whose contributions are recognized, and how the researcher's own judgments shape the record.

Rightful presence would inform how I examine the relation between scientific decisions and the authority to make them. A question about sex determination can be evaluated by examining developmental mechanisms and evidence. It can also be received through assumptions about which bodies or experiences are typical, whose interpretations seem credible, and what is considered appropriate to discuss. The study would need to distinguish a scientifically justified evaluation from a pattern in which a student's contribution receives little consideration for reasons bound up with classroom hierarchy or inequitable expectations.

Neither participation in the research nor discussion of a biological representation should require students to disclose their sex characteristics, gender identity, medical history, or family circumstances. Published scientific cases and instructional representations can support inquiry without making anyone's body or identity an object of classroom investigation. The research procedures would also require consent, secure handling of recordings, and attention to how classroom power affects participants' ability to disagree or decline.

My account would remain partial. Camera placement, transcription, episode selection, and the labels I apply to students' reasoning all shape what readers can see, a concern also developed in Haraway's (1988) writing on situated knowledge. I would document these choices and attend to moments that complicate my expectations. Where participants offer different accounts of an exchange, the differences should remain visible rather than being treated as errors to be reconciled into one interpretation.

Scholarly writing

In a separate essay on biological classification, I examine how evidence about haemoglobin variation and sex chromosomes has been interpreted beyond the relationships it can establish. The discussion develops questions about the scientific authority of categories and what students might be able to change when those categories are examined in class. This is a critical reading of published scholarship, independent of my two empirical inquiries.
