Research

Much of my research begins with a fairly ordinary teaching difficulty. A student may correct an answer without showing how they understand the underlying process. Another may express an idea more fully through a diagram or conversation than through a written response. I want to learn how to interpret those differences responsibly and how teachers can respond to what students bring into the scientific work of a classroom.

Where I am working now

My current projects examine assessment records and the evidence used to interpret multilingual learners' scientific understanding. I am also developing questions about youth participation, biological classification, and whose concerns influence classroom discussions.

How these questions began

In my AP Biology classroom, I noticed students could sometimes identify an error after receiving feedback and still struggle to explain the causal steps in a later question. That led me to look more carefully at reflections, preparation records, and successive explanations. Each record offered something useful, though the records did not always agree.

Teaching in English with students who have different language experiences has raised a related question. A short answer may reflect difficulty with the scientific idea, the language needed to explain it, the representation used in the task, or several things at once. I became interested in how researchers distinguish these possibilities when they make claims about students' understanding.

Questions I hope to study next

I am interested in what happens when students question the categories and representations used in biology, especially in discussions of biological sex and human variation. How does a teacher respond? Does the question change the evidence the class examines, the explanation being developed, or the way the topic is taught? I would like to understand these exchanges from the perspectives of both students and teachers.

In future work, I hope to examine classroom video and invite participants to revisit particular moments with me. I am also interested in developing curriculum with young people and teachers. These are directions I hope to pursue, and I have much to learn about approaching them carefully.

How I hope to work with students

A student's question about sex, gender, or human variation should not require them to share anything about their own identity. I want students to have control over what they disclose, and I want to attend to the scientific substance of their questions without making assumptions about why they asked. These commitments also shape how I think about consent, interpretation, and returning research findings to participants.
