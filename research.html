Science education research · Secondary biology and chemistry

Research

I am interested in how a student's scientific idea becomes something a teacher or a classroom can recognize, question, and work with.

In an English-medium science classroom, a student may express parts of an explanation through different languages, a drawing, a calculation, or a conversation. The teacher must decide what the contribution establishes, what remains unclear, and what to ask next. Those decisions affect both our interpretation of learning and the opportunities students have to participate in the science.

My questions have developed through teaching biology and chemistry in South Korea, studying linked classroom assessment records, and reviewing research on multilingual science learning. I am also interested in what happens when young people question the categories and representations used to teach human biology.

How my questions developed

In teaching Advanced Placement Biology and Chemistry, I spend a great deal of time reading explanations. Sometimes a student identifies the correct outcome but leaves the intervening processes unstated. Sometimes a diagram carries a relationship that the written response does not. A correction can make an answer look more complete, although I still need to know which connections the student can explain without that support.

My classroom action research gave me a way to examine these questions across records. I could compare an original explanation with a reflection, a preparation report, and a later reconstruction of the same mechanism. They gave me different information. In two paired cases, the later reconstructions even omitted relationships students had articulated earlier. That observation left me with more precise questions to ask about the task, the response, and the student's reasoning.

Reviewing published studies of multilingual science learners broadened the problem. Researchers interpreted students' ideas from written answers, talk, drawings, and models, often under different language and participation conditions. I became interested in the decisions that connect these forms of work to a claim about understanding, and in whose contributions can influence the scientific activity of a classroom.

A question that stays with me

When a student offers an idea, what can I reasonably learn from the form in which it appears, and what opportunity should the student have to develop it further?

The answer depends on the biological or chemical concept, the task, the interaction, and the purpose of the judgment.

Research interests

These questions overlap, although each calls for different evidence and forms of inquiry.

Language, modeling, and explanation

Recognizing scientific ideas across forms of expression

What becomes visible when students can move among speech, writing, languages, and representations?

Biology explanations can require students to coordinate entities, processes, and relationships that are difficult to express in one sentence. A student might draw movement across a membrane, use a familiar Korean term while describing a process in English, or develop an explanation through exchanges with classmates. I want to understand how a teacher judges the science in those contributions, including cases where their different forms offer different levels of detail.

Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021) examined how multilingual and multimodal work became part of scientific modeling in an English-dominant classroom. Their study gives me a concrete way to think about the relationship between expression, interpretation, and participation. It also raises questions about which parts of a contribution remain available when students are asked to translate or condense their reasoning for an assessment.

Assessment and mechanistic reasoning

Following causal relationships over time

What can linked responses establish about the reasoning a student can express under different task conditions?

When a student explains the inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport, naming ATP or plant death is only part of the work. The explanation needs connections among electron movement, proton gradients, ATP and NADPH production, carbon fixation, and the consequences for plant function. I am interested in where students establish those links, where they hesitate, and what changes when feedback or a differently worded question is introduced.

Russ and colleagues (2008) offer a framework for noticing mechanistic reasoning as it emerges in student discourse. Kane's (2013) account of validity reminds me to examine the inferences connecting a response to the claim made about it. Together, these ideas help me ask which relationships a task reveals and what additional evidence would be needed before drawing a broader conclusion about understanding.

Biology, classification, and human variation

Teaching categories with scientific and human care

How do classrooms respond when students examine the limits of a biological category or representation?

Biologists use categories for specific investigative purposes. In teaching sex determination or differentiation, for example, chromosomes, gene expression, gonadal development, hormones, gametes, and other traits require attention to distinct biological processes. An illustration that treats all these features as one inevitable sequence can hide relevant variation. I want students to be able to ask what a diagram represents, which organisms or populations it describes, and where its explanatory reach ends.

Work by Zemenick and colleagues (2022) examines ways to teach biological diversity without relying on misleading simplifications. Wright and Delgado (2023) connect gender and sexual diversity in STEM education with the epistemic and social conditions of learning. My interest also concerns the choices teachers make when scientific classification has consequences for how young people are discussed. A student's question about a biological category is an opportunity for disciplinary examination; it does not give a teacher grounds to assume anything about that student's identity.

Participation and justice-oriented science education

Whose questions change the scientific work?

When students raise a concern, how is it considered in the explanations, materials, or decisions the class develops?

Participation involves more than receiving an opportunity to speak. I am interested in what happens afterward. Does a student's question lead the class to reconsider a diagram, distinguish two kinds of evidence, or revise a claim? Who is invited to determine what counts as an adequate explanation? A teacher's authority over tasks, turns, and assessment judgments makes those questions particularly important.

Calabrese Barton and Tan's account of rightful presence (2020) attends to how historical injustices and classroom relationships shape consequential participation. Bang and colleagues (2012) examine expectations that restrict what forms of knowledge science education recognizes. These works encourage me to consider who can influence the work of science in a classroom, while remaining careful about what any single interaction can establish.

A classroom question worth examining

This hypothetical teaching moment illustrates how the research interests connect. It is a proposed example, not a reported incident or a student research record.

An illustrative biology discussion

A textbook diagram presents an XY developmental pathway as if it describes sex development in all organisms. A student asks whether the same sequence occurs across species and how the diagram accounts for variation.

There is biological work to do here. The class can identify the organism for which the diagram was designed, distinguish chromosomal mechanisms from developmental outcomes, and investigate other sex-determination systems. The teacher also decides how the question enters the discussion and whether the representation is revised.

What I would want to examine

The science
Which mechanisms does the diagram represent accurately? Where has a species-specific example been generalized beyond the available evidence?
The interaction
How does the teacher respond, and which subsequent contributions influence the explanation the class develops?
The judgment
What evidence supports an interpretation of the student's scientific understanding? What remains unresolved without further conversation or work?

The discussion can remain focused on the biological question. Students retain control over whether they share anything personal. A careful account of such a moment would consider both scientific accuracy and participation, without presuming a student's reasons for asking.

How I hope to investigate these questions

From records to classroom interaction

My existing inquiries use document review, assessment records, coding, and close comparisons of student explanations. I want to extend this work through video-based interaction analysis, including attention to what happens before and after a particular contribution. Returning with participants to selected moments could help me learn how teachers and students understood an exchange from their different positions.

Learning with teachers and young people

I am interested in collaborative curriculum development that allows students and teachers to examine representations and classroom questions together. Such work would require negotiated decisions about the problems worth studying, the changes worth trying, and the evidence used to judge them. These are directions for future inquiry, not methods I am claiming to have implemented already.

Care with evidence and participation

Classroom research brings responsibilities that cannot be resolved by removing names from a document. The teacher may also be responsible for grading, and a student's question may involve experiences they have no wish to disclose. I want to examine how research and curriculum decisions can be made with meaningful consent, reasonable privacy, and student control over personal information. Interpretation also needs an audit trail. A compelling moment is still only one moment, and a proposed explanation should remain open to challenge.

Scholarship on gender-inclusive science teaching, including Rende Mendoza and Johnson (2024), is useful here because it considers teaching decisions together with questions of authority and whose experience enters curriculum development.
