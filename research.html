Justice-oriented science education

Research

A science lesson is full of decisions that students rarely see. Someone chooses the question, selects the evidence, draws the diagram, and decides what will count as a satisfactory explanation.

I am interested in how those decisions shape whose knowledge and experiences have a place in science learning. My work as a biology and chemistry teacher has led me toward justice-oriented approaches that examine racialized, gendered, linguistic, and other inequalities within the practices of science education. I want to understand how teachers and students might question those arrangements together, while attending carefully to the disciplinary science.

The questions below concern cultural knowledge, scientific authority, and the evidence needed to interpret classroom participation. I distinguish these developing interests from the studies I have already undertaken.

What a curriculum makes possible

One question I keep returning to concerns the authority built into ordinary school science. A curriculum can offer accurate content and still present its choice of examples, investigative questions, and historical accounts as though these decisions required no explanation. Students learn the science through that selection. They also encounter assumptions about which questions are legitimate and who is allowed to ask them.

In Desettling Expectations in Science Education, Bang and colleagues (2012) examine how established expectations restrict the knowledge valued in school science and students' positions within it. Calabrese Barton and Tan's account of rightful presence (2020) presses further on the relationships of authority and injustice that can persist even when students are welcomed into a learning activity. These readings leave me considering what teachers can examine in the curriculum itself, alongside what students learn from it.

For me, this connects anti-racist, decolonial, and queer-inclusive science education with questions of scientific practice. How have particular categories been developed? What histories of scientific knowledge are made visible? Whose observations or concerns could cause the class to change a procedure or revisit an explanation? Answering those questions calls for substantial disciplinary knowledge and a willingness to examine the institutions and relationships in which that knowledge is taught.

A question for curriculum work

Can a student's concern lead to a change in the evidence collected, the explanation developed, or the question the class is pursuing?

I would want to know what happened after the concern was raised, and who had a say in what followed.

How culture enters scientific observation

Learning, classification, and the forms of knowledge that school science recognises.

What counts as a meaningful relationship among organisms depends partly on the questions being asked and the experience brought to the task. A classification that privileges taxonomic similarity can organize observations differently from an account concerned with ecological interactions. Such differences have consequences for how a learner approaches evidence about the natural world.

Bang, Medin, and Atran (2007) investigated cultural models of nature and how people reason about biological relationships. Their analysis considers the influence of cultural frameworks and experience on what is noticed and inferred. It cautions against treating a participant's explanation as an isolated measure of general reasoning ability. Nasir and colleagues (2014) describe learning as participation in historically developed cultural practices, involving tools, social relationships, and shared activities. Reading these works together raises a question about how school assessments recognize the reasoning students bring to scientific problems.

A possible investigation would compare how students explain an ecological relationship during an initial conversation, a diagramming task, and the interpretation of a published data display. I would examine the organisms and processes they select, the causal relationships they propose, and any revisions they make when different evidence becomes available. Differences between responses would need to be interpreted in relation to task demands and opportunities to express an idea. A comparison of formats alone would not establish that one form is more authentic to a student's cultural knowledge.

This area also raises questions about the researcher's own categories. Describing a contribution as everyday knowledge, cultural knowledge, or disciplinary knowledge is itself an interpretive decision. I would need to justify those descriptions with attention to the particular activity and to the people whose work is being interpreted.

What makes an account of science accountable?

Objectivity, representation, and the position from which knowledge claims are made.

A scientific account has to be open to scrutiny. Its credibility depends on the methods used to produce evidence, the limits of those methods, and the possibility of questioning its conclusions. Yet the decisions that make an account possible, including where to observe, what to measure, and which classifications to use, are not independent of the people and institutions making them.

In Situated Knowledges, Haraway (1988) challenges the idea of a view from nowhere and argues for an account of objectivity attentive to partial perspective and responsibility for knowledge claims. I am interested in what this demand would mean for science education research itself. A classroom recording captures certain actions and words, while leaving histories, intentions, and experiences only partly accessible. The researcher's transcription, selection of episodes, and analytic labels all influence the resulting account.

That creates a methodological obligation. If I describe a student's question as disagreement, uncertainty, or resistance to a scientific category, I need evidence for that interpretation. Other participants may understand the moment differently, and a participant's later explanation may complicate what appeared evident in the recording. Returning to the material with participants could help examine those differences, although agreement would not by itself settle the analysis.

I also want to read scholarship on classification and human variation with this question in mind. Biological categories have specific explanatory uses and limitations. Examining how a category was constructed, the evidence supporting it, and the consequences of its application is part of evaluating a scientific explanation. The intellectual challenge is to preserve biological precision while examining the history and authority of the categories through which that precision is expressed.

How I would know that a question made a difference

A student raising an important question is a beginning. To understand whether it influenced the scientific work, I would need to follow what happened next. Did the class examine a different source? Did the teacher revise a diagram or change an investigation? Who proposed the revision, and who decided whether it was adequate? An encouraging reply from a teacher may be meaningful, although it offers limited evidence of a change in curricular authority.

For a classroom-based inquiry, I would want to examine the original task, the interaction surrounding the question, any revisions to the material, and participants' accounts of those decisions. Returning to a moment with the people involved might reveal interpretations that my reading of the recording missed. I would also need to ask who could disagree with the researcher's account and what it meant for students to take part when the researcher or teacher held institutional authority.

This is a methodological question I am still learning to approach. Work on rightful presence by Calabrese Barton and Tan (2020) and Krishnamoorthy, Duncan, and Tan (2026) helps me consider participation through consequential decisions and the relationships in which scientific knowledge is produced. I am interested in developing the preparation and partnerships that such research requires.
