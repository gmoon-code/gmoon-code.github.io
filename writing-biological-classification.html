What can a biological classification explain?

Biological classifications help researchers organize observations and compare cases. Their usefulness depends on what has been measured and the question being asked. Explaining a mechanism requires evidence about relationships that a category alone cannot supply. Classroom use raises another question. A label introduced for a particular scientific purpose can become an explanation for much more than the evidence supports. I am interested in how students might examine that extension and whether their questions could alter the evidence or representations the class considers.

Two cases bring different parts of this problem into view. Haemoglobin S and malaria show how genetic effects depend on genotype, environment, and the outcomes a study measures. The history of claims about sex chromosomes shows how a measurable biological feature acquired explanations of behaviour that required evidence beyond the chromosome itself. Both raise questions about which interpretations become part of a science curriculum and who can ask for them to be reconsidered.

What the haemoglobin evidence establishes

Haemoglobin S (HbS) is a useful example of how an explanation changes when its level of analysis changes. The variant can be described through a specific alteration in the β-globin protein, and its consequences for red blood cells can be examined at the molecular and cellular levels. A population-level account introduces another set of relationships. Where falciparum malaria has affected survival and reproduction, the malaria-related advantage associated with carrying one HbS allele can contribute to the persistence of that allele, even though having two copies can cause serious disease.

The claims within that account still depend on what a study measured. In two cohorts of children on the Kenyan coast, Williams and colleagues (2005) reported different estimates of protection associated with sickle cell trait (HbAS). They observed approximately 50% protection against mild clinical malaria, 75% against hospital admission for malaria, and almost 90% against severe or complicated malaria, with no reduction in symptomless parasitaemia. These findings provide a more specific account than the statement that HbAS protects against malaria. They distinguish infection without symptoms from several clinical outcomes and retain the population in which the comparisons were conducted.

The differences among these malaria outcomes become consequential when the class moves from clinical protection to the persistence of HbS across generations. Modelling that change requires estimates of how each genotype contributes to subsequent generations through survival and reproduction, with malaria exposure and population history also affecting those relationships. The cohort's estimates of protection against particular illnesses do not supply those reproductive fitness values. Asking what the researchers followed over time would therefore help students establish which parts of an evolutionary explanation the study supports and where additional evidence is needed.

A second difficulty arises if a lesson substitutes a racial designation for the genotype measured in the study. HbS has a history associated with malaria selection in geographically and historically varied populations. A person's racial identity therefore cannot establish whether they carry the allele. The educational problem extends beyond correcting a factual mistake. When racial labels appear to explain a genetic outcome, the class needs to examine what was measured, how the participants were described, and which relationships the proposed category actually helps explain. In his review of genetic essentialism and genetics education, Donovan (2022) discusses how the framing of genetic content can influence students' beliefs about racial groups. The biological precision of a lesson and its social implications are connected through the claims students are asked to accept.

When a chromosome acquires a wider meaning

Sex-chromosome research presents a different history of classification. Genetic features are measurable, but what researchers infer from them depends on the biological relationship under investigation. Chromosomes, developmental pathways, endocrine processes, and reproductive functions describe related aspects of biology whose relationships require explanation. Treating a chromosomal designation as sufficient evidence for a behavioural characteristic involves an additional inference that must be justified.

Richardson's (2013) history of sex-chromosome science traces how cultural understandings of gender influenced scientific questions and interpretations. Her discussion of the XYY chromosome pattern is particularly revealing. During the 1960s and 1970s, claims about aggression and criminality became associated with the presence of an additional Y chromosome. Later reviews did not find sufficient evidence to treat the XYY pattern itself as a cause of aggressive or criminal behaviour (Re & Birkhoff, 2015). Identifying a chromosomal pattern and explaining a person's behaviour require different kinds of evidence.

The history of the XYY claims gives students an opportunity to investigate how the association was constructed and why later evidence challenged it. They could begin by examining how the original researchers defined aggression and selected participants, then consider which comparisons would be needed to evaluate the proposed association. Reading the descriptions of the chromosomal pattern alongside those methodological choices would also invite questions about how assumptions concerning masculinity influenced the interpretations. In this way, the historical episode becomes a study of how researchers define outcomes, select evidence, and justify claims about biological variation.

The two histories concern different biological questions and kinds of classification. An allele identified through genetic analysis, a racial category used to describe a population, and a chromosomal pattern identified by karyotyping have different evidential roles and histories. Comparing the cases helps locate where an interpretation extends beyond the relationship established by the original measurement, while keeping those distinctions intact.

From an evidential question to a curricular decision

A teacher can plan a lesson to distinguish genotype from population history or measured physiology from claims about behaviour. Yet the scientific direction of the lesson may change when students identify a limitation that was absent from the planned explanation. Suppose a class is asked to interpret the HbAS cohort findings and a student proposes comparing how different studies defined malaria outcomes. Pursuing that proposal would require the class to reconsider which sources it needs and how it will judge whether findings are comparable.

Another student might question why a textbook map groups populations under a racial label when the scientific claim concerns allele frequencies. Investigating that question could involve examining the sampling methods behind the map, separating geographic patterns from individual genotypes, and tracing how the labels were selected. The teacher would still be responsible for the scientific demands of the inquiry, including what the evidence can support. Students' contributions could affect which sources are examined and what needs to be justified within the explanation.

The educational question concerns who can influence those choices. Students might be permitted to critique a category without having any effect on the sources or conclusions that structure the lesson. A question that leads the class to consult different evidence, qualify a claim, or revise a representation would have a more consequential role. Scholarship on rightful presence (Calabrese Barton & Tan, 2020) helps me consider whose contributions can influence the work of a learning community, including how racialised, gendered, and linguistic hierarchies affect which proposals are recognized as scientifically worthwhile.

These classroom decisions have limits and responsibilities. A question about chromosomes or population genetics must never be treated as evidence about a student's identity, ancestry, or medical history. Published and hypothetical records can support scientific discussion without requiring personal disclosure. Where a student's proposal cannot be pursued because suitable evidence is unavailable or the course has other constraints, examining the reasons for that decision may still help the class understand how a scientific investigation is bounded.

Following whose questions change the work

My classroom action research left me with questions about what a student had understood when an earlier explanation contained connections absent from a later one. The scoping review raised related questions about which students' scientific contributions became recognizable as evidence. Reading these projects together has drawn my attention to who gets to decide what the class examines after a question or unexpected explanation arises. The historical cases in this essay suggest ways to study that decision as part of science teaching.

The initial response to a student's question gives only part of the picture. A teacher might acknowledge a concern while retaining the original activity, or the class might return to it after examining a new source. In either case, the significance of the contribution depends on the subsequent scientific work and on how participants understand the decision. A careful account would need to distinguish a change in the representation itself from a change in the questions and evidence used to evaluate it. This distinction also matters when teachers and students disagree about whether a suggestion has been taken seriously.

In pursuing anti-racist, anti-colonial biology education that is inclusive of queer and trans students, I want to examine how particular explanations acquired authority and which questions students are able to bring into scientific discussion. Doing so would require attention to the histories, relationships, and circumstances of the students and teachers involved. The cases considered here inform questions for future research and curriculum design; they do not describe an intervention I have implemented.
