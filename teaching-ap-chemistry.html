Teaching / AP Chemistry

AP Chemistry Unit 7 · Original instructional design, prepared for this website

Chemical equilibrium, molecular models, and evidence

The reaction quotient helps students predict the direction in which a chemical mixture will change under specified conditions. I am interested in what happens when they connect that numerical comparison with the molecular processes and observations it is meant to describe.

I developed this four-meeting proposal around a hypothetical ammonia equilibrium problem and a separate compression example. The calculations provide opportunities to examine the relationship between reaction quotient, equilibrium, and reaction rates. A later investigation of nitrogen in waterways asks what additional evidence is needed once the scientific question moves beyond the reacting mixture.

Reaction quotient and equilibrium direction

Consider ammonia formation from nitrogen and hydrogen. In AP Chemistry Unit 7, students calculate the reaction quotient, compare it with the equilibrium constant, and predict the system's net response to a disturbance. The challenge is to connect that prediction with an account of the forward and reverse molecular processes.

Balanced gas-phase reaction

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)

Qp = PNH32 / (PN2 × PH23)

For an illustrative mixture at a fixed temperature, suppose the initial partial pressures are 1.0 atm of N2, 2.0 atm of H2, and 1.0 atm of NH3. Let Kp = 0.50 at this hypothetical temperature.

First interpretation

Qp = 1.02 / (1.0 × 2.03) = 0.125

Here, Qp is below Kp. The reaction therefore has a net tendency toward the products as the mixture approaches equilibrium at the same temperature.

Comparing Qp with Kp predicts the net direction of compositional change. To explain how the mixture approaches equilibrium, students must also consider the ongoing forward and reverse reactions and the difference between their rates at a given moment.

The initial pressures and Kp value are invented solely for this mathematical exercise. They are not industrial measurements or data gathered from students.

Macroscopic, particulate, and symbolic accounts

Observable or measurable

Macroscopic evidence

What would a sensor, sample analysis, or pressure measurement show as composition changes? Measurements make a change observable, but the explanation requires an account of the underlying particles and processes.

Molecules and interactions

Particulate reasoning

Forward and reverse reactions continue to occur. At equilibrium their rates are equal, even though nitrogen, hydrogen, and ammonia can remain present in different amounts. “Equilibrium” does not imply equal quantities of each gas.

Equations and comparison

Symbolic reasoning

Qp is constructed from the current partial pressures. Kp applies at a specified temperature. Comparing them gives the net direction required to approach equilibrium under those conditions.

A measurement can establish a change in composition, while a particulate model depicts molecular events and an equation expresses quantitative relationships. These accounts offer different information that students need to connect (Johnstone, 1991; Talanquer, 2011). I would examine whether an explanation uses the comparison between Qp and Kp to reason about the mixture, while recognizing that the comparison does not determine reaction speed.

A particulate drawing might show more NH3 after a disturbance without explaining what led to the change. I could then ask the student to relate the drawing to the forward and reverse reactions and to the comparison between Qp and Kp. A correct calculation would invite a similar question if the explanation of net direction remained unclear.

Compression of an equilibrium mixture

For this second example, begin with an equilibrium mixture at the same hypothetical temperature. Suppose PN2 = 1.0 atm, PH2 = 2.0 atm, and PNH3 = 2.0 atm. The initial Qp is 4.0 / (1.0 × 8.0) = 0.50, matching Kp. If the container volume is then halved at constant temperature, the ideal-gas approximation predicts that all three partial pressures initially double before reaction changes the composition.

Qp,new = (4.0)2 / [(2.0) × (4.0)3] = 0.125

The new value of Qp is one-quarter of Kp, so the mixture has a net tendency to form more ammonia as it re-equilibrates. The immediate doubling of all partial pressures and the later change in composition occur at different stages. At constant temperature, compression changes Qp, while Kp remains fixed.

This separate example begins at equilibrium. Its pressure calculation assumes ideal-gas behaviour, an instantaneous volume change, and no intervening reaction during compression. All stated pressure values are illustrative.

The proposed meetings would need to accommodate the class's prior knowledge, laboratory or simulation access, and time for feedback. Interpreting possible student responses

These are invented diagnostic examples, not quotations from students or measured misunderstandings in my AP Chemistry class.

Hypothetical response

“At equilibrium, all concentrations are equal.”

The response confuses equal reaction rates with equal amounts. I would ask for a particle-level explanation showing two reactions continuing at equal rates while the amounts of species remain unequal.

Hypothetical response

“Pressure increases, so K increases.”

The response confuses the reaction quotient's immediate change with the equilibrium constant. I would ask students to calculate Qp before and after compression at the same temperature and explain what controls Kp.

Hypothetical response

“The reaction shifts right because there are fewer gas molecules.”

The statement predicts the expected direction for the compression case but leaves the relationship with current composition implicit. A Qp calculation could test and clarify the reasoning.

The hypothetical responses call for different follow-up questions. Confusing equal rates with equal quantities requires an account of continuing molecular reactions; treating Kp as pressure-dependent requires returning to the distinction between temperature and composition. The point of gathering responses is to determine which question will help a student examine the particular relationship that remains unclear, a central concern of formative assessment (Black & Wiliam, 2009).

Independent equilibrium task

A constructed equilibrium example checking whether a numerical result is supported by an explanation of reaction rates. It has not been administered as part of the documented classroom record.

Student-facing task · Constant temperature, ideal-gas approximation

For N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), the equilibrium constant Kp is 0.50 at a fixed hypothetical temperature. A mixture has PN2 = 2.0 atm, PH2 = 1.0 atm, and PNH3 = 1.0 atm. Calculate Qp and predict the initial net direction of reaction. Describe the forward and reverse reaction rates at the equilibrium represented by these values. Explain why the numerical relationship between Qp and Kp does not establish a reaction rate.

Worked explanation and feedback

Using the course-level partial-pressure expression, Qp = (1.0)2/[(2.0)(1.0)3] = 0.50. The mixture is already at equilibrium under the stated conditions, so no net composition change is predicted. The forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates, although those rates cannot be calculated from Qp or Kp alone. A correct equality is therefore a starting point for checking the explanation of dynamic equilibrium.

The equality Qp = Kp establishes the equilibrium condition under the stated assumptions, but it supplies no numerical reaction rate. If a student concludes that both reactions have stopped, I would ask them to account for an unchanged composition alongside continuing molecular events. A claim that all partial pressures must be equal calls for attention to the equilibrium expression, while a question about speed would require kinetic evidence.

CriterionWhat an adequate answer suppliesInterpretive boundary
Symbolic expressionCorrect stoichiometric exponents, partial-pressure substitution, and Qp = 0.50.Arithmetic accuracy alone does not demonstrate particulate reasoning.

Limits of inferenceRate constants or time-dependent measurements would be needed to discuss speed.An equilibrium constant alone cannot establish the time needed to reach equilibrium.

The pressure-based equations use the usual AP Chemistry convention. In a thermodynamic treatment, gas activities are referenced to a standard pressure, making the equilibrium quantities dimensionless.

Immediate and subsequent effects of compression

Newly authored worked prompt, linked to the pressure example above. Values are hypothetical.

Student-facing reasoning check

An ideal-gas equilibrium mixture of N2, H2, and NH3 starts at the pressures given in the compression example. The container volume is halved rapidly while temperature is kept constant. Explain (a) what happens to each partial pressure immediately, before the reaction proceeds; (b) how the resulting Qp compares with Kp; and (c) why the mixture subsequently changes composition. Would a catalyst change the equilibrium composition under the same conditions? Justify your answer.

Criteria for a complete explanation

Immediately after idealized compression, each partial pressure doubles to 2.0, 4.0, and 4.0 atm, respectively. Qp becomes 0.125, below the unchanged Kp of 0.50. Net ammonia formation follows as the composition re-equilibrates. During that later response, the partial pressures change again, so the doubled values are not the final equilibrium values. A catalyst can accelerate both forward and reverse processes without changing Kp or the equilibrium composition at the specified temperature.

A prediction based on the smaller number of gas particles on the product side gives the expected direction for this compression case. I would still ask students to connect that prediction with the immediate Qp calculation and the subsequent response of the mixture. If they claimed that Kp changed during compression, we would return to the conditions that determine the equilibrium constant.

Environmental nitrogen and evidence

A prospective inquiry architecture, kept separate from the hypothetical ammonia calculations above.

The Haber equilibrium model describes ammonia formation within a reacting system. Fertiliser production and use lead to a different investigation when nitrogen compounds enter waterways and undergo biological and chemical transformations. Explaining a measured nitrate concentration could require evidence about sources, transport, and changing chemical species, including possible contributions from manure or wastewater. Students would first need to identify where the equilibrium model no longer explains the phenomenon. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's account of nutrient sources provides a starting point for examining possible inputs.

Students could begin with a published water-quality dataset that specifies location, collection date, units, analytical method, and the chemical species measured. Ammonia or ammonium, nitrate, and total nitrogen are different measurements. Their values cannot be substituted for one another without a defensible chemical and analytical rationale. Speciation of dissolved ammonia is influenced by pH and temperature, while nitrate has different transport and transformation pathways. A single concentration also cannot establish a source, an exposure history, or a health consequence.

The choice of dataset depends on the question students decide to investigate. A comparison across sites needs sampling dates, methods, units, and information about possible upstream inputs. A question about a source requires additional evidence about transport and chemical transformations. In either case, I would want students to decide what the available measurements can support before beginning to explain differences between locations.

QuestionEvidence neededClaim the evidence would not justify alone
What is measured?Named nitrogen species, concentration units, analytical procedure, detection limit, and sampling record.That different nitrogen measurements represent an interchangeable quantity.
Does concentration vary?Comparable observations across sites or times, with sampling conditions and uncertainty documented.That one difference identifies a responsible source.
What processes might explain it?Information about nitrogen inputs, chemical transformations, transport, and relevant environmental conditions.That the Haber equilibrium expression predicts concentration in a waterway.

Selecting an investigative question

Once students identify suitable records, I would ask them to distinguish the observed pattern from their interpretation of it. They could examine a competing explanation and identify one further measurement that might help. Their responses would guide the next task, whether that meant revisiting the analyte and units, comparing sampling conditions, or evaluating possible sources.

The investigation also raises a question about who decides what is worth measuring. In a justice-centred advanced chemistry class, students worked with a concern identified in their community (Morales-Doyle, 2017). That account makes me attentive to how a question is chosen and with whom findings are shared. My proposed source audit does not involve a comparable community partnership. If a class were to pursue a community concern, the people affected would need a part in shaping the investigation and deciding how its results were communicated.

Using public water-quality data

Verified data source and proposed source-selection exercise. No site-level concentrations have been invented or extracted for this page.

The Water Quality Portal, a cooperative data service involving the United States Geological Survey, the Environmental Protection Agency, and the National Water Quality Monitoring Council, provides downloadable water-quality observations from multiple monitoring programs. This is a possible starting point for a real data investigation. Because records originate in different programs, students would need to check sampling methods and metadata before comparing values.

To prepare a usable subset, I would first select one named nitrogen analyte, one result fraction and unit, a defined collection period, and stations with adequate location and sampling metadata. The resulting download would need to retain site identifiers, dates, detection or reporting limits where available, and information about the analytical method. I would inspect missing values and qualifiers before treating a numerical result as comparable. A map of observations does not explain their sources, and proximity to an activity does not alone establish causation.

Student-facing source audit · Before making a claim

Use the portal documentation to identify the fields that would allow comparison of nitrate measurements from two locations. Record the analyte name, fraction, units, collection dates, methods, and any quality qualifiers. Explain one comparison the selected records might support and two reasons why the records might remain incomparable. Propose one additional observation that could help evaluate a possible source of nitrogen.

This exercise names a real public data source, but it does not assert that a comparable dataset has already been selected or analyzed. A future classroom dataset would need a reproducible query, retained metadata, and a checked download.

Data retrieval and screening exercise

Teacher preparation protocol · A real data service, with the site-specific nitrate observations still to be extracted and checked.

For an initial retrieval trial, I would use the US Geological Survey discrete sample download. The USGS data retrieval tutorial demonstrates the site identifier USGS-04183500 using a phosphorus characteristic. The tutorial does not demonstrate nitrate availability at this station. The nitrate investigation must begin with a separate site-by-characteristic availability check and must stop if the data are unsuitable. The USGS site results summary is a documented way to begin checking available characteristics, although I have not retrieved its current response.

Retrieval procedure

  1. Open the USGS discrete sample download and enter USGS-04183500 as the monitoring location. Inspect the site results summary and identify whether nitrate is actually present. Record the site name, agency identifier, query URL, retrieval date, and the available characteristic labels. Do not proceed to a nitrate trend plot without confirming relevant records.
  2. Check the exact nitrate characteristic and its chemical reporting basis, such as nitrate reported as nitrogen, only if offered. If the station has no comparable nitrate results, preserve the negative availability result as part of the audit, then find another monitored location by filtering for the specified characteristic in the USGS interface. Record the replacement identifier and repeat the availability check. Do not substitute phosphorus, total nitrogen, or ammonia measurements.
  3. Restrict the date range to 2020-01-01 through 2024-12-31 for this exercise. Download the Full physical chemical CSV profile, retaining original column names and metadata. A historical window is used so that a repeat query has a stable stated boundary; records may still be revised.
  4. Retain collection date, monitoring location, analyte name, result value, units, fraction or basis, detection qualification, and laboratory method where available. Record missing fields. Exclude results that cannot be compared on a common analyte definition and basis, explaining the exclusions.
  5. Make a simple date-ordered plot only after screening. Mark non-detects or censored results distinctly and do not silently assign them a concentration of zero. Preserve the raw file separately from any cleaned extract, and report the number of records retained and excluded.

The student question would be modest. What temporal variation is visible in the screened measurements at this monitoring location, and what can reasonably be inferred about that variation? A single-station series cannot isolate an upstream source or represent the exposure of a whole community. Seasonal patterns would need adequate sampling across years and appropriate consideration of streamflow and sampling conditions.

Assessment after retrieval

Provide your screened data table, a date-ordered visualization, and a statement of one pattern the observations support. Then identify one possible sampling artefact and one additional kind of evidence needed to examine its cause. Explain whether the dataset supports a claim about a specific source of nitrogen. Cite the query and retrieval date so that a reader can repeat the analysis.

The USGS tutorial demonstrates this monitoring site with phosphorus data. Nitrate availability for the selected period has not been verified, and the current API response was inaccessible in this review. No measured concentrations, record counts, trends, or community impacts are reported here. This remains a source-discovery and screening exercise until a nitrate CSV has been retrieved and audited.

Assessment across representations

Students could begin with an annotated particulate diagram, discuss what it represents, and then complete an individual calculation. Comparing these responses would let me examine where they connect the models. At equilibrium, the particulate account should show forward and reverse processes continuing at equal rates; the balanced equation specifies stoichiometric relationships; and Qp = Kp identifies an equilibrium condition. None of these establishes a numerical reaction rate alone.

In the compression exercise, placing the immediate pressure change and later reaction response on one diagram could help students follow their sequence. They would then write an individual explanation of what changes first, what remains constant, and why composition subsequently adjusts. I could use differences between these accounts to decide which relationship needed another explanation or representation.

For the environmental extension, students could examine the reliability or relevance of a dataset before using it to make a claim. Their objections and proposed alternatives would be part of the scientific decision about whether the evidence is suitable.

These are proposed ways of organizing the lessons. Evaluating them would require students' actual work, including how they respond to feedback and whether their explanations become more defensible.
