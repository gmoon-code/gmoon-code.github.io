AP Chemistry Unit 7 · Original instructional design, prepared for this website

Chemical equilibrium and molecular explanations

Comparing the reaction quotient with the equilibrium constant allows students to predict the net direction of a reaction as a mixture approaches equilibrium. That prediction leaves further questions about how quickly the composition changes and what the forward and reverse reactions are doing. I developed this AP Chemistry sequence around those distinctions, using an ammonia equilibrium problem and a compression example to connect symbolic calculations with particulate explanations.

The three proposed meetings build from an explanation of dynamic equilibrium to the calculation and interpretation of a reaction quotient. The final meeting asks students to distinguish the immediate pressure change during compression from the later adjustment in composition. The worked values and diagnostic responses are constructed examples, and this sequence has not been evaluated in a classroom.

Reaction quotient and equilibrium direction

Consider ammonia formation from nitrogen and hydrogen. In AP Chemistry Unit 7, students calculate the reaction quotient, compare it with the equilibrium constant, and predict the system's net response to a disturbance. The challenge is to connect that prediction with an account of the forward and reverse molecular processes.

Balanced gas-phase reaction

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)

Qp = PNH32 / (PN2 × PH23)

For an illustrative mixture at a fixed temperature, suppose the initial partial pressures are 1.0 atm of N2, 2.0 atm of H2, and 1.0 atm of NH3. Let Kp = 0.50 at this hypothetical temperature.

First interpretation

Qp = 1.02 / (1.0 × 2.03) = 0.125

Here, Qp is below Kp. The reaction therefore has a net tendency toward the products as the mixture approaches equilibrium at the same temperature.

Comparing Qp with Kp predicts the net direction of compositional change. To explain how the mixture approaches equilibrium, students must also consider the ongoing forward and reverse reactions and the difference between their rates at a given moment.

The initial pressures and Kp value are invented solely for this mathematical exercise. They are not industrial measurements or data gathered from students.

Macroscopic, particulate, and symbolic accounts

Observable or measurable

Macroscopic evidence

What would a sensor, sample analysis, or pressure measurement show as composition changes? Measurements make a change observable, but the explanation requires an account of the underlying particles and processes.

Molecules and interactions

Particulate reasoning

Forward and reverse reactions continue to occur. At equilibrium their rates are equal, even though nitrogen, hydrogen, and ammonia can remain present in different amounts. “Equilibrium” does not imply equal quantities of each gas.

Equations and comparison

Symbolic reasoning

Qp is constructed from the current partial pressures. Kp applies at a specified temperature. Comparing them gives the net direction required to approach equilibrium under those conditions.

A measurement can establish a change in composition, while a particulate model depicts molecular events and an equation expresses quantitative relationships. These accounts offer different information that students need to connect (Johnstone, 1991; Talanquer, 2011). I would examine whether an explanation uses the comparison between Qp and Kp to reason about the mixture, while recognizing that the comparison does not determine reaction speed.

If a particulate drawing shows more NH3 after a disturbance, I would want to know how the student connects that change to the continuing forward and reverse reactions. Asking them to compare Qp with Kp could help clarify the account, just as a student who calculates the quotient correctly may still need to explain the net direction.

Compression of an equilibrium mixture

For this second example, begin with an equilibrium mixture at the same hypothetical temperature. Suppose PN2 = 1.0 atm, PH2 = 2.0 atm, and PNH3 = 2.0 atm. The initial Qp is 4.0 / (1.0 × 8.0) = 0.50, matching Kp. If the container volume is then halved at constant temperature, the ideal-gas approximation predicts that all three partial pressures initially double before reaction changes the composition.

Qp,new = (4.0)2 / [(2.0) × (4.0)3] = 0.125

The new value of Qp is one-quarter of Kp, so the mixture has a net tendency to form more ammonia as it re-equilibrates. The immediate doubling of all partial pressures and the later change in composition occur at different stages. At constant temperature, compression changes Qp, while Kp remains fixed.

This separate example begins at equilibrium. Its pressure calculation assumes ideal-gas behaviour, an instantaneous volume change, and no intervening reaction during compression. All stated pressure values are illustrative.

The proposed tasks connect numerical predictions with particulate explanations in progressively more demanding situations. In the compression problem, students must distinguish the immediate increase in partial pressures from the mixture's later response. Their accounts of those two stages would help me decide whether to revisit the calculation, the model of continuing reactions, or the connection between them.

Interpreting possible student responses

These are invented diagnostic examples, not quotations from students or measured misunderstandings in my AP Chemistry class.

Hypothetical response

“At equilibrium, all concentrations are equal.”

The response confuses equal reaction rates with equal amounts. I would ask for a particle-level explanation showing two reactions continuing at equal rates while the amounts of species remain unequal.

Hypothetical response

“Pressure increases, so K increases.”

The response confuses the reaction quotient's immediate change with the equilibrium constant. I would ask students to calculate Qp before and after compression at the same temperature and explain what controls Kp.

Hypothetical response

“Compression shifts the reaction right because the product side has fewer gas molecules.”

Comparing the numbers of gas molecules on either side correctly predicts the direction for this particular compression at constant temperature. I would ask the student to calculate the immediate Qp and compare it with Kp as a second way to justify that prediction. The original argument is scientifically valid; the follow-up examines whether the student can connect it with the quantitative model.

The first two responses contain scientific errors, while the third gives a defensible explanation for the stated conditions. I would use the first two to revisit a mistaken relationship and the third to ask for another form of justification. The distinction affects the purpose of the feedback, as well as the question a student is asked next (Black & Wiliam, 2009).

Independent equilibrium task

A constructed equilibrium example checking whether a numerical result is supported by an explanation of reaction rates. It has not been administered as part of the documented classroom record.

Student-facing task · Constant temperature, ideal-gas approximation

For N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), the equilibrium constant Kp is 0.50 at a fixed hypothetical temperature. A mixture has PN2 = 2.0 atm, PH2 = 1.0 atm, and PNH3 = 1.0 atm. Calculate Qp and predict the initial net direction of reaction. Describe the relationship between the forward and reverse reaction rates at the equilibrium represented by these values. Explain why the numerical relationship between Qp and Kp does not establish a reaction rate.

Worked explanation and feedback

Using the course-level partial-pressure expression, Qp = (1.0)2/[(2.0)(1.0)3] = 0.50. The mixture is already at equilibrium under the stated conditions, so no net composition change is predicted. The forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates, although those rates cannot be calculated from Qp or Kp alone. A correct equality is therefore a starting point for checking the explanation of dynamic equilibrium.

If a student concludes that both reactions have stopped, I would ask them to account for an unchanged composition alongside continuing molecular events. A claim that all partial pressures must be equal calls for attention to the equilibrium expression, while a question about speed would require kinetic evidence.

Transfer task after expansion

Newly authored reasoning check. This reverses the volume change in the worked compression example, so students must reconsider the reaction direction rather than repeat its calculation.

Student-facing reasoning check

An ideal-gas equilibrium mixture of N2, H2, and NH3 starts at the same equilibrium pressures as the earlier compression example. Instead of compressing the mixture, the container volume is rapidly doubled at constant temperature. Explain (a) what happens to each partial pressure immediately, before the reaction proceeds; (b) how the new Qp compares with Kp; and (c) why the subsequent net reaction differs from the compression case. Would a catalyst change the final equilibrium composition at this temperature and volume? Justify your answer.

Criteria for a complete explanation

Immediately after idealized expansion, the initial equilibrium pressures of 1.0, 2.0, and 2.0 atm fall to 0.50, 1.0, and 1.0 atm, respectively. The new reaction quotient is Qp = (1.0)2/[(0.50)(1.0)3] = 2.0, above the unchanged Kp of 0.50. The net reaction therefore favours nitrogen and hydrogen as the mixture approaches its new equilibrium. These immediately halved pressures are not the final equilibrium pressures, since the ensuing reaction changes the composition again. A catalyst may change how quickly equilibrium is reached, but not its position under the same temperature and volume conditions.

Expansion favours the side with more gas molecules in this example, providing a qualitative prediction that students can compare with their Qp calculation. I would examine whether they can explain why expansion changes Qp in the opposite direction from compression while leaving Kp unchanged. A response that uses the familiar rule correctly without separating the immediate physical change from the subsequent chemical change would still invite a question about that sequence.

Assessment across representations

After calculating Qp for the initial mixture, students could annotate a particulate representation to show how they understand continued forward and reverse reactions. Read alongside the written interpretation, the diagram could help me examine whether a correct calculation was accompanied by an account of continuing reactions. Each account would be examined for what it actually expresses; a diagram would not be treated as a direct measurement of individual reaction events.

Students could use the same three-state representation for compression and expansion, marking the starting equilibrium, the immediate pressure change, and the later change in composition. The compression and expansion cases ask students to distinguish the immediate pressure change from the subsequent chemical response as the reaction quotient moves back toward the equilibrium constant. If a response treats the immediately changed pressures as final equilibrium values, I would ask what continues to happen after that instant and which measurements would be needed to describe the final state.
