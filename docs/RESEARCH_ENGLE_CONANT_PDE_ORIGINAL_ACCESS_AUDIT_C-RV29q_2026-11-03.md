## Publisher and study identity

The original [Taylor & Francis publisher abstract and DOI record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S1532690XCI2004_1), [2002 journal issue contents](https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/hcgi20/20/4), [ERIC EJ665018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ665018) and [JSTOR original journal issue](https://www.jstor.org/stable/i362256) establish:

- **Randi A. Engle and Faith R. Conant** (2002), “Guiding principles for fostering productive disciplinary engagement: Explaining an emergent argument in a community of learners classroom”, *Cognition and Instruction* **20(4), 399–483**, DOI **10.1207/S1532690XCI2004_1**.
- Taylor & Francis currently displays a **7 June 2010 online platform publication date** for this original *2002* issue. That later online listing is **not an error in the site's 2002 citation**.
- Original case concerned **fifth-graders' sustained classification controversy** during an endangered-species unit in a **Fostering Communities of Learners** environment. It was an analytic case of one student-group debate and an attempt to interpret two other published cases using the same design framework, rather than a randomized comparison or demonstration of outcomes for secondary-school biology students.
- The four principles described in the original abstract are **problematizing subject matter**, giving students authority to address legitimate problems, **accountability to other people and shared disciplinary norms for reasoning/evidence**, and **access to adequate material, informational and interactional resources**. Authority is therefore accompanied by collective and disciplinary responsibility. It is not simply unstructured free choice or teachers withdrawing judgment.
- According to the authors' abstract, the classification dispute became sustained and intellectually productive as students advanced arguments, drew on evidence, questioned taxonomic classifications, and had opportunities to contribute to decisions about their problem. The authors distinguish a contextual explanatory account of engagement from a universally effective intervention.

The website reports only a narrower part of these connected principles, focusing on student authority, responsibility to peers and disciplinary resources. It does **not** suggest that these are the complete four principles, that children always exercise epistemic authority equally, or that autonomy alone causes achievement gains. A four-item list inside this already differentiated paragraph would obscure its comparison with Barrett's teacher study and Puvirajah's robotics setting. No revision is justified merely to enumerate the complete framework.

## Stanford original-record derivative and its provenance

The [Stanford Graduate School of Education teaching case “Learning Moments” and the Nature of Student Interactions in the Orca Controversy Discussions](https://caselib.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/cases/LearningMomentsCase.pdf) is explicitly identified as **Case 2007-01, dated 2 May 2007**, prepared by **David Diehl** under Daniel A. McFarland's guidance and funding. Its first-page copyright statement explains that it was made for teaching and research, and **does not identify the 2007 case as the Engle and Conant 2002 journal article or author-accepted manuscript**. The source is instead a secondary teaching case discussing, quoting, and compiling original study material.

Its account, sourced to Engle and Conant (2002) and later Engle work, describes two Grade 5 Fostering Communities of Learners classrooms in the San Francisco Bay Area, their endangered-species unit, research groups focused on whales, and **eight recorded occasions** when students contested whether orcas belonged with whales or dolphins. The controversy began following statements by a marine-animal trainer on a class visit, and continued as students worked with library sources and shared their arguments. The case describes **two sets of five named students** in related whale groups, with the focal group's debate recurring across sessions. This detail supports the original publisher abstract's description of a specific sustained controversy. However, the number of students mentioned in this **case** is **not** a representative sample size to which one can generalize causal learning outcomes.

The Stanford case's **Appendix A and Appendix B** explicitly adapt timeline/argument descriptions from Engle and Conant printed pp. **411–414**. **Appendix C** identifies and reproduces a transcript originally published as Engle and Conant printed **pp. 465–473**. The web text extractor exposed these reproduced original data records, which help contextualize why accountability, argumentation and access to evidence appeared together. **These borrowed archival sections alone do not establish that the original 2002 paper's complete methods, case analysis, comparisons or authorial interpretation were read.** Stanford's 2007 teaching commentary and licensing restrictions are also different from the original journal's. Therefore this derivative source is **corroboration of original case context**, not an original full-article substitute.
