## Original methods, population and inference boundary

- The authors studied **two experienced US middle-school teachers** who attended a 2018 phenomenon-based science curriculum design workshop. Both had more than ten years of teaching. **Nadine taught Grade 7** in a suburban Midwestern school; **Samantha taught Grade 8** in a rural Midwestern school. **Six teachers initially agreed** to participate, but these two completed the complete point-of-view data collection required for analysis. Their purposive recruitment from a curriculum co-design programme constrains transfer to teachers who were not similarly prepared or supported.
- The researchers gathered point-of-view video from **wearable teacher cameras**, stationary classroom recordings and post-lesson reflective interviews. Nadine was observed for **eight class periods** across two units, and Samantha for **six periods** across one unit. A wrist remote allowed each teacher to tag decisions about opening/closing spaces for students' epistemic agency. There were **seven tagged moments for Nadine and six for Samantha**, making **13 analysed teacher-identified moments**.
- **Crucial sampling caution:** These **13 tags do not measure the overall frequency** with which student-epistemic-agency opportunities arose. The paper explicitly explains that the counts show what teachers themselves noticed and judged consequential to tag. Nadine had difficulty identifying unplanned moments because collaborative sensemaking was already central to her teaching. Samantha experienced technical difficulties with the tagging device, so **only two of her six observed periods yielded tagged moments**. The study's effectiveness evaluations are situated, interpretive judgments using subsequent classroom interaction and interview records, not randomized outcome measures.
- The analysis differentiated what teachers attended to, including disciplinary substance, the epistemic nature of contributions, students' epistemic stance and engagement. It characterized responses as **amplifying/connecting**, **redirecting/resetting** and **reflective**, examining whether the subsequent episode appeared to enlarge students' opportunities to pursue and evaluate ideas. Nadine and Samantha did not interpret or use all decision points similarly. The authors explicitly frame the two-teacher study as a **proof of concept for their point-of-view noticing method**, calling for more varied teacher populations and comparisons with different age groups.
- An illustrative Grade 7 discussion around evaporation and a student idea about the sun became a class question when Nadine invited peers to connect and challenge the idea. In other episodes, teacher questions returned discussion toward the expected response or a predesigned task, including an episode involving mitosis models. The Grade 8 class explored heat transfer with design work involving a baby incubator and sometimes reconsidered conflicting experimental evidence. The different teacher responses offer situated evidence of decision-making, not general proof that increasing critique always redistributes epistemic agency.

## Exact Research sentence and writing evaluation

The public section writes:

> Curricular planning can make room for students to influence the methods of investigation and the models through which a class develops an explanation (Ko & Krist, 2019). Once the lesson begins, those opportunities depend on what teachers notice in students' contributions and how they respond. Studies of teachers' noticing and classroom discourse show how a student's contribution can become a question for collective inquiry or be treated primarily as an answer to evaluate (Krist et al., 2023; Stroupe, 2014). The teacher can acknowledge a contribution while retaining control over which questions the class pursues.

This is a proportionate synthesis of **two different study designs**. The original Krist study explores teachers' noticing and decisions from their own tagged teaching moments, whereas Stroupe (2014) examines five first-year teachers, valued public/private contributions and cognitive authority in science practice communities. Both contribute to a question about what happens to students' ideas after the lesson begins. The website does not attribute Stroupe's five classrooms to Krist, suggest the co-designed curriculum project was a controlled trial, or generalize the two experienced teachers' responses to every science classroom. It also avoids claiming that teachers' acknowledgement guarantees shared authority.

The hypothetical cell-signalling question preceding the paragraph illustrates a scientific issue that *could* alter teaching, rather than being falsely presented as an observed episode from Krist or Stroupe. The following paragraph moves to scaffolds and material resources, a distinct aspect of decision-making. In continuous reading, the transition from **plans**, through **teacher interpretation**, to **the boundaries of intellectual authority**, and then to **disciplinary support** remains varied and conceptually motivated.
