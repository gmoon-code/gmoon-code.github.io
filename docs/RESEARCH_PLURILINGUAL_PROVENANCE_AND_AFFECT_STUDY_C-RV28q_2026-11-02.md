## Prasad (2015) | Abstract and original study-level records, no full-text assertion

The [original Taylor & Francis publisher abstract and article record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14675986.2015.1109775) directly describes **five school-based cases, four in Toronto and one in Montpellier**, with children acting as co-researchers. Its unit of interpretation is the **iterative plurilingual multimodal identity-book creation process**, focusing on **one identity text from each of the five cases**, alongside children's *research conversations* about their creations and interviews with classroom teachers and parents. This is **collaborative ethnographic action research**, not a causal classroom trial or a study of secondary science explanations.

The original journal issue is **volume 26, issue 6, year 2015**, although the paper was first posted online **1 February 2016**. ### Methods and inferential scope

The original **two samples were each 30 learners**.

- **Group (i)**: 30 **non-science undergraduates** at a higher-education institution in southwestern England, sampled across programmes outside traditional science. Researchers conducted **Interviews About Scenarios**, using illustrated radioactivity situations to elicit understandings of radiation, risks and participants' emotional expressions.
- **Group (ii)**: 30 **A-level physics students** in two school sixth-form classes. Under tighter instructional time constraints, researchers used **verbal questionnaires** with some probing to record their thoughts about radioactivity, perceived risks and feelings about its learning.
  
The authors expressly caution that these groups differ in age, subject specialization and gender composition, and the **two questionnaires were not identical**. They did **not observe students in a learning intervention** or measure subsequent concept improvement. They interpreted descriptive response patterns, including the mix of conditionality, alarm and emotional expression; the conclusions are exploratory and do not permit attributing differences only to physics expertise.

### Identified factual issue and actual public correction

Previous public `research.html#cultural-knowledge`:

> In a separate study, two groups of physics learners approached radioactivity in ways that the researchers examined in relation to their feelings about the subject (Alsop & Watts, 2000).

The phrase **“two groups of physics learners”** wrongly suggests both participant samples studied physics. One group comprised **non-science university students**; only the second was studying A-level physics. This misidentification is material to a source used to think about feelings, disciplinary explanation and classroom interpretation.

**Corrected public wording, committed:**

> A study comparing non-science undergraduates with A-level physics students examined how their feelings about radioactivity appeared in their accounts of the subject (Alsop & Watts, 2000).

HTML anchor `<a href="#reading-alsop">Alsop &amp; Watts, 2000</a>` was preserved. The change names the two learner populations accurately, avoids a causal claim about emotion or learning, and retains the author's own gentle, precise voice.
