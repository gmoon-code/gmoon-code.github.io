## Original publication evidence and page-claim crosswalk

**Tang, Delgado & Moje (2014)**. [Original *Science Education* article, DOI 10.1002/sce.21099](https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.21099), 98(2), 305–326. This is an **integrative conceptual/analytic framework**, illustrated using middle-school nanoscience episodes. The authors distinguish changes and re-representation **across** multiple representations over a typically longer time scale, from the finer-grained **integration of modal elements within a representation**, such as written labels, symbols, gestures and visual details. Their two analytic dimensions are **timescale** and **compositional grain size**. The site's sentence, “An analytic framework distinguishes changes across representations from the ways learners combine words, gestures, and visual elements within a representation,” is **accurate and appropriately concise**. It does not misrepresent the paper as a controlled demonstration of improved learning.

**Suárez (2020)**. [Original fully accessible *Science Education* article, DOI 10.1002/sce.21588](https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.21588), 104(5), 791–826, originally published online June 2020. The study involved an **out-of-school, library-partnered elementary programme** examining electrical flow and resistance. Twelve children participated across three iterations, with detailed analysis of a smaller group in the described translanguaging episodes. Participants communicated as they developed models of electrical circuits and resistive elements, drawing on Spanish/English linguistic resources, gesture and physical/diagrammatic representations. The author was also the instructor and analysed how their own communication choices signalled the acceptability of different language resources. The site's claim about **elementary age, out-of-school context, models, peers/instructor, and pedagogical moves** is faithful. A singular short classroom vignette elsewhere on the site should not be mistaken for data from this library programme.

**Ünsal, Jakobson, Molander & Wickman (2018)**. [Original open-access *Research in Science Education* article, DOI 10.1007/s11165-016-9597-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11165-016-9597-8), volume 48, 1027–1048, initially online April 2017, final issue October 2018. This is the **multilingual classroom language-use study**, involving **elementary science instruction about electricity** in Swedish, with teacher-led discussions and small-group activities. Students' language resources shaped how they explained, argued and collaborated differently in these settings; access to minority languages could support group sensemaking but did not automatically remove all language barriers. The site cautiously says language resources used **differed between teacher-led and small-group activities**, which is an accurate inference. It avoids suggesting either setting was universally successful.

**Important citation-identity distinction:** Another paper by some of the same authors is [Ünsal et al. (2018), *Gesticulating science: Emergent bilingual students' use of gestures*](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.21415), *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* 55, 121–144, including **Grade 3 electricity and Grade 7 acids and bases**. The website's `#reading-unsal` entry correctly links the **language-use study** (DOI 10.1007/s11165-016-9597-8), and its claim about classroom interaction settings belongs to that study. Do not replace the citation with the similar gesture article.
