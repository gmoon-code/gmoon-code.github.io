## Engle and Conant (2002) | Publisher/ERIC original abstract only, remain A

**Randi A. Engle and Faith R. Conant**, “Guiding Principles for Fostering Productive Disciplinary Engagement: Explaining an Emergent Argument in a Community of Learners Classroom”, *Cognition and Instruction* **20(4), 399–483 (2002)**, DOI `10.1207/S1532690XCI2004_1`.

The original [Taylor & Francis journal abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S1532690XCI2004_1), [2002 issue index](https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/hcgi20/20/4) and [ERIC record EJ665018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ665018) verify bibliographic identity, population and four stated principles. The publisher notes a **2010 electronic platform posting**, which does **not** supersede the **2002 volume/issue year** in the published site's citation.

The original summary identifies **one focal group of fifth-grade students** in an endangered-species research unit in a **Fostering Communities of Learners classroom**. Their sustained dispute concerned whether **orcas should be classified as whales or dolphins**. The model principles involve (1) **problematizing** subject matter, (2) granting students authority to address a problem, (3) requiring accountability to other people and disciplinary evidence norms, and (4) giving students the resources needed to sustain inquiry. The paper uses this group and discusses two other published cases when considering possible transfer; it is **not** a randomized trial, nor a claim that student autonomy without disciplinary accountability is sufficient.

The website currently writes:

> Productive disciplinary engagement provides a further account of how student authority can develop alongside responsibility to peers and access to disciplinary resources (Engle & Conant, 2002).

This accurately reflects the jointly necessary principles and uses “can develop” rather than claiming that this happened as a measured outcome in secondary biology. The sentence follows a distinct robotics competition example of situated negotiated authority and contrasts with the preceding longitudinal teaching-practice account. It does not misidentify the focal participants or conflate informal robotics and Grade 5 endangered-species inquiry. **No public prose edit warranted.**

Attempts to locate the complete original Engle–Conant 85-page article through direct article search, author/university copies, research-sharing platforms and publisher links did **not** produce a verifiably accessible original full text. ResearchGate currently offered **Request full text**; other available documents primarily *cited or summarized* the 2002 paper, which is insufficient for F. **Engle and Conant row 20 remains A.**

## Derry et al. (2010) | Complete original co-author-hosted article and verified PDF title page, upgrade F

**Sharon J. Derry, Roy D. Pea, Brigid Barron, Randi A. Engle, Frederick Erickson, Ricki Goldman, Rogers Hall, Timothy Koschmann, Jay L. Lemke, Miriam Gamoran Sherin and Bruce L. Sherin**, “Conducting Video Research in the Learning Sciences: Guidance on Selection, Analysis, Technology, and Ethics”, *Journal of the Learning Sciences*, **19(1), 3–53 (2010)**, DOI `10.1080/10508400903452884`.

The complete original journal-formatted scholarly PDF was opened at [co-author Roy Pea's Stanford academic website](https://web.stanford.edu/~roypea/RoyPDF%20folder/A149_Derry_Pea_Barron_etal_JLS_VideoGuidelines.pdf). **The journal article title page (zero-index PDF page 1) was successfully screenshot-inspected**, confirming publisher, full title, original author order, source identity, 2010 volume/pages and DOI. The [first-party Taylor & Francis issue record](https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/hlns20/19/1) and [ERIC EJ880347](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ880347) independently agree. Substantive original text was examined across the introduction and methodological framework, the selection and analytic procedure sections, the technology/sharing and ethical guidance, and the concluding material. The source is available as original research-methods **guidelines from expert synthesis**, not a student-learning experiment or a modern statement of every relevant current jurisdiction's regulations.

**Selection and interpretation.** The authors distinguish sampling video clips as **representative data** from choosing illustrations for **narrative or expressive purposes**; the two may be productively combined when made explicit. Researchers must document what was recorded, how episodes were sampled and how analytic frameworks/units of time were selected. A short clip may support discussion of a specific interaction, while a conclusion about recurring classroom practices requires an appropriately sampled corpus and supporting context. Video cannot automatically tell a researcher what students were thinking or which interpretations they rejected in unrecorded settings.

**Ethical scope.** Original video of children is not intrinsically anonymous, and publishing or reusing identifiable recording presents different considerations from restricted confidential analysis. The authors discuss informed and voluntary participation, the importance of minors' guardians and independent institutional/school requirements, restrictions on dissemination and the need to describe who may access recordings. Their detailed legal framework concerns the United States as at 2010, so the website should **not** imply that this source independently establishes present-day South Korean consent requirements or replaces a particular local ethics review.

**Original Research wording.** In `research.html#cultural-knowledge`, the website writes:

> The researcher's interpretation brings its own assumptions into view. Labelling one contribution as everyday knowledge and another as scientific reasoning may overlook how a student connected experience with an emerging explanation. What can be known also depends on which interactions are recorded, how episodes are selected, and whose account informs the interpretation. Guidance for video research examines these practical and ethical decisions (Derry et al., 2010), while the concept of situated knowledge makes the partial perspective of any account an explicit concern (Haraway, 1988). Those decisions affect how confidently I can move from a recorded contribution to a claim about a student's reasoning.

This accurately connects evidence selection and analytic interpretation with bounded inference. It avoids saying that the author has already implemented a video study, that particular students were actually filmed, or that consent is legally satisfied by following this publication. The conceptual bridge to Haraway is appropriately separate from Derry’s practical methodological guidance. The following section's hypothetical Korean/English science-discussion example illustrates the consequence of missing conversational evidence without pretending to reconstruct a student's private reasoning from an English-only final answer.

The five adjacent sentences move from **interpretive classification** to **selection and source dependence**, then to methodological guidance and **limits on inference**. The paragraph's variable sentence constructions and meaningful progression match the established gentle, precise, Canadian-English academic voice, with no artificial connector or claim of unwarranted personal transformation. **No public prose revision warranted.**
