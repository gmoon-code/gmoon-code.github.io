# C-RV29cw | Unit 2 chytridiomycosis prompt and dehydration premise

**Date:** 3 November 2026. **Continued from:** [C-RV29cv](BENTAZON_QB_SITE_TEACHING_WORDING_AUDIT_C-RV29cv_2026-11-03.md). **Scope:** The author-retained edited Unit 2 assessment's amphibian skin sodium-uptake/dehydration passage, original amphibian chytridiomycosis pathology literature and all current public site descriptions mentioning that Unit 2 mechanism. **Decision:** Existing `research-action.html` explicitly frames dehydration as a **proposed assessment pathway**, separately reporting original work finding electrolyte depletion, cardiac dysfunction and hydration measures unchanged. No new website change is supported.

## Evidence examined

The private **`Unit 2 Test VA - Edited.pdf`**, page 5, contains a multi-part free response about amphibian skin. Its relevant two-point **subpart (b)** asks how chytrid-associated sodium-uptake disruption could lead to dehydration, with a preceding passage asserting electrolyte imbalance causes dehydration. The form remains named “Edited” and does **not independently establish the actual 28 students' historically administered version**.

Public original research:
- [Voyles et al. (2007)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17972752/), *Electrolyte depletion and osmotic imbalance in amphibians with chytridiomycosis*, *Diseases of Aquatic Organisms*, 77, 113–118, DOI **10.3354/dao01838**. Its original paper abstract reports depleted plasma sodium, potassium, magnesium and chloride, reduced osmolality, and stable albumin, hematocrit and urea markers, which the authors interpreted as **no measurable change in hydration status** in severely affected green tree frogs.
- [Voyles et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19900897/), *Pathogenesis of chytridiomycosis, a cause of catastrophic amphibian declines*, *Science*, 326, 582–585, DOI **10.1126/science.1176765**. The original article's abstract reports **more than 50% inhibition** of epidermal electrolyte transport, approximately **20% lower plasma sodium** and **50% lower plasma potassium** in diseased animals, and associated asystolic cardiac arrest.
- [*Frog skin epithelium: electrolyte transport and chytridiomycosis*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3288393/), an open-access mechanistic review, explains epithelial Na⁺ channels and Na⁺/K⁺ transport roles, while emphasizing that species and environmental conditions affect susceptibility and responses.

These sources support disrupted ion uptake and electrolyte homeostasis in severely affected susceptible amphibians. They **do not establish a universal primary pathway from Bd infection through dehydration to death**. In particular, the 2007 original observations explicitly caution against assuming altered blood electrolytes directly demonstrate a changed hydration state.

## Exact public text and cross-page check

Re-fetched `research-action.html` (**blob `80277bba0aa0ad715be87865e5a30e609b419147`**) and `teaching-ap-biology.html`, `research.html`, `index.html` and `teaching.html`. A targeted search for chytrid, amphibian, sodium uptake, dehydration, electrolytes and *Batrachochytrium* found two substantive locations, **both on `research-action.html`**.

First, the study sequence explains that the Unit 2 **question asked students to explain a proposed pathway** from chytrid-associated sodium transport impairment to dehydration. This is a representation of the classroom prompt, not a separate claim that dehydration was established biologically.

Second, the Research Action limitations paragraph explicitly says that **studies of severe infection documented electrolyte depletion and cardiac dysfunction, and one reported hydration measures unchanged**, with links to both Voyles original studies. This preserves the crucial distinction between a student-facing task's scientific premise and physiological evidence, and it avoids turning a potentially misleading assessment assertion into a generalized biological claim.

The page retains the methodological limitation that the three assessment questions involve **different mechanisms**, so cross-unit classification change does not establish a common-scale mechanistic learning gain. The question and its source caveat serve different purposes and are appropriate in their respective sections.

## Editorial disposition

**No academic HTML edited.** A corrective rewrite claiming Bd chiefly causes dehydration would weaken accuracy. Removing the word *dehydration* everywhere would also obscure the actual Unit 2 question students reportedly addressed. The present text accurately labels it as a proposed assessment pathway and qualifies the biological evidence in the limitations discussion. That is the appropriate source-conscious distinction for prospective PhD readers.

The private edited test's assertive lead-in should be handled cautiously in any future classroom reuse, preferably with wording centred on electrolyte loss/osmotic consequences and an explicit distinction from hydration changes, once the instructional objective and species model are specified. This is a **recommendation about a potential future teaching instrument**, not an alteration of historically administered materials.
