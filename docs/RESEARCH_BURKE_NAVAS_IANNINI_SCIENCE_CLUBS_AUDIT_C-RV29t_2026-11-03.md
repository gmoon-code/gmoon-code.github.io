## First-party 2021 publication and design

The [Wiley original 2021 article record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tea.21714), [original Wiley 58(9) issue](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/toc/10982736/2021/58/9), [ERIC EJ1310767 index](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1310767), and [coauthor's Simon Fraser University faculty publication listing](https://www.sfu.ca/education/faculty-profiles/aiannini/publications/journal-articles.html) independently substantiate:

- **Lydia E. Carol-Ann Burke and Ana Maria Navas Iannini (2021)**, “Science engagement as insight into the science identity work nurtured in community-based science clubs”, *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* **58(9), 1425–1454**, DOI **10.1002/tea.21714**. The **online version of record was first published on 24 May 2021**, followed by the **November 2021** journal issue. Both are the same study, not competing publication years.
- **Population and site:** children of **late-elementary to middle-school age** from **low-income communities** in an East-Central metropolitan area of Canada, participating in **out-of-school** community science clubs. The original abstract does not justify generalizing to all children in Canada, all science clubs or secondary biology classrooms.
- **Study records:** surveys from **202 children** enrolled for at least one academic year across **21 clubs**; focus-group commentary from a **subset of 45 children** attending **14 clubs**; additional **semistructured interviews with nine club staff members**; contextual **field notes after promotional events**. The numbers are distinct sources and units, **not 202 + 45 + 9 independent child respondents**.
- **Reported pattern:** children and/or their families often entered with prior enthusiasm about science; club sessions differed from anticipated school science through **hands-on activity** and **positive relationships with club staff**. Staff and children sometimes reinforced a sharp division between club science and school science. Children **reported** improvement in their performance at school but often struggled to connect the enjoyment of club sessions to the learning expected in school science.
- **Inference limit:** the authors' claim concerns **reported emotional engagement and science-identity work**, and a difficulty connecting contexts. The abstract does **not** establish independently measured school marks, a controlled learning effect, a representative national sample, longitudinal transitions into STEM careers or the precise extent to which club identity became school identity. The authors propose strengthening **club/school/home** science connections; the proposal is distinct from a demonstrated intervention effect.

## Editorial and intellectual evaluation

The site's first sentence gives a bounded description of the children and the aspects of the club experience they valued. The second identifies the **tension in how the two science settings were understood**, directly following from the first, and accurately uses the modal **“could”** to avoid saying every participant experienced the same divide. The final sentence asks **“I wonder how”**, distinguishing the author's prospective question from an observed result. “Relationships they formed” is less specific than the abstract's “positive relationships built with club staffers”, but its broader phrasing is fully consistent with the source, and there is no evidence-backed reason to narrow it at the cost of naturalness.

The paragraph has three different sentence actions, describing an experience, interpreting a documented institutional boundary and opening a tentative research question. It avoids inflated claims about permanent science identities, causality, measured school performance, or general transfer. The preceding two paragraphs introduce different influences on a scientific explanation; the following paragraph's focus on **recognition and positioning** is a meaningful advancement rather than merely another sentence about belonging. The whole five-paragraph passage maintains the author's gentle, precise Canadian-English style without forced transitional phrases, em dashes, unsupported generalizations or mechanically repeated ending patterns. **No prose edit improves the existing evidence-to-meaning fit.** The exact existing reference is correct.
