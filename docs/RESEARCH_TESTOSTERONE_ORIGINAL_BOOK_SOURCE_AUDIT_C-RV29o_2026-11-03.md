## Original scientific and methodological warrant

**Different things measured as “testosterone.”** In *Multiple Ts* (especially original printed pp. **24–30**), the authors explain how testosterone is produced in ovaries and adrenal glands as well as testes and through peripheral conversion. Free hormone, testosterone bound to SHBG or albumin, and downstream metabolites have distinct biological and measurement implications. Blood, saliva, urine and muscle measurements are **not interchangeable**, and the timing, context and laboratory technique of collection affect interpretation. For instance, different immunoassays have particular challenges at low concentrations, while more specific mass spectrometry methods are often more resource-intensive. The authors describe how choices of which sample and chemical complex to measure are themselves part of what a study can establish.

**Tissue receptor activity and pathways.** In *Multiple Ts* (original printed **pp. 30–34**), testosterone's actions depend on other hormones, enzymes and **androgen receptor distribution/activity**, which can differ among people and across tissues within a person. The original discussion distinguishes genomic and more rapid non-genomic pathways, as well as measurement timing, variable hormone-receptor sensitivity and some uncertainty about mechanisms. The authors describe clinical observations of apparently healthy participants with hormone concentrations outside commonly used reference ranges. This is not proof that testosterone lacks physiological effects; it directly demonstrates the limited inference possible from a **single measured level** without its biological context.

**Strength, athletic performance and group versus individual inference.** In *Athleticism* (especially pp. **169–172**), the authors review studies in which endogenous testosterone's associations with strength are modified or become weak after accounting for training, body size, age, other steroids or tissue differences. Hormone conversion, receptor variability and individual developmental histories complicate attributing strength or sport performance to testosterone concentration alone. At printed **pp. 183–185**, the authors examine how excluding some women with intersex variation from an athlete dataset influences constructed hormone reference ranges, and at later pages discuss discrepancies between **group-level correlations** and heterogeneous individual responses. The analysis concerns particular historical sporting policies and published studies, **not a claim about current 2026 eligibility criteria**. Where original text discussed exogenous testosterone treatment or doping, the authors explicitly distinguish such contexts from interpreting naturally occurring concentrations.

**Limits of the original work:** This is a scholarly critical synthesis of a body of scientific literature, research histories and interviews with experts, with specific biochemical interpretations and limitations. The study-level descriptions are the authors' analysis of publications and interviews, not a new randomized clinical experiment proving an individual's behavioural response. Even where the book's synthesis is forceful, its methodological lesson is about the limits of **prediction from a concentration in isolation**. The website's wording is cautious and correctly acknowledges mediated physiological pathways instead of treating hormone measurement as biologically irrelevant.
