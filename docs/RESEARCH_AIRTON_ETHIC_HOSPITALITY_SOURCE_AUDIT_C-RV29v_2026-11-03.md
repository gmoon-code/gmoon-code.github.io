## Original Airton publication and precise source warrants

**Publication:** Lee Airton (2023), “You don’t know me: Welcoming gender diversity in schools via an ethic of hospitality,” *Curriculum Inquiry* **53(2), 148–168**, DOI **10.1080/03626784.2023.2200810**, [original publisher article/abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03626784.2023.2200810), [publisher 53(2) contents](https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/rcui20/53/2), [ERIC EJ1388811](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1388811), and [Queen's University's original faculty publication listing](https://educ.queensu.ca/people/lee-airton). The original journal identifies **first online 19 April 2023**. The publisher, ERIC and author faculty listing agree with the site's year, journal, volume, issue and page range.

The published **abstract** situates the argument in **Canadian public-school gender-identity/gender-expression discrimination policy**. Airton draws on **Claudia Ruitenberg's discussion of a Derridean ethic of hospitality** to question approaches that anticipate and identify a particular “transgender student” as the subject of teachers' policies and specific remedies. They propose preparing to welcome an unknown student **without knowing that student's gender in advance**, including where already out transgender pupils are present. The abstract explicitly names **three educational orientations**: (1) do not seek to know the transgender student before arrival; (2) do not aim specifically to be a “good teacher for transgender students” as a separate preidentified group; and (3) remain wary of curricular representation as a sufficient strategy of welcome. This is a **normative, theoretical educational argument**, not a published trial assessing particular classroom lessons, students' measured attitudes, clinical care or science achievement.

The publisher page also makes visible original-author **numbered notes**, distinct from inaccessible continuous body paragraphs. Note 5 defines **gender diversity** more broadly than trans identification alone and explicitly addresses gender nonconformity among cisgender people. Note 7 acknowledges that different students might prefer ordinary or exceptional recognition over time; educators cannot know this prior to encounter. Note 14 explicitly cautions that the argument takes **Canadian contextual conditions** as its starting point and is not a uniform description of US school policy. The website does not claim South Korean anti-discrimination law follows Canadian law and does not generalize Airton's philosophical argument to every student preference.

**Source-to-claim conclusion:** The site's second sentence gives a modest, direct paraphrase of the abstract's first two orientations, avoiding any claim that all trans or gender non-conforming students have identical needs or that educators can accurately identify which pupils will disclose. Omitting the third curricular-representation orientation is an appropriate *selective citation*, not misleading selectivity in this short paragraph about teachers' assumptions and hospitality. The article does not require an inferred causal student-learning result, which the website does not make.

## Adjacent Gilbert (2021) and the conceptual transition

The original [Jen Gilbert (2021) publisher abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03626784.2021.1947732) verifies “Getting dirty and coming clean: Sex education and the problem of expertise,” *Curriculum Inquiry* **51(4), 455–472**, DOI **10.1080/03626784.2021.1947732**, first online **31 July 2021**. Gilbert recounts serving as an expert witness in the **2018–2019** Human Rights Tribunal dispute concerning Ontario's removal of a sex-education curriculum and a **Grade 6 transgender student referred to as AB**. The author asks how experts' desire to make sexuality scholarship and progressive sex education professionally respectable can obscure the ways students **make and remake curriculum**. It is **reflective, critical curriculum scholarship**, not a quantitative effect estimate or direct observation of a secondary biology intervention. The website's third sentence accurately selects the argument about adult claims to expertise and youth agency. The co-located Gilbert citation remains **A**, and no new claim of original Gilbert full-body access has been made.

The preceding paragraph's history of clinical and sporting classifications concerns how evidence and institutional decisions interact. The focal paragraph then changes the subject to **assumptions educators make when students enter classrooms**, not an unfounded claim that Airton or Gilbert empirically studied biological classification diagrams. The next paragraph distinctly addresses wider political and arts contexts, and the closing paragraph returns to the specific question of what happens when student criticism of a scientific model might influence classroom decisions. This argument moves through different institutions and claims with natural continuity.

## Sentence-level and section-level editorial judgement

All **three sentences** in the focal paragraph have a purposeful role. The first locates the classroom decision problem. The Airton sentence develops how assumptions about student identity affect **welcome** and is explicitly attributed to the author's ethic. The Gilbert sentence extends classroom authority from presumed student identities to who may influence curricula and what expert authority can exclude. It does not equate the distinct normative projects or pretend a single study demonstrated both. Phrasing such as “asks educators” and “considers” accurately signals argumentation rather than observed causal effects.

Across all seven paragraphs, openings and grammatical shapes vary. The final paragraph's two kinds of teacher judgement give an appropriately specific ending, without a rule-of-three generalization or stock moral. The author maintains Canadian English, noninflated thoughtful language, a restrained point of view, and meaningful conceptual connections without added mechanical transitions, em dashes, ordinary-prose colons or habitual corrective oppositions. Introducing a new statement about a child's presumed preferences, a specific intervention or a universal gender-policy rule would degrade precision. **No warranted published HTML rewriting was found**.
