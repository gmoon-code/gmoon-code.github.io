## Original source and access verification

**Stroupe, David (2014).** “Examining classroom science practice communities: How teachers and students negotiate epistemic agency and learn science-as-practice”, *Science Education* **98(3), 487–516**, DOI `10.1002/sce.21112`.

- The complete **author-hosted, journal-formatted original research article PDF** was opened at [David Stroupe's University of Utah academic website](https://davidstroupe.utah.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2024/10/Examining-science-practice-communities.pdf), including the publisher-formatted title/DOI, theory, participants and context table, classroom data collection, analytic coding, observed episodes, limitations and discussion. The text was independently cross-checked against the [first-party Wiley publisher record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sce.21112) and [ERIC original indexed record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1023268). It matches the original paper's 2014 journal volume and pages.
- The web-PDF **page-zero screenshot was attempted**, as required when reviewing a PDF through the source reader, but the screenshot service returned **404**. The paper's directly accessible extracted original text and external first-party metadata justify substantial-text review; **do not claim successful PDF image inspection**.
- The author studied **five first-year secondary science teachers in the Pacific Northwest**, purposively selected from alumni of the same teacher-preparation course. **Three** had earlier experience working toward ambitious science teaching in their preparation, and **two** had more conservative practice histories. School contexts included **eighth-grade general science** and **high-school biology/physical science**, with one teacher additionally teaching algebra. This is a purposive **qualitative multicase study**, not a representative survey or randomized intervention.
- Original data included classroom video and observation notes at several points across the school year, one full instructional unit between January and April, initial/final semistructured teacher interviews, classroom artefacts and researcher memos. The author collected and initially analysed the data as **one researcher**, consulted colleagues about patterns and conducted participant member checks. He explicitly identified both the small purposive sample and his own advocacy for ambitious instruction as possible limits to interpretation.
- The original analysis differentiates **cognitive authority** and whether knowledge construction was organized as **public/community practice** or a **private/individual enterprise**. In some episodes teachers invited students to develop consequential science ideas publicly, and in others they treated student contributions primarily as a correct-or-incorrect answer to be evaluated. These differences were associated with the opportunities for students to put ideas forward and retain them in class discussion. The study's reported percentages and participation patterns are descriptive within these five contexts; they cannot establish a general causal effect for other secondary science classes.

## Public claim and paragraph-by-paragraph interpretation

The directly associated public sentence reads:

> Studies of teachers' noticing and classroom discourse show how a student's contribution can become a question for collective inquiry or be treated primarily as an answer to evaluate (Krist et al., 2023; Stroupe, 2014).

The Stroupe article directly warrants the **classroom discourse/valuation** half of this statement. Its distinction between public knowledge-building and teacher-controlled right/wrong judgment is closely reflected. The adjacent **Krist et al. (2023)** teacher-noticing citation has a different source role, established at original publisher/research-team abstract tier in C-RV23e. Its authors asked **two teachers** to identify in-the-moment decisions using point-of-view video; this study must not be silently treated as part of Stroupe's five-teacher dataset. The two are jointly cited for distinct aspects of teacher decisions, not for a common causal effect.

The paragraph moves from the hypothetical cell-signalling diagram and a teacher's opportunity to pursue a student's question, through **Ko and Krist (2019)** on curriculum planning, to teacher noticing and the treatment of students' contributions once instruction begins. The last sentence acknowledges that teachers may welcome a contribution while retaining control of curricular questions. This is a meaningful progression from planning to enactment to the boundary of student authority. The next paragraph turns to materials, disciplinary guidance and learners pursuing questions beyond the plan. The sequence remains grounded, natural and in the author's gentle, precise Canadian-English style. An extra clause listing study sample size or the author's researcher positionality would not improve the existing scholarly-interest essay sentence.

**Decision:** The published `research.html` sentence and the surrounding prose are supported. **No public academic HTML edit warranted**. There is no invented student outcome, misidentified sample, unsupported causality or need to force a new connector.
