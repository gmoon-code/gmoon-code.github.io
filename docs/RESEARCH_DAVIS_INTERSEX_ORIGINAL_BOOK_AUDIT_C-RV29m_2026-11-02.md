### Original empirical and theoretical scope

Davis's original book introduction (printed pp. **8–14**) frames the inquiry through gender structure, medicalization and diagnostic naming, distinguishing institutional, individual and interactional questions. The original research collected **65 in-depth interviews**, comprising **36 adults with intersex traits**, **17 parents**, **10 medical experts** and **two other organizational board members**, alongside **over 300 hours of ethnographic observation** of public interaction at organizational meetings. The interviews were transcribed and coded with ATLAS.ti. Davis identifies a consequential sampling limitation: recruitment through intersex organizations favoured relatively educated, middle-class and support-group-connected participants; results should not be assumed to represent every intersex person or family.

In **selected passages from chapter 3, “Medical Jurisdiction and the Intersex Body” (pp. 55–86)**, Davis contextualizes the history of intersex medical treatment, John Money's influential model, activism and feminist critiques, and the profession's adoption of *disorders of sex development* terminology. She interprets the new diagnostic language as a way medical authority and jurisdiction could be reasserted. Importantly, she describes some interviewed medical professionals as critical of the dominant approach and cautions that multidisciplinary teams can either disperse or consolidate authority, depending on whose voices are included. This is an interpretive, historically situated analysis of interviewed practitioners and institutions, **not evidence that all doctors share one position**.

In **selected passages from chapter 4, “The Power in a Name” (pp. 87–115)**, Davis analyzes varied preferences for *intersex* and *disorder of sex development*, showing some participants embrace, some resist and others use terms flexibly. This heterogeneity directly supports the website's refusal to present intersex people as unanimous about medical classifications.

In **selected passages from chapter 5, “A Different Kind of Information” (pp. 116–144)**, Davis examines how clinicians present diagnoses to parents and how families describe their decisions. Her account links medical framing, availability of information from intersex people and support networks, decisions to pursue or defer interventions, and retrospective regret for some participants. These are **interviews, ethnographic interpretation and the author's arguments**, not clinical measurements or a randomized intervention. Selected passages in Davis's conclusion (pp. **145–170**) return to medical authority and the contested meaning of diagnostic categories while identifying how different groups can strategically engage with terminology.

## Claim-to-paragraph and writing evaluation

The website sentence, “The history of medical classifications of intersex variation raises questions about who defines a condition and how diagnostic categories influence decisions about care”, is a **source-faithful, gently framed reflection**. Davis provides both analysis of naming and interview-grounded accounts of care decisions. The sentence does not say that all such decisions were identical or that diagnosis is biologically meaningless. It does not describe a particular contemporary clinical guideline as validated by a 2015 sociological study, or imply that reading the book demonstrates learning outcomes in the author's secondary biology classroom.

The same paragraph moves to a **distinct** inferential problem in interpreting testosterone concentration, then a specifically **2011** elite-sport policy critique and the possible institutional consequences of those rules. Its predecessor considers XYY behavioural inference, while the next paragraph introduces teachers' ethical assumptions through Airton and Gilbert. The progression from scientific claims to institutional authority and then to pedagogical responsibility is coherent. The four sentences vary in purpose and do not require a forced transitional expression. The writing is restrained and remains consistent with the author's Canadian-English style; no correction was justified.
