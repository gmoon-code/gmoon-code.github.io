## Airton (2023) | Original publisher abstract only, A retained

**Lee Airton** (2023), “You don’t know me: Welcoming gender diversity in schools via an ethic of hospitality”, *Curriculum Inquiry* 53(2), 148–168, DOI `10.1080/03626784.2023.2200810`. Inspected [original Taylor & Francis article, full record and abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03626784.2023.2200810), [issue contents](https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/rcui20/53/2) and [ERIC EJ1388811](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1388811). Original first-online date **19 April 2023** agrees with the final **2023** issue.

The original author's abstract develops a **Derridean ethic of hospitality** through Claudia Ruitenberg's theory, contrasting preparing to welcome students whose gender identities teachers cannot know in advance with concentrating institutional arrangements on only students publicly recognizable as transgender. Its proposed orientations include refraining from trying to know a transgender student before arrival, not treating “the good teacher of transgender students” as an already knowable special role and questioning curricular representation alone as a method of welcome. This is a **philosophical and pedagogical argument**, not a new measured classroom intervention.

Public sentence: “Airton's ethic of hospitality asks educators to prepare to welcome students whose gender identities they cannot know in advance, without organizing their efforts only around students already identified as transgender.” The original author abstract supports this precise paraphrase. Publisher full content was paywall restricted and ResearchGate displayed **Request Full-text**; notes and full citations on the publisher page do not amount to substantial original main article reading. **Source row 1 remains A.** The text's ordinary sense of “prepare to welcome” is grounded; replacing it with a prescribed school policy would exceed this source.

## Gilbert (2021) | Original publisher abstract and notes, A retained

**Jen Gilbert** (2021), “Getting dirty and coming clean: Sex education and the problem of expertise”, *Curriculum Inquiry* 51(4), 455–472, DOI `10.1080/03626784.2021.1947732`. Inspected [original Taylor & Francis article abstract and notes](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03626784.2021.1947732) and [original issue contents](https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/rcui20/51/4). Published online **31 July 2021**.

The author reflects on her role as a **paid expert witness** in a 2018–2019 Ontario Human Rights Tribunal challenge involving a **Grade 6 transgender girl** and the provincial government's withdrawal of a sex-education curriculum. Gilbert's essay examines how claims to scholarly expertise and arguments about school health or well-being can inadvertently privilege adult framing, leaving less room to see how youth participate in or remake the lived curriculum. The original article's publisher notes clarify the adjudication ended as **moot**; the website wisely does not offer a simplified judicial victory story. It is a reflective scholar's account of advocacy, expertise, curriculum and politics, **not a measured effect of biology curriculum co-design**.

Public sentence: “Gilbert's account of a sex-education curriculum dispute considers how adults' claims to expertise can overlook the ways youth engage with and reshape the curriculum.” Supported by the original abstract. The publisher's `full` page exposes abstract/notes, while complete substantive article body remains access-restricted. **Source row 23 remains A.** Publisher notes alone are not a pretext to claim complete article access or to recast Gilbert's argument as observed student dialogue.

Two distinct **participatory visual and arts-based research projects**, Pride/Swell+ and the Queer Sexual Joy (QSJ) Project, collaboratively engaged **more than 300 queer and trans youth, adults and elders**, ages approximately 14 to 50+ years **across North America, primarily Canada**. This combined project reach is **not the number present in every focal workshop**. The article specifically examines an August 2022 **35-person Pride/Swell+ workshop** in Fredericton with participant-generated collages, discussion and co-analysis. Separately, the **QSJ Project involved 100 participants** in workshops, focus groups, surveys and interviews, with documented recruitment and thematic analysis. Both original projects primarily gathered **outside formal school classrooms**, though their authors draw conceptual and pedagogical implications for educators. The researchers expressly acknowledge boundaries in who participated, including difficulties adequately including Black, Indigenous and other racialized queer youth in Pride/Swell+, and the possibility that adult–student authority relations alter the conditions of school-based workshops.

They are neither scores from a science assessment nor demonstrated outcomes of a secondary biology intervention. The researchers suggest how classrooms might attend to community and joy but do **not** establish that these activities would have the same outcomes under formal curricular constraints.
