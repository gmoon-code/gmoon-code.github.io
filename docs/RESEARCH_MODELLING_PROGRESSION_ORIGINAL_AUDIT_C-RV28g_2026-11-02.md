## Original journal article inspected

**Schwarz, Christina V., Brian J. Reiser, Elizabeth A. Davis, Lisa Kenyon, Andres Achér, David Fortus, Yael Shwartz, Barbara Hug and Joe Krajcik (2009)**, “Developing a learning progression for scientific modeling: Making scientific modeling accessible and meaningful for learners”, *Journal of Research in Science Teaching*, 46(6), 632–654, DOI `10.1002/tea.20311`.

I opened the [complete 23-page journal-formatted article PDF](https://www.mrpalermo.com/uploads/9/8/9/6/9896107/developing_a_learning_progression_for_scientific_modeling_1.pdf), a public teacher-hosted copy of the published scholarly article, and inspected its **page-zero screenshot successfully**. The image confirms the journal masthead, authors, title, year, volume, pagination and DOI, independently cross-checked against [Wiley's original journal record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tea.20311). This is genuine original **published article text** despite the nonpublisher web host. The relevant theoretical development, empirical design, examples, tables and final methodological cautions were read directly from the PDF; figures beyond the first page were not independently image-checked.

The authors propose a **two-dimensional learning progression** incorporating modelling practice with metamodeling knowledge. The dimensions concern the use of scientific models as tools for **explaining and predicting**, and recognition that models **can be revised as understanding improves**. Models support sensemaking as well as communication, and model evaluation draws on evidence and explanatory adequacy. The proposed construct maps are illustrated by **Grade 5 evaporation and condensation/solar stills** and **Grade 6 light/shadows and matter** investigations at different school sites in the Midwestern United States. Original records consulted included written assessments, reflective interviews and classroom discourse.

The article explicitly cautions that these empirical excerpts are **not an overall validation of the proposed progression, a test of one teaching approach, or a causal generalization**. In the authors' discussion, students' models often remained oriented toward the teacher, and teachers commonly instructed students when to revise. Those observations are methodologically important when comparing the paper with literature on student authority over evaluative criteria. The source does not establish that a model-revision task alone redistributes epistemic authority.

## Public wording, paragraph relationships and decision

The published Research paragraph reads:

> A learning progression for scientific modelling describes how students may come to treat models as tools for explaining and predicting, as well as representations that can change with new understanding (Schwarz et al., 2009). The questions students are trying to resolve also shape how scientific practices and argumentation become meaningful (Berland et al., 2016; Manz, 2015).

The first sentence correctly characterizes the two dimensions and uses **“may come to treat”** rather than treating the progression as universally demonstrated. The preceding paragraph distinguishes **fifth-grade students helping revise class criteria** in Murphy et al. (2023) from a **seventh-grade task where criteria were chosen in advance** in Cheung and Yang (2026). Schwarz's original discussion of students being told to revise, despite improvements in model content and use, supports the importance of that distinction, though the website does not attribute the particular criterion-control comparison to Schwarz. The subsequent sentence about the epistemic purpose of argumentation is attributed separately to Berland and Manz, both reviewed at publisher/record tier in C-RV24d. It should not be interpreted as a measured outcome of the Schwarz activities.

The paragraphs move coherently from **who establishes criteria**, to **what models do and how revision becomes meaningful**, to broader questions about epistemic purposes in scientific practices. The section does not repeat an abstract conclusion about the author's identity or force a causal bridge to the following section on classifications. Sentence structure, semantic transitions, restraint, Canadian spelling, and the author's gentle, curious scholarly voice already meet the required style rules.

**Editorial decision:** **No public HTML wording or citation was changed.** The original prose is scientifically defensible, properly conditional, and appropriately sized for a reflective academic research overview. The original article's methodological reservations are recorded here rather than inserted as unnecessary detail into the brief public paragraph.
