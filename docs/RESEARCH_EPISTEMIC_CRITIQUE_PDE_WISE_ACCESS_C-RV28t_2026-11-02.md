## González-Howard and McNeill (2020), original journal abstract and incomplete publisher preview only

**María González-Howard and Katherine L. McNeill.** “Acting with epistemic agency: Characterizing student critique during argumentation discussions.” *Science Education* **104(6), 953–982 (2020)**, DOI `10.1002/sce.21592`.

The [original Wiley abstract and 2020 original issue metadata](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/sce.21592) confirm the authors investigated **instances of student critique in three middle-school classrooms**, using **social network analysis** to characterize patterns of critique exchanges and **discourse analysis** to characterize student language moves. These analyses describe potentially important routes for exercising epistemic agency in peer discussion. The journal record does not substantiate equal agency for every student, a causal intervention effect or a generalized secondary biology gain. [ResearchGate's corresponding publication record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343105721_Acting_with_epistemic_agency_Characterizing_student_critique_during_argumentation_discussions) displays **Publisher preview available / Request full-text**, with a journal-formatted **first-page transcription and abstract only**. Related 2019 papers by the same researchers on teachers' argumentation frames and sociogram methods are **distinct studies** and must not be substituted for the 2020 three-class paper. Wiley's `/doi/full/` and `/doi/pdf/` endpoints redirected to its **abstract**, rather than disclosing full article methods/results.

The public passage states:

> Classmates may question an explanation's claims or ask for further evidence. Analyses of critique during discussions in three middle-school classrooms describe how students could exercise epistemic agency by responding to peers' reasoning (González-Howard & McNeill, 2020).

This is source-specific and appropriately qualified through **“could”**. Its connection from which linguistic/scientific practices count as evidence to critique as an exchange of reasons is meaningful. The following paragraph examines **different** language-policy conditions in a Malaysian elementary English-language classroom and a Canadian university programme, expressly declining to infer secondary science learning. The source genres and populations are distinct and remain so.

**Decision:** Retain citation **row 26 at A**. No public HTML edit warranted and **no original complete article access claimed**.

## Engle and Conant (2002), original journal abstract and indexed case description only

**Randi A. Engle and Faith R. Conant.** “Guiding principles for fostering productive disciplinary engagement: Explaining an emergent argument in a community of learners classroom.” *Cognition and Instruction* **20(4), 399–483 (2002)**, DOI `10.1207/S1532690XCI2004_1`.

The [first-party Taylor & Francis abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S1532690XCI2004_1) and [ERIC EJ665018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ665018) confirm that the article considers an **emergent Grade 5 endangered-species classification controversy** in a Fostering Communities of Learners class. The original framework offers four mutually reinforcing principles: **problematizing a subject; authority to address a problem; accountability to people and disciplinary norms; and relevant resources**. The authors propose a situated interpretation of the children's sustained argument and compare it with two previously published illustrative cases. The 2002 journal issue year must not be replaced with Taylor & Francis's later **2010 platform-online date**. The original complete 85-page paper was not independently accessed after targeted searches. Later National Academies summaries, reprints of the abstract and separate pedagogical guides do **not** count as original paper methods and results.

The website states:

> Productive disciplinary engagement provides a further account of how student authority can develop alongside responsibility to peers and access to disciplinary resources (Engle & Conant, 2002).

This is a faithful, conditional synthesis that does not suggest teacher authority simply disappears, or that an informal robotics competition has the same teaching conditions as the article's Grade 5 classification debate. The preceding sentences deliberately distinguish an early-career teacher's professional commitments and a separate high-school robotics study. The paragraph's differing genres support a thematic inquiry into authority without asserting an intervention outcome.

**Decision:** Retain citation **row 20 at A**. No public HTML edit warranted.

## Slotta and Linn (2009), substantial original book abstract/description, not complete book text

**James D. Slotta and Marcia C. Linn.** *WISE Science: Web-Based Inquiry in the Classroom*. Teachers College Press, **2009**, publisher ISBN `9780807749494` (paperback) / `9780807749500` (hardback). The [original indexed ERIC book record ED527596](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED527596), whose synopsis is explicitly **“Abstractor: As Provided”**, and [WISE Research's research-team publications list](https://wise-research.berkeley.edu/publications/) corroborate the first-party book identity. Library/catalogue records and [Google Books publication description](https://books.google.com/books/about/WISE_Science.html?id=r1QkAQAAMAAJ) additionally list the book's contents, including **knowledge integration, instructional design patterns, WISE curriculum partnerships, classroom teaching and professional development**. This original supplied description explains WISE as a resource for teachers adapting web-supported inquiry for Grades 6–12. Catalogue records differ between **202** and **216** pages; the available records do not establish whether this reflects preliminary matter, format or a cataloguing convention. The site does not rely on either count.

Crucial provenance caveat: the readily downloadable PDF “The Web-based Inquiry Science Environment (WISE): Scaffolding knowledge integration in the science classroom” by **Slotta alone** is a **separate earlier book chapter** in *Internet Environments for Science Education*, **not** this jointly authored 2009 book. A different SERC record likewise discusses a **2004 WISE chapter**. Neither can justify claiming to have read the original 2009 book. The [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED527596) is substantially descriptive, but contains **no original full 2009 book chapter methods, student responses, lesson artefacts or analytic passages**. Its book metadata remains historically indexed as **M**, since the rolling tier scheme has treated complete article research abstracts differently from book descriptions. That cautious status is retained rather than silently reclassifying the M source from a catalogue blurb.

The published `research.html#curriculum-decisions` sentence reads:

> Web-based science inquiry environments raise a related question about how the resources prepared for a lesson shape the explanations students develop (Slotta & Linn, 2009).

The authors' original book description supports the **existence and curricular purpose of WISE**; the website does not claim the book itself measured how the author's students' explanations changed. The sentence is a **researcher's forward-looking question**, and belongs within the paragraph's logical movement from worthwhile inquiry problems to scaffolds, physical-material investigations and prepared online resources. The next sentence returns to the teacher's planning challenge. The connection is varied and deliberate rather than a repeated “this shows why” conclusion.

**Decision:** Retain **Slotta row 47 at M** under the current conservative classification, while adding source-specific verification details for its original book description. No public HTML edit warranted.

## Cross-paragraph accuracy and writing judgment

The three paragraphs address different dimensions of the user's developing research interests:

1. **Peer interactions** that can make student critique consequential, with classroom data from three middle-school science classes.
2. **Authority and accountability** in disciplinary inquiry, illustrated by a Grade 5 case and a general conceptual framework.
3. **Prepared curriculum resources** that may shape what forms of investigation and explanation are possible, described as a planning question rather than an observed student outcome.

They are deliberately connected without collapsing participants, methods or evidentiary strength. The transitions proceed through specific purposes in classroom work. Replacing the existing conditional language with stronger claims would be less accurate; adding more formal transition words would weaken the author's gentle, intellectually curious voice. No public citation, style or wording revision meets the requirement for a justified improvement.
