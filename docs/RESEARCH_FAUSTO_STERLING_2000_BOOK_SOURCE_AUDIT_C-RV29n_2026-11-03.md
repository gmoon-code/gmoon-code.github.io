## Original claim and inferential boundaries

**Who defines sex and a medical condition.** The historical account toward the end of chapter 2 (printed pp. 42–44) describes shifting authority from religious and legal institutions to medical professionals. It acknowledges that emerging biological knowledge and surgery were sometimes motivated by efforts to help patients live safely and comfortably, while analyzing the additional cultural assumptions about the number of sexes, sexual orientation and acceptable gender roles. This nuanced account does **not** imply that anatomical variation lacks biological mechanisms or that every intervention has the same indication.

**How doctors explained and classified intersex development.** Chapter 3 opens with the medical response to an intersex newborn (printed pp. 45–51), discussing which professionals are consulted, the interpretation offered to parents and the institutional impulse to assign a binary sex quickly. Later selected passages inspect clinical publications and surgeon judgement, highlighting variation in clinical decision-making, disclosure to patients, the distinction between endocrine/metabolic treatment and surgery, and a range of evidence on operations. The book's description of a common or recommended practice reflects **its historical 2000-era period**, not a verified current clinical protocol.

**How categories enter care decisions.** In chapter 4 (printed pp. 78–94, with selected discussion later in the chapter), Fausto-Sterling revisits her deliberately provocative earlier five-sex proposal, explicitly recognizes its rhetorical character and advocates changing nonessential infant genital surgery practices. Her proposed alternative distinguishes clinically necessary treatment for immediate wellbeing from operations pursued to make anatomy fit normative expectations, and calls for long-term information and counselling for families and children. The chapter examines intersex advocates, clinicians' arguments, reports of treatment consequences, parental disagreement or refusal, and published medical case evidence. The book analyses limitations in the evidence and conflicts about what constitutes successful treatment, rather than establishing uniform medical outcomes for every patient.

**Relationship to Davis (2015).** Fausto-Sterling's 2000 book synthesizes historical clinical research, categories and intersex advocacy to question prevailing authority. Davis's separate 2015 sociological book supplies later interviews and observations about medical jurisdiction and contested diagnostic naming, including perspectives after the **2006** institutional terminology shift, and documents variability among intersex people and clinicians. The website's joint citation uses the two works for a cautious, shared question about the social/clinical consequences of categorization, without collapsing their publication periods, research methods or terminologies.

**Relationship to the adjoining website sentences.** Jordan-Young and Karkazis (2019) separately examine unjustified inference from testosterone concentrations. Karkazis and colleagues (2012) address **2011** women's sport eligibility policies. These are distinct interpretive and institutional examples, neither an outcome evaluation of Fausto-Sterling's proposals nor proof about current sport rules. The next paragraph moves from professional categories to teacher assumptions through Airton and Gilbert, then to student participation. Every transition has a substantive intellectual function; no conventional filler phrase or moralising concluding sentence would improve the author's voice.
