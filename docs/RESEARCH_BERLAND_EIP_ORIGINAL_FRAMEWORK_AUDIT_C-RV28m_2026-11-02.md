## Actual analytic contribution, examples and claims

The authors develop an **Epistemologies in Practice (EIP)** framework. It seeks to distinguish the **appearance of scientific practices** such as citing evidence or drawing a model from **purposeful science knowledge construction and evaluation**. Its two broad components are students' **epistemic goals** for the work and their **epistemic understandings or considerations** guiding decisions about how to do it. The specific considerations are **nature, generality, justification and audience**. These characterize the choices learners make about their knowledge products and the usefulness of those choices for resolving questions. They are not prescriptive procedural steps or four independently established causal mechanisms.

The original article provides three **illustrative classroom episodes** involving the authors' previous curriculum projects **IQWST and MoDeLS**. The episodes include students considering the generality and audience of a model and using evidence and mechanism to clarify claims about natural phenomena. The original methods note explicitly says a student merely reporting “scientists change models” or “claims require evidence” is **insufficient evidence** of meaningful engagement. Instead, analysts examine how students actually deploy these considerations in deciding what evidence to pursue or what model features to revise.

The examples establish that the EIP framework can **describe and interpret possible meaningful uses** of epistemic considerations in real classroom activity. They do not test a controlled intervention, show that every practice necessarily improves understanding, or establish that all learners have equivalent authority to choose a problem. The paper's later discussion identifies questions for future research on how considerations interact and support one another over time; those remain **proposed and exploratory**, not established longitudinal development for the website author's students.
