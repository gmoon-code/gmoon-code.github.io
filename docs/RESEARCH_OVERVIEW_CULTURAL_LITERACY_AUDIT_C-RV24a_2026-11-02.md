## Source-to-claim crosswalk

**Asghar (2013), *Evolution: Education and Outreach* 6, article 10.** [Original open-access research article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1936-6434-6-10). Original Methods reports **25 Muslim secondary science teachers** in Canada and Pakistan, with semi-structured interviews and focus groups. Findings describe teachers negotiating theological commitments and evolutionary science, differentiated responses to human and nonhuman evolution, and gaps in participants' conceptual grasp of scientific evolutionary mechanisms. The site's summary is **accurate**: “Interviews with Muslim secondary science teachers in Canada and Pakistan examined how religious commitments informed their views of evolution and its teaching, while also identifying gaps in some teachers' understanding of evolutionary mechanisms.” The wording avoids claiming that all teachers rejected evolution or that religious affiliation alone caused incorrect explanations. The original article is fully accessible and its relevant methods/discussion were read.

**Alsop & Watts (2000), *Physics Education* 35(2), 132–138.** [Original IOP publisher record and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1088/0031-9120/35/2/311). The publisher confirms a **comparative study of two groups of learners** exploring how feelings about an emotive topic, **radioactivity**, might influence approaches to physics learning. The website's exact wording is cautious: “two groups of physics learners approached radioactivity in ways that the researchers examined in relation to their feelings about the subject.” **Supported at the original journal abstract tier.** Full study text, learner ages and exact measures are **not independently authenticated** here; no generalization about achievement or causality is made.

**Prasad (2015 journal issue, online February 2016), *Intercultural Education* 26(6), 497–514.** [Original Taylor & Francis abstract and publication metadata](https://doi.org/10.1080/14675986.2015.1109775). This exploratory collaborative ethnographic/action research investigated five elementary/language schooling cases, **four in Toronto and one in Montpellier**, where students created multimodal plurilingual identity texts, researched and discussed their compositions, and teachers/parents were interviewed. Public statement that “research on plurilingual identity texts has examined conversations alongside students' written and visual work” is **accurate at the original publisher abstract tier**. The website correctly avoids describing the identity-text study as a measurement of secondary science understanding. The 2015 bibliography year is **the issue year**, despite the original article's online release in February 2016, and is not an error.

**Simon (2011), *Contemporary Issues in Early Childhood* 12(4), 362–366.** [Original SAGE journal abstract](https://doi.org/10.2304/ciec.2011.12.4.362). This is a theoretical/reflective response on **early-childhood** multiliteracies pedagogy. It explicitly treats literacies as *relationally and culturally situated* and children's languages, cultural histories and classroom relationships as meaningful resources in pedagogical practice. The public sentence “A relational account of multiliteracies considers how meaning develops within social and cultural relationships” is a fair paraphrase. **Publisher abstract-tier** verification; not proof of a secondary science treatment effect, which the site does not claim.

## Paragraph and style analysis

The fish-knowledge paragraph, previously checked in C-RV23a, frames what participants spontaneously bring to an explanation. The next paragraph introduces a distinct case where personal commitments and affect enter conceptually scientific work. The transition explicitly notes that these are other settings and later turns back to the need to evaluate the scientific adequacy of an explanation. The subsequent community-science paragraph, already checked in C-RV23b, asks a different participation question. These passages distinguish study participants from the author's teaching and do not merge cultural, affective and conceptual findings into a single explanatory mechanism.

The plurilingual-identity paragraph develops a separate methodological question: how much evidence to preserve when meaning moves through conversation, written text and images. It juxtaposes a primary ethnographic classroom study with a theoretical early-childhood literacy argument. “The literacy settings differ from those of secondary science” provides a necessary scope qualification and is followed by a personal research interest, not an unsupported transfer claim. Each sentence serves the progression, and the plain voice, Canadian spelling, meaningful transitions and balance of uncertainty remain intact.

**Public changes:** None warranted. Editing the 2015 Prasad year to 2016 would lose the original issue-year convention, and replacing the conditional transfer language with a stronger science-learning claim would exceed the cited evidence.
