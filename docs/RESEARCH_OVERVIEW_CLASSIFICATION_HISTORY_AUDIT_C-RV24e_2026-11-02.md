## Original-primary-source-to-public-claim mapping

### Richardson (2013), XYY historical science

**Citation** Sarah S. Richardson, *Sex Itself: The Search for Male and Female in the Human Genome* (University of Chicago Press, 2013), [publisher page](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo16835663.html) and [specific chapter 5 publisher abstract](https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226084718.003.0005). Original publisher book and chapter-index material describes **1960s–1970s XYY supermale/aggression hypotheses**, how gendered ideas informed scientific inference, and subsequent epidemiological evidence undermining a simple XYY-aggression association. The website's statement that early XYY aggression claims drew on cultural assumptions about masculinity is **accurate**. The later review (Re & Birkhoff 2015) is separately checked in C-RV23a. The historical critique is of a particular poorly supported behavioural inference, not of the existence of XYY variation.

**Verification tier A:** first-party chapter abstract, publisher book description and contents. Original full chapter not asserted checked.

### Fausto-Sterling (2000), medical categorization and intersex care

**Citation** Anne Fausto-Sterling, *Sexing the Body: Gender Politics and the Construction of Sexuality* (Basic Books, 2000), [2000 publisher-catalogue/Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Sexing_the_Body.html?id=c3lhYfZzIXkC), [Wellcome library collection catalogue](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/kb5tvk4r), and [Intersex Society of North America historical book synopsis](https://isna.org/books/sexing_the_body/). The book examines the historical scientific and medical construction of sex, including three chapters on the social management of intersex variation, patient categories and intervention. The site cautiously states that historical medical classifications raise questions about who defines a condition and how categories influence decisions about care. **Supported at the book description and original community-organization synopsis tier**. The original complete 473-page book was not consulted in this stage; do not infer a specific present-day clinical standard of care from it.

**Verification tier A:** primary book descriptive record with contemporary community-organization characterization, not full-text inspection.

### Georgiann Davis (2015), diagnoses and medical authority

**Citation** Georgiann Davis, *Contesting Intersex: The Dubious Diagnosis* (NYU Press, 2015), [original publisher description](https://nyupress.org/9781479887040/contesting-intersex/). The publisher explicitly reports the sociological interviews with intersex participants, parents and clinicians, and the dispute about the diagnostic term *disorder of sex development* in relation to medical authority, treatment decisions, secrecy and activism. The published site does not presume all intersex people share a position about that contested terminology. **Claim accurate** as a sociological interpretation, without being mistaken for a clinical treatment trial.

**Verification tier A:** detailed original publisher description, not complete book text.

### Jordan-Young and Karkazis (2019), testosterone and inference

**Citation** Rebecca M. Jordan-Young and Katrina Karkazis, *Testosterone: An Unauthorized Biography* (Harvard University Press, 2019), [original author book summary](https://www.katrinakarkazis.com/testosterone-an-unauthorized-biography), and [publisher edition hosted by JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv24trbtg). The authors examine the biological and social effects attributed to testosterone in multiple areas, including competition, aggression, power and sport. The journal-hosted book description and chapter synopsis challenge treating a **single observed blood concentration** as sufficient to infer a person's capacity or conduct. The public text says these claims require examination of the mechanisms mediating the hormone's effects; that is **scientifically cautious and faithful to the book's expressed scope**. The book also contextualizes 2012 high-testosterone sport policies, while the site's separate **Karkazis et al. (2012)** reference specifically concerns **2011 policies**. No current-policy assertion is made.

**Verification tier A:** authors' description, original publisher edition/chapter preview and book metadata, **not** a full 2019 book read.

### Qui Dorian Alexander (2023), politics and carceral logics

**Citation** Qui Dorian Alexander, “TERF Logics Are Carceral Logics: Toward the Abolition of Gender-Critical Movements or Black Trans Life as Pedagogical Praxis”, *Women's Studies in Communication* 46(2), 230–234, 2023. [Original publisher bibliographic listing](https://doi.org/10.1080/07491409.2023.2193543), [original journal article as indexed by EBSCO](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi:10.1080/07491409.2023.2193543). Publisher record confirms year, author, journal and article. The indexed article abstract describes the intersection of **trans-exclusionary/gender-critical logics, punitive carceral structures and Black trans life**. The site's claim that Alexander examines such politics through carceral power and Black trans life is **accurate at abstract level**. This is a critical political/pedagogical essay, not data on the effectiveness of a secondary science classroom intervention; the website explicitly limits how far it extrapolates to classroom lessons.

**Verification tier A:** original journal bibliographic page and original journal indexed abstract; original full article restricted/403 in the available source tools.

## Synthesis and editorial evaluation

The source sequence is conceptually differentiated. Richardson establishes a documented history of XYY behavioural inference. Fausto-Sterling and Davis then shift from the validity of a historical scientific assertion to who defines medical categories and what those definitions do in practice. Jordan-Young/Karkazis addresses the invalid leap from a hormone measurement to individual attributes, followed by a **historically specific** institutional rule illustration. Airton/Gilbert and Alexander/Burkholder introduce separate social and pedagogical questions, with a clear limitation against asserting that findings from other settings predict outcomes in the author's own classroom.

No public paragraph implies that biological mechanisms are socially arbitrary, that clinical variation is a moral deficiency, or that an author's normative argument constitutes observed teaching-effect evidence. The prose remains specific, modest, naturally connected and consistent with the author's Canadian-English preference. No forced connective words, generic moral flourish or overstatement improves its precision.

**Decision:** **No public HTML correction** warranted. Historical vs contemporary status, research method vs conceptual inquiry, and clinical vs political authority are correctly delimited. Past stages' `#sex-variation` reference is clarified here as a **ledger-section-ID error**, not an error in the actual website.
