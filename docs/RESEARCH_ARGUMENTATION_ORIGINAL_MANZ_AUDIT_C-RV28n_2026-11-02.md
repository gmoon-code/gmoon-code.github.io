## Actual research contribution and scope

Manz offers a **theoretical review and synthesis of existing research**, not a new randomized intervention, not a sample of one newly recruited school cohort and not a meta-analytic effect estimate. The paper examines how classroom scientific argumentation has been represented through **argument structure**, **dialogue/process** and **discipline-specific content**, then asks what those analyses can lose if practices are divorced from the enterprise that makes them useful. Scientific arguments emerge from uncertainty, community goals, evidence-making and model construction. Classrooms have different tools, roles and incentives from professional scientific laboratories, so simply asking students to complete a claim–evidence–reasoning format may become compliance with school tasks rather than purposeful knowledge-building.

The review draws on literature examples in which students challenged what counted as a theory, used disagreement to refine proposed interpretations and continued investigating questions. These studies are cited **as previously published research by other authors**, not incorrectly recast as new participant observations gathered by Manz. The paper's recommendations include making argumentation integral to uncertain scientific inquiry, letting students scrutinize both their knowledge and ways of knowing, attending to cultural and linguistic resources, and understanding the development of classroom **epistemic cultures**. Its conclusion explicitly describes these ideas as ongoing research directions and emphasizes that teachers need context-specific materials and support, not a simple replicable procedure that reliably causes higher achievement.

The conceptual relationship with **Berland et al. (2016)** is supported but their studies are distinct. Berland’s EIP framework distinguishes epistemic goals and epistemic considerations; Manz attends to activity systems, the conditions that make argumentation functionally useful and how students’ roles evolve in a community. Neither source proves that scientific engagement is automatically deep whenever students pose questions.
