## Original published article and what it supports

**Publication:** María González-Howard and Katherine L. McNeill (2020), “Acting with epistemic agency: Characterizing student critique during argumentation discussions”, *Science Education* **104(6), 953–982**. DOI **10.1002/sce.21592**. Original [Wiley publisher article/abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/sce.21592), [Wiley publisher PDF route](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/sce.21592), [EBSCO indexed record](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi%3A10.1002%2Fsce.21592), and [ERIC original journal record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1271401) corroborate the original issue, two authors and publication date. Wiley identifies first-online **21 July 2020** and the November 2020 issue. The website correctly cites the year **2020** and the original DOI.

**Original published abstract findings and limits:**

- Data were **argumentation discussion episodes from three middle-school classrooms**, not an experimental comparison of three interventions in secondary biology. The original abstract does not identify a numerical child sample or participants' linguistic backgrounds, so **no such details have been invented**.
- The authors used **social network analysis** to characterize who engaged in classroom critique and the interactional patterns of those exchanges. They also used **discourse analysis** to examine the language moves made around instances of peer critique. The two analyses address different dimensions of the argumentation exchange.
- The authors interpret critiques as opportunities in which students may act with **epistemic agency**, thereby contributing to their classroom community's knowledge-making. Their abstract describes **multiple and sometimes conflicting approaches** to supporting agency, along with the importance of recognising and using all students' ways of critiquing.
- The article does **not**, on the accessible abstract evidence, demonstrate equal epistemic agency for every student, measured improved biology scores, effects of a randomized teacher intervention or causal gains arising from bilingual teaching. The website does not make any of these claims.

The focal paragraph introduces a theoretical concern about how language and school science practices are treated as valid evidence, then makes the meaning of **student critique** concrete. The adjacent sentence on asking for evidence gives readers an ordinary language example before specifying a study of students responding to peer reasoning.
