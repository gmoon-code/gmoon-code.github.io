## Neighbouring primary studies, factual scope and claim interpretation

**Bang, Medin and Atran (2007).** The freely accessible [original *PNAS* full article on PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1955807/) distinguishes **nonprofessional but knowledgeable adult fish experts** from the separate studies of children and parents. For the adult experts, a spontaneous **44-fish sorting task** showed cross-community consensus and reliable differences, with Menominee participants more likely to invoke habitat in their grouping explanations. On an **explicit sort-by-habitat task**, the authors reported strong consensus and no group difference. The website accurately reports those separate conditions, and its inference that absent mentions need not indicate absent knowledge is **cautious**, rather than a proof that all unmentioned knowledge was held by every participant. No populations are wrongly combined.

**Gonsalves (2014).** The [study record and abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257682412_Physics_and_the_girly_girl-there_is_a_contradiction_somewhere_Doctoral_students%27_positioning_around_discourses_of_gender_and_competence_in_physics) describe doctoral physics students' positioning in gendered discourses of competence, using observation, photo-elicitation and life-history interviews; the website does not pretend this is a study of secondary students. It identifies the role of expectations without claiming any uniform experience or quantifiable learning effect.

**Carlone and Johnson (2007).** The [original Wiley published abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tea.20237) confirms **15 women of colour**, ethnographic interviews during undergraduate science study, follow-ups **six years later** and contrasting trajectories into science-related careers and graduate study. Recognition by relevant others operated differently for participants whose trajectories the authors labelled **research scientist, altruistic scientist and disrupted scientist**. The website's “mattered differently” accurately conveys heterogeneity without assigning it solely to one factor or claiming the cohort represents all women of colour in STEM.

**Burke and Navas Iannini (2021).** The [original Wiley publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tea.21714) describes surveys from **202** children at **21** Canadian club sites and focus groups with **45** children at **14** sites. Its aim concerns science engagement and identity work in informal settings, alongside ways to strengthen connections across home, clubs and school. The website's third paragraph keeps the distinction between informal club science and school contexts; it does not claim a measured transfer effect. Original full-body access for this separate A-tier source is a valid next target.

## Sentence connections, voice and non-revision decision

The five paragraphs develop five different, appropriately ordered ideas. The first specifies an empirical case where knowledge organisation affects a response. The second bounds this case and introduces situated commitments and feelings, then asks how to attend to these while evaluating scientific adequacy. The third moves to informal-school boundary crossing. The fourth changes the question to **who can be recognised as a competent science participant** through two distinct identity studies and invokes Nasir's cultural-learning synthesis as a conceptual frame. The fifth makes the **researcher's own selection and interpretation** accountable through situated knowledge and video-method guidance. This movement is intellectually connected without converting every case into evidence for one sweeping conclusion.

The repeated use of recognition and cultural context supplies necessary thematic continuity rather than synonymous sentence endings. The focal paragraph does not collapse cultural membership into an individual's identity, treat all Menominee or European American people as uniform, or conflate gender-based doctoral physics research with indigenous ecological expertise. Sentence-level grammar, restraint, varied openings and qualified methodological interpretation fit the owner's established thoughtful Canadian-English style; no repetitive stock connectives, em dash or formulaic correction is needed. A prospective doctoral supervisor can read where original observation ends and interpretive interest begins. Rewriting only to vary wording would weaken the clear distinctions.

**Public `research.html` edits:** None. Bibliographic entry **#reading-cultural-process** is already correct and should remain unchanged.
