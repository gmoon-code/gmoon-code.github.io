Classroom action research · 2026 · Manuscript in preparation

Post-assessment reflection and mechanistic reasoning

In my AP Biology class, students revised written explanations after receiving feedback. Some corrections supplied the missing causal connections, yet I could not tell from those revised answers which relationships students could explain independently. I began following their reflections and subsequent assessment work to examine what the different records could support.

Study title · Revising post-assessment correction to examine mechanistic reasoning in Advanced Placement Biology

On this page Assessment patterns Paired responses Teaching decisions

Research design and assessment sequence

I examined de-identified records from 28 students across three consecutive unit assessments over approximately three months. Each selected question required an explanation of a biological mechanism. Since the scientific content and explanatory demands changed between units, I considered the tasks separately before comparing their classifications.

  1. Unit 2

    Unit 2 assessment and reflection

    Students explained how disruption of sodium transport associated with a chytrid fungus could contribute to dehydration in amphibians. After receiving feedback, they revised their answers and reflected on one difficulty, a transferable idea, and a preparation plan.

  2. Before Unit 3

    Preparation before Unit 3

    Students reported what they actually did to prepare. These were self-reports, so they gave me an account of preparation without documenting every study activity independently.

  3. Unit 3

    Unit 3 assessment and reconstruction

    The assessment asked how inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport by bentazon could lead to plant death. After correction, I added an individual reconstruction of that same mechanism, completed without access to reference materials.

  4. Unit 4

    Unit 4 assessment

    A subsequent assessment concerned altered phosphatase regulation and cancer-related cell growth. I considered the explanation alongside the earlier records, without treating the different unit questions as equivalent tests.

The number of complete explanations increased across these assessments. The questions, however, required different mechanisms, and students continued to receive instruction and practise between them. I therefore read the counts as descriptions of what students wrote at each point. The sequence alone cannot establish that the reflection routine produced the change.

These groups differed in their later classifications, although the reflections were written while students consulted feedback and the Unit 3 question involved another biological mechanism. The relationship in these records does not establish that reflection specificity caused the later difference, nor does it show which connections students could reconstruct when returning to an earlier question.

Cycle 2 reconstruction

I addressed part of that uncertainty in the second cycle by asking students to reconstruct the Unit 3 photosynthesis mechanism independently after correction. Comparing two explanations of the same mechanism made it possible to examine which causal relationships appeared in each, while taking account of the changed prompt and access to feedback.

The comparison concerned ten students whose original Unit 3 explanations were below complete. I read their original answers alongside their subsequent reconstructions, tracking the relationships among electron transport, the proton gradient, ATP and NADPH, carbon fixation, and downstream plant function.

Seven of the ten reconstructions met the item-specific criterion for a complete explanation. Some developed additional links or repaired a contradiction; others omitted a relationship expressed earlier. The students had received feedback, and the reconstruction prompt explicitly requested intermediate steps, so I could describe these differences without assuming that a change in the written pathway directly measured a change in understanding.

Follow-up questions developed from the records

The paired records helped me distinguish questions that would otherwise have been grouped together as requests to explain the mechanism again. For S20, the next question concerned relationships expressed earlier but omitted during reconstruction. For S17, it concerned how the student had resolved a contradictory account of the proton gradient. For S06, neither explanation had developed the intermediate pathway, so the starting connection remained uncertain. I formulated the questions below from those different patterns.

S20 · An earlier connection was omitted

Clarifying omitted connections

Could you explain or draw how inhibited electron transport affects the proton gradient, ATP, and NADPH, then connect both branches to carbon fixation? Asking for the intermediate connections would help me examine whether the student could supply the relationships omitted in the reconstruction.

S17 · A contradiction was repaired

Examining a corrected causal claim

How did you decide which direction the proton gradient would change when electron transport was inhibited? The reconstruction repaired an earlier contradictory causal claim.

S06 · The pathway remained undeveloped

Identifying the next causal connection

What process is affected immediately after photosynthetic electron transport is inhibited, and why? Neither response developed the required intermediate pathway.

These are follow-up questions developed from the de-identified records. The reconstruction activity was implemented during the inquiry; the subsequent use and effects of these questions have not yet been examined.

Methods, ethics, and limitations

All students completed the activities as part of their classwork, while the research used only consented, de-identified records. I checked the assessment classifications again after a delay, and a second reader examined a subset of the reflection and reconstruction coding.

The inquiry involved one class, three unit questions with different explanatory demands, and ten paired reconstructions of the Unit 3 mechanism. Students received instruction and practice between assessments, and the reconstruction followed feedback and a revised prompt. Those conditions limit what differences in written responses can establish about changes in understanding.

What changed through the inquiry

After the initial correction and reflection routine, I introduced a reconstruction of the same mechanism to examine connections that the earlier records left uncertain. Comparing those explanations gave me different questions to pursue. When a later response omitted a connection previously expressed, I needed to ask whether the student could still explain it. When a contradiction was repaired, I needed to examine the reasoning behind the change. An undeveloped pathway called for finding a starting connection before assuming what the student understood. The records helped me formulate those questions more precisely; how students respond to them remains to be examined.
