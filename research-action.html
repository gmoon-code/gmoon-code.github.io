Classroom action research · 2026 · Unpublished manuscript

Reconsidering assessment evidence

After an AP Biology assessment, my students corrected their explanations using feedback. I could see what they changed, but I could not tell which causal relationships they would be able to explain without that support. I introduced structured reflection and, later, an independent reconstruction of a photosynthesis mechanism. Following the records across these adjustments made some instructional questions more specific while also revealing how difficult it was to judge the effects of the adjustments themselves.

Research design and assessment sequence

The inquiry involved 28 students in one secondary AP Biology class over approximately three months. All completed the ordinary assessment and correction activities. With the required consent or assent and applicable guardian permission, their de-identified work entered the research analysis after grades were finalized. The three selected questions involved different biological mechanisms, so their completeness classifications do not measure one general ability across time.

  1. Unit 2

    Unit 2 assessment and reflection

    The Unit 2 question asked students to explain a proposed pathway from chytrid-related disruption of sodium transport across amphibian skin to dehydration. After feedback, they revised their answers and reflected on one difficulty, a transferable idea, and a preparation plan.

  2. Before Unit 3

    Preparation before Unit 3

    Students reported what they actually did to prepare. These were self-reports, so they gave me an account of preparation without documenting every study activity independently.

  3. Unit 3

    Unit 3 assessment and reconstruction

    The assessment asked how inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport by bentazon could lead to plant death. After correction, I added an individual reconstruction of that same mechanism, completed without access to reference materials.

  4. Unit 4

    Unit 4 assessment

    In Unit 4, students examined how altered phosphatase regulation could affect cell signalling and cancer-related growth. Their responses gave me another account of how individual students explained a biological mechanism.

I therefore treated the counts as descriptions of how students answered each task, without interpreting their difference as a measured gain in a single underlying ability.

Reconstructing the same mechanism

During my teaching review of Unit 3, several answers named photosynthetic processes without connecting the intermediate changes leading to plant death. Two school days after feedback-assisted correction, students completed an ungraded, individual 15-minute reconstruction of the same bentazon mechanism without notes, earlier answers, or feedback. The revised prompt explicitly requested intermediate causal steps. This offered another written explanation to examine, while changing what the question invited students to express.

I compared the original and subsequent answers of ten students whose Unit 3 explanations had been classified as below complete. The analysis followed the relationships they expressed among electron transport, the proton gradient, ATP and NADPH, and the consequences for carbon fixation and plant function. The AP Biology teaching case presents the detailed biological pathway; here, the comparison centres on what was present, absent, or contradictory in each pair of written responses.

A further comparison in Unit 4

In Unit 4, 25 of the 28 students gave explanations that met the criteria for that question, which concerned phosphatase regulation and cancer-related cell growth. The higher count could not be interpreted as progress on a common measure because the biological mechanism and assessment demands had changed. Individual records made this limitation especially clear. S06 had left the photosynthetic pathway undeveloped in both Unit 3 answers, yet produced a complete Unit 4 explanation. S22 showed the reverse pattern, reconstructing the photosynthetic pathway completely while leaving the connection between sustained signalling and growth restraint incomplete in Unit 4. Completeness on one mechanism could not stand in for an assessment of the student's reasoning across contexts.

Questions developed during retrospective analysis

The paired records suggested different starting points for subsequent assessment. S20 had previously expressed connections that were absent in the reconstruction, while S17's later answer no longer included an earlier contradiction. For S06, neither response developed the necessary intermediate pathway. I drafted the following questions after examining those differences, rather than using a single request to repeat the entire explanation.

Clarifying omitted connections

Could you explain or draw how inhibited electron transport affects the proton gradient, ATP, and NADPH, then connect both branches to carbon fixation? Asking for the intermediate connections would help me examine whether the student could supply the relationships omitted in the reconstruction.

Revisiting a previously contradictory claim

Present the original competing claims about the proton gradient and ask which is scientifically defensible and why. The later reconstruction no longer expressed the contradiction, leaving open how the student had evaluated the alternatives.

S06 · The pathway remained undeveloped

Identifying the next causal connection

What process is affected immediately after photosynthetic electron transport is inhibited, and why? Neither response developed the required intermediate pathway.

These questions arose after grading, during retrospective analysis. They were not administered as part of the reported inquiry, and their use or effects have not been evaluated.

How my practitioner judgment developed

I examined four practitioner reflection records alongside the student work. Notes A and B were written during the classroom inquiry. The first distinguished students’ reports of preparation from the explanations they could produce without feedback. The second documented my decision to retain original Unit 3 responses for later comparison, together with a concern that the more explicit reconstruction prompt might change what students wrote.

Notes C and D were written during retrospective analysis after grades were finalized. Comparing the response pairs showed that later completeness classifications concealed different earlier relationships and contradictions. I could identify more specific questions for subsequent assessment, although I could not determine how much of the difference came from correction, the changed prompt, or other learning between attempts. The inquiry thus informed what I needed to investigate next without establishing the adjustment’s effect on understanding.

Methods, ethics, and limitations

The school director authorized the inquiry. A volunteer without teaching or grading responsibilities managed consent, and I learned which students participated only after the relevant grades were finalized. To check the stability of my classifications, I recoded 24 assessment responses after two weeks and retained the same categories in every case. A second reader independently classified twelve reflections and all ten reconstructions. Initial agreement was eleven of twelve and nine of ten, respectively, and the reader agreed with the predominant comparison pattern for all ten response pairs. Disagreements about individual classifications were discussed using the coding criteria.

The inquiry involved one class, three unit questions with different explanatory demands, and ten paired reconstructions of the Unit 3 mechanism. Students received instruction and practice between assessments, and the reconstruction followed feedback and a revised prompt. Those conditions limit what differences in written responses can establish about changes in understanding.

What the inquiry contributes

The first adjustment made students’ reflections and reported preparation available for comparison with later explanations. The second provided another account of the same bentazon mechanism. Returning to the original responses revealed differences obscured by the later completeness classifications. Some reconstructions omitted connections students had previously expressed, while others no longer repeated earlier contradictions.

Evaluating the adjustment therefore involved two judgments. The paired records helped me decide which causal relationships and competing accounts required further assessment. They did not establish why students’ responses differed, or whether the adjustment improved their understanding. This distinction became the central lesson of the action research, changing how I interpreted the evidence generated by my own intervention and what a subsequent inquiry would need to examine.
