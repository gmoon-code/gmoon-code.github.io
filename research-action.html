Research / Classroom inquiry

Classroom action research · 2026 · Manuscript in preparation

What does a corrected explanation tell me?

After marking AP Biology assessments, I could read students' corrected answers and reflections. I still had questions about the causal relationships they could explain later, particularly when they no longer had their corrections in front of them.

Study title · Revising post-assessment correction to examine mechanistic reasoning in Advanced Placement Biology

What I followed

This inquiry followed 28 students' de-identified records from three consecutive unit assessments over approximately three months. Each selected question asked for a biological mechanism, although the science and explanatory demands differed from one unit to the next.

  1. Unit 2

    Assessment, correction, and reflection

    Students explained how disruption of sodium transport associated with a chytrid fungus could contribute to dehydration in amphibians. After receiving feedback, they revised their answers and reflected on one difficulty, a transferable idea, and a preparation plan.

  2. Before Unit 3

    Preparation record

    Students reported what they actually did to prepare. These were self-reports, so they gave me an account of preparation without documenting every study activity independently.

  3. Unit 3

    Assessment, then a new task

    The assessment asked how inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport by bentazon could lead to plant death. After correction, I added an individual reconstruction of that same mechanism, completed without access to reference materials.

  4. Unit 4

    Another explanation to examine

    A subsequent assessment concerned altered phosphatase regulation and cancer-related cell growth. I considered the explanation alongside the earlier records, without treating the different unit questions as equivalent tests.

The number of complete explanations was higher on each successive assessment. The questions covered different mechanisms, instruction and practice continued between assessments, and students could prepare in different ways. These counts describe the written responses at each point. They cannot establish that the reflection routine caused the differences.

What reflection added

For the 21 students whose Unit 2 explanations were below complete, I examined whether their reflections identified a specific reasoning difficulty. Among 12 students with such reflections, 10 later gave complete Unit 3 explanations. Among nine students with other reflections, three did so.

The reflection and later classification both concerned students' ability to express causal relationships, which may partly explain the pattern. Students wrote their reflections while consulting feedback and corrected answers. The responses therefore showed difficulties students could identify with help; they could not establish independent reconstruction of the earlier mechanism.

Why I added reconstruction

Looking back at the first cycle, I had records of what students could explain with feedback and of how they answered a different mechanism later. I still lacked a direct comparison with what they could reconstruct from the corrected mechanism itself, without consulting their earlier response. I added that task after Unit 3.

The comparison concerned ten students whose original Unit 3 explanations were below complete. I read their original answers alongside their subsequent reconstructions, tracking the relationships among electron transport, the proton gradient, ATP and NADPH, carbon fixation, and downstream plant function.

Connecting another part of the mechanism

The original response focused on ATP. In the reconstruction, S01 also connected NADPH, carbon fixation, and sugar production. Those added relationships made the later explanation more complete.

Question for the teacher · Which newly expressed connections could the student explain in another setting?

De-identified record · S20

A relationship appeared earlier, then disappeared

S20 had articulated proton-gradient and ATP relationships in the original response but left them out of the reconstruction. The second response was incomplete, although the earlier one contained relevant reasoning.

Question for the teacher · Was that relationship unavailable during reconstruction, or left unstated under the new prompt?

The case descriptions paraphrase the linked records. They do not reproduce student answers verbatim. Two of the ten reconstructions omitted previously expressed relationships, so the omission pattern is limited and still worth keeping visible when interpreting an incomplete response.

What I changed in my assessment thinking

I began distinguishing what students could identify while correcting an answer, what they reported doing afterward, and what relationships they could articulate in a later task. Each record answered a somewhat different question for me.

The paired reconstructions also gave me more specific reasons to follow up. A student who extends a pathway may need a chance to use it again. One who omits an earlier relationship may need a question that makes that particular connection available for discussion. I developed relationship-specific follow-up questions for a subsequent instructional cycle. Their implementation and effects remain to be examined.

How far these records take me

All students completed the classroom activities, and the research used only consented, de-identified records. The assessment classifications were checked after a delay, with a second reader examining a subset of reflection and reconstruction codes.

The findings describe one class, three different assessment questions, and a small set of paired reconstructions. Feedback, practice, changing questions, and the reconstruction prompt all shaped the available evidence. These limits matter when interpreting patterns and deciding which questions to pursue next.
