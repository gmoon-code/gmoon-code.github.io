Relationships Among Post-Assessment Reflection, Reconstruction, and Later Mechanistic Reasoning in Advanced Placement Biology

A two-cycle classroom action research inquiry into what students' corrections, reflections, and later explanations reveal about mechanistic reasoning.

Classroom action research · 2026 · Manuscript in preparation

The classroom question

After an assessment, a student might identify the term they missed or correct a sentence with the help of feedback. I was less certain whether the student could explain the causal relationships behind the corrected answer when a new question came along. I wanted to understand what I could learn by following several kinds of student work over time.

How the inquiry developed

Classroom and time

I worked with de-identified records from 28 students in one Advanced Placement Biology class in South Korea. The inquiry followed three assessments across approximately three months, covering Units 2, 3, and 4.

First cycle

After the Unit 2 assessment, students reviewed feedback, explained an error, and considered one change to their preparation. I also collected preparation records before the Unit 3 assessment so I could compare what students planned with what they reported doing.

Second cycle

After Unit 3, I added a written causal-reconstruction task. Students were asked to connect the inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport by bentazon with consequences for the plant. The task gave me another way to examine relationships that a corrected answer might leave unstated.

Linked records

I considered assessment explanations alongside reflections, preparation records, and reconstructions. Responses were classified as nonmechanistic, partially mechanistic, or completely mechanistic using explicit criteria. I repeated the classification after a delay to check the consistency of my judgments.

Participation

The activities were part of ordinary classroom learning. The research used only consented, de-identified student records.

What I saw in the records

Across the three assessments, the number of responses classified as complete mechanistic explanations was 7 of 28, then 18 of 28, and finally 25 of 28. Some students who described changes to their preparation later gave more complete explanations. Others continued to leave parts of a causal sequence unexplained even when they reported practicing.

Those changes are useful descriptive evidence, though the assessments asked different questions and relevant instruction continued between them. I cannot attribute the pattern to the reflection routine alone. Reading the linked records helped me see both the progress and the uncertainty more clearly.

What changed in my practice

I became more attentive to the steps between an identified error and a revised explanation. I now have a stronger reason to ask students to reconstruct a causal chain and to revisit related ideas in later work. Their reflections help me understand what they noticed, while their explanations help me judge which scientific relationships they can make explicit under a particular task's conditions.
