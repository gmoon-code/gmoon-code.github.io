Teaching / Osmosis practice

A sample learning activity

Osmosis and changes in mass

This illustrative activity uses mass changes in potato tissue across sucrose solutions to practise percentage-change calculations and biological explanations. The measurements are hypothetical and are not presented as classroom results.

Back to Teaching

Student activity

Three potato cylinders have the same starting mass. Each is placed in a different sucrose solution for 30 minutes. The cylinders are then removed, gently blotted, and weighed again. The solutions are labelled by the mass of sucrose dissolved per 100 mL of final solution.

Illustrative mass measurements for three potato samples
Sucrose solutionStarting massMass after 30 minutes
0 g per 100 mL10.0 g10.8 g
10 g per 100 mL10.0 g9.6 g
20 g per 100 mL10.0 g8.7 g

Percentage mass change = (final mass − starting mass) ÷ starting mass × 100

Data and calculations

  1. Describe the measurements. Calculate the change in mass, then the percentage change for each potato cylinder. Identify which gained mass and which lost mass.
  2. Propose a mechanism. Use what you know about concentration differences and movement of water across partially permeable cell membranes to explain the direction of each mass change.
  3. Check what the table tells you. Does a change in mass directly demonstrate water passing through a cell membrane? Explain what the data support and what you are inferring.
  4. Improve the investigation. Only one cylinder is represented in each solution. What would you change before drawing a more reliable conclusion about the relationship between sucrose concentration and mass change?

Teacher notes

Open the explanation and suggested follow-up questions

The calculated mass changes are +8%, −4%, and −13% for the 0, 10, and 20 g per 100 mL sucrose solutions, respectively. These values are illustrative, so the next step is to consider how a change in mass could be explained biologically.

A mechanism-based explanation would connect differences in water potential between each solution and the potato tissue with net water movement across partially permeable membranes. That movement can account for a gain or loss of tissue mass. The calculations provide evidence of a mass change, while the proposed membrane mechanism would need further evidence to be examined directly.

Students could then consider whether the cylinders began with comparable dimensions, whether blotting was consistent, and how measurements from independent pieces might vary. Plotting sucrose concentration against percentage mass change would help them examine the pattern across the three solutions, although additional replicates would be needed to assess variability.

The activity offers a short progression from calculation to biological explanation and questions about measurement. The level of detail and the follow-up investigation could be adjusted to the learners and the time available.
