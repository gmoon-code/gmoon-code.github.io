A sample learning activity

Reading a change in mass

This short activity connects observations, calculations, and a biological explanation. It was prepared as a shareable example for this website. The data below are illustrative, and no student results or classroom outcomes are reported.

Back to Teaching

For students

Three potato cylinders have the same starting mass. Each is placed in a different sucrose solution for 30 minutes. The cylinders are then removed, gently blotted, and weighed again. The solutions are labelled by the mass of sucrose dissolved per 100 mL of final solution.

Illustrative mass measurements for three potato samples
Sucrose solutionStarting massMass after 30 minutes
0 g per 100 mL10.0 g10.8 g
10 g per 100 mL10.0 g9.6 g
20 g per 100 mL10.0 g8.7 g

Percentage mass change = (final mass − starting mass) ÷ starting mass × 100

Work through the evidence

  1. Describe the measurements. Calculate the change in mass, then the percentage change for each potato cylinder. Identify which gained mass and which lost mass.
  2. Propose a mechanism. Use what you know about concentration differences and movement of water across partially permeable cell membranes to explain the direction of each mass change.
  3. Check what the table tells you. Does a change in mass directly demonstrate water passing through a cell membrane? Explain what the data support and what you are inferring.
  4. Improve the investigation. Only one cylinder is represented in each solution. What would you change before drawing a more reliable conclusion about the relationship between sucrose concentration and mass change?

Teacher notes

Open the explanation and suggested follow-up questions

The percentage mass changes are +8%, −4%, and −13% for the 0, 10, and 20 g per 100 mL solutions respectively. These numbers are designed for practice and do not represent measured classroom data.

A mechanism-based explanation should connect differences in water potential between the surrounding solutions and the potato tissue with net water movement across partially permeable membranes. That movement can help account for a gain or loss of tissue mass. The table alone does not directly observe membrane transport or establish the internal water potential of the cells.

Useful follow-up questions include how the samples were made comparable, whether they were blotted consistently, how many independent potato pieces should be tested, and how variation among pieces might be represented. Students could also sketch a graph with sucrose concentration on the horizontal axis and percentage mass change on the vertical axis.

The task is designed to make the transition from calculation to explanation visible. It is a starting point to adapt to the age of the learners, the time available, and the scientific focus of a lesson.
