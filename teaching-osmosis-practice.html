Teaching / Osmosis practice

A sample learning activity

Osmosis and changes in mass

This illustrative activity uses changes in potato tissue mass across sucrose solutions to practise percentage calculations and explanations of water movement. The measurements are hypothetical, giving students a common dataset to examine without presenting it as a classroom result.

Back to Teaching

Student activity

Three potato cylinders have the same starting mass. Each is placed in a different sucrose solution for 30 minutes. The cylinders are then removed, gently blotted, and weighed again. The solutions are labelled by the mass of sucrose dissolved per 100 mL of final solution.

Illustrative mass measurements for three potato samples
Sucrose solutionStarting massMass after 30 minutes
0 g per 100 mL10.0 g10.8 g
10 g per 100 mL10.0 g9.6 g
20 g per 100 mL10.0 g8.7 g

Percentage mass change = (final mass − starting mass) ÷ starting mass × 100

Data and calculations

  1. Describe the measurements. Calculate the change in mass, then the percentage change for each potato cylinder. Identify which gained mass and which lost mass.
  2. Propose a mechanism. Use what you know about concentration differences and movement of water across partially permeable cell membranes to explain the direction of each mass change.
  3. Check what the table tells you. Does a change in mass directly demonstrate water passing through a cell membrane? Explain what the data support and what you are inferring.
  4. Improve the investigation. Only one cylinder is represented in each solution. What would you change before drawing a more reliable conclusion about the relationship between sucrose concentration and mass change?

Teacher notes

Open the explanation and suggested follow-up questions

The calculated mass changes are +8%, −4%, and −13% for the 0, 10, and 20 g per 100 mL sucrose solutions, respectively. These calculations establish the direction and size of each change. Students can then consider which biological processes could account for the pattern.

An explanation of osmosis would connect the water-potential difference between the solution and potato tissue with net water movement across partially permeable cell membranes. That process could account for a change in tissue mass, although weighing a cylinder does not directly observe water crossing an individual membrane. Students therefore need to distinguish what the measurement records from the mechanism they infer.

With that distinction established, students could examine the reliability of the measurements. They might ask whether cylinder dimensions and blotting procedures were consistent and how much results would vary across independent samples. Plotting sucrose concentration against percentage mass change would make the three-point pattern easier to discuss, while replicate measurements would be needed to assess its variability.

The activity moves from calculation to a proposed mechanism and then to questions about measurement and inference. A follow-up investigation could be developed according to students' responses and the time available.
