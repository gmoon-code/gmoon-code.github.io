AP Biology · Documented classroom assessment and instructional revision

Photosynthetic electron transport and plant function

How can inhibiting photosynthetic electron transport eventually affect a plant's survival? Following the consequences requires an explanation of proton movement, ATP and NADPH production, and carbon fixation, along with attention to the conditions under which impaired photosynthesis can lead to injury.

I used this mechanism in an AP Biology assessment and encountered different explanations of the intermediate causal relationships. Examining students' corrections and subsequent reconstructions raised questions about the scientific relationships they could explain. I used these to develop the proposed follow-up teaching sequence and assessment task. The action research account reports the linked student records, coding, and findings.

Original assessment question

Unit 3 assessment · Original two-point free-response prompt

Discuss how Bentazon binding at the plastoquinone-binding site of photosystem II inhibits electron transport and can lead to plant death.

Reproduced from the student-facing question documented in my classroom inquiry. The prompt was selected before student responses were examined.

The assessment question identifies the site of inhibition and asks students to explain how it could lead to plant death. Their answers therefore need to account for the intermediate biological relationships. As I reviewed them, I distinguished responses that omitted a connection from those that proposed a process moving in the wrong direction, since these called for different follow-up questions.

Mechanistic pathway after electron transport inhibition

Explaining a causal relationship

When a student writes that ATP decreases, the response identifies a consequence without explaining what produces it. I would follow that claim back through proton translocation and the thylakoid gradient, asking the student to explain how ATP synthase depends on those processes.

ATP, NADPH, and carbon fixation

The proton-gradient explanation develops the ATP branch. Since carbon fixation also relies on NADPH, students need to follow the restriction of downstream electron supply to NADP+ reduction and connect both changes to the availability of ATP and reducing power for carbon fixation.

Scientific accuracy and mechanistic completeness

The distinction between a missing link and a contradictory causal claim affects the question I can ask next. If a student omits the gradient, I need to establish whether they can supply it; if they claim the gradient becomes stronger, I need to examine the reasoning behind that direction of change. For this reason, I recorded critical scientific inaccuracies separately from mechanistic completeness.

A mechanistic explanation connects entities, processes, and their consequences. Work on recognizing mechanistic reasoning in scientific discussion (Russ et al., 2008) and in biology and chemistry explanations (Shiroda et al., 2024) informed how I examined those connections. I then developed criteria for the particular relationships required by the bentazon question.

Correction and independent reconstruction

01

Feedback and correction

Students reviewed a two-point answer, feedback identifying incorrect or missing biological terms and causal steps, and their own correction. Feedback indicated what needed attention without supplying an entire model pathway.

02

Feedback-assisted reflection

Three questions asked students to explain an earlier difficulty, identify reasoning transferable to another biology question, and plan preparation. They could consult their responses, feedback, and corrections.

03

Independent reconstruction

After Unit 3 correction, students explained the bentazon mechanism without earlier work, notes, or reference materials. They could use writing, equations, or a labelled diagram, but the intermediate causal connections had to be explained.

04

Comparing the two explanations

I examined where a later account extended a pathway, resolved a contradiction, omitted a relationship expressed earlier, or remained undeveloped. This comparison informed questions I wanted to ask in a subsequent instructional cycle.

What the comparison changed for teaching

A reconstruction becomes more informative when I can compare it with the student's earlier explanation. If a previously expressed relationship disappears, I can ask about that particular connection before deciding whether to revisit the whole mechanism. The questions below follow this approach to the different patterns identified in the paired responses.

Classification of mechanistic explanations

These research classifications were distinct from the two-point scores students received on the original assessment.

The analysis distinguished explanations that developed a coherent causal pathway from those that supplied only some intermediate relationships or named an outcome without explaining how it arose. A sufficiently developed alternative mechanism, such as reactive oxygen species contributing to cellular injury, could also meet the complete criterion. I considered scientific inaccuracies separately, since a missing relationship and an explicitly incorrect relationship could require different follow-up questions. The criteria below summarize the categories applied to the Unit 3 records.

Complete mechanistic

A coherent, sufficiently developed causal pathway from inhibition through intermediate processes to impaired plant function and death, including a defensible alternative mechanism when explained fully.

Partial mechanistic

At least one valid intermediate causal connection, with a necessary later relationship still missing or incomplete.

Nonmechanistic

Components, disruption, or outcomes named without an intermediate causal explanation. For example, naming impaired photosynthesis and plant death alone does not explain how one could lead to the other.

The research account explains how these classifications were used across the assessment sequence and describes selected de-identified records.

The proposed sequence would begin by examining the relationships students already understand and the representations they use to explain photosynthesis. An idealized thylakoid diagram could help organize the causal pathway, but its simplified spatial arrangement would also give students something to examine as they developed their explanations.

After reconstructing the bentazon pathway, students could examine a related disruption through a different representation or unfamiliar prompt. Their responses would help me see which causal connections they could still explain and where further discussion was needed. Completing the corrected original task alone would leave that question unresolved.

Examining evidence and reconstructing a mechanism

Newly authored teaching material using constructed chloroplast-assay data. The task and its criteria have not been tested with students.

Student-facing extension · Independent analysis

In a hypothetical experiment, isolated chloroplast preparations are illuminated under matched conditions. A compound that inhibits electron transfer from photosystem II to plastoquinone is added to the treatment group. The control group has a mean net oxygen-evolution rate of 8.0 nmol O2 per minute per milligram of chlorophyll, compared with 2.0 in the treated group. Each mean represents three independently prepared samples, but the individual measurements are unavailable. ATP, NADPH, and carbon fixation were not measured.

  1. Explain what the difference in oxygen evolution supports. Identify a conclusion the two means cannot establish without further information.
  2. Develop separate causal pathways predicting how inhibited electron transfer could affect ATP production and NADPH production. Explain how the two pathways relate to carbon fixation, identifying relationships inferred from the mechanism.
  3. Propose a measurement that could examine a relationship in each pathway. Describe what the measurements would add to the oxygen-evolution result.
  4. Consider whether the experiment establishes that a treated plant would die. Explain what additional evidence would be needed for that claim.

Interpreting the response

Lower oxygen evolution is consistent with impaired photosystem II activity in this constructed comparison. The rates, however, do not directly measure proton-gradient formation, ATP synthesis, NADPH production, or the Calvin cycle. Students would need to use their understanding of electron transfer to explain why those processes might be affected, and propose measurements that could examine the predicted connections.

The absence of individual measurements also prevents students from examining variation among the three preparations in each group. Even if that variability were available, extending the result to plant death would require evidence about whole-plant function, exposure, and duration. The task asks students to follow a plausible biological mechanism while identifying where the measured record ends and further evidence would be needed.
