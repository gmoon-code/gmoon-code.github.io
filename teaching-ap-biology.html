AP Biology · Documented classroom assessment and instructional revision

Photosynthetic electron transport and plant function

This case examines how AP Biology students explained the effects of inhibiting photosynthetic electron transport, including the intermediate relationships connecting the molecular disruption to plant function.

The original assessment asked how bentazon could contribute to plant death. Following feedback and correction, I added an independent reconstruction of the same mechanism. I describe the scientific pathway, the evidence from the paired explanations, and a proposed teaching sequence developed from this inquiry.

Original assessment question

Unit 3 assessment · Original two-point free-response prompt

Discuss how Bentazon binding at the plastoquinone-binding site of photosystem II inhibits electron transport and can lead to plant death.

Reproduced from the student-facing question documented in my classroom inquiry. The prompt was selected before student responses were examined.

The original question identified bentazon as the initiating disruption and plant death as a possible outcome. Because bentazon inhibits electron transport at photosystem II, a developed explanation needed to trace its effects through the processes that depend on the light reactions. Those intermediate connections were left for students to supply.

The amount of guidance given in the question became relevant when I introduced the later reconstruction task. Unlike the original prompt, the reconstruction explicitly requested intermediate causal connections, so I considered that difference when interpreting the paired responses.

The Calvin cycle requires ATP and reducing power from NADPH. Reduced availability can limit carbohydrate production and, under sufficient sustained stress, compromise metabolism, growth, and survival.

This is a simplified principal pathway for the instructional question. The speed and severity of injury depend on exposure and plant conditions; a reduction in electron transport alone does not specify an immediate or inevitable time to death. Alternative scientifically defensible explanations were allowed in the research classifications.

Explaining a causal relationship

A statement such as “ATP decreases” identifies one consequence of inhibited electron transport. To explain the mechanism, a student also needs to connect the reduced movement of electrons with proton translocation, the resulting gradient, and the ATP made through that gradient.

ATP, NADPH, and carbon fixation

Following the gradient explains one part of the pathway, although carbon fixation also depends on NADPH. A response that develops the ATP branch while omitting reduced NADPH supply may therefore leave the connection to carbohydrate production incomplete.

Scientific accuracy and mechanistic completeness

The distinction between an omitted relationship and an incorrect causal claim also affects the feedback I might give. An omission calls for clarification of the missing connection, while an incorrect direction of change requires examining the reasoning that produced it. I therefore recorded critical scientific inaccuracies separately from the three levels of mechanistic completeness.

Mechanistic explanations involve relationships among entities, activities, and the outcomes those activities produce. Frameworks for recognizing such reasoning in student discourse (Russ et al., 2008) and across biology and chemistry explanations (Shiroda et al., 2024) informed my attention to those relationships. The classifications in this inquiry were developed for the particular scientific demands of the bentazon question.

Correction and independent reconstruction

01

Feedback and correction

Students reviewed a two-point answer, feedback identifying incorrect or missing biological terms and causal steps, and their own correction. Feedback indicated what needed attention without supplying an entire model pathway.

02

Feedback-assisted reflection

Three questions asked students to explain an earlier difficulty, identify reasoning transferable to another biology question, and plan preparation. They could consult their responses, feedback, and corrections.

03

Independent reconstruction

After Unit 3 correction, students explained the bentazon mechanism without earlier work, notes, or reference materials. They could use writing, equations, or a labelled diagram, but the intermediate causal connections had to be explained.

04

Comparing the two explanations

I examined where a later account extended a pathway, resolved a contradiction, omitted a relationship expressed earlier, or remained undeveloped. This comparison informed questions I wanted to ask in a subsequent instructional cycle.

Interpretation of paired explanations

In ten paired responses from students whose original Unit 3 mechanism was below complete, five later reconstructions extended the pathway and two corrected a contradiction. Two reconstructions omitted previously expressed relationships, and one left the intermediate mechanism undeveloped in both responses. The seven extended or repaired reconstructions met the post-feedback reconstruction criterion for a complete mechanism.

Because students completed the reconstruction after correction and received a more explicit prompt, the paired responses could show which relationships appeared under each condition. They could not establish that a difference in wording reflected a comparable change in understanding. I used the patterns to identify relationships worth examining further with individual students.

Classification of mechanistic explanations

Documented research procedure, drawn from the Unit 3 item-specific criteria in the action research manuscript. These categories were used for analysis; they were separate from the original classroom two-point scores.

I applied three categories to the causal relationships students articulated. For the main bentazon pathway, a complete explanation had to connect the electron-transfer blockade to weaker proton pumping or gradient formation and reduced ATP production. It also had to connect restricted downstream electron supply to less NADPH, then explain the consequences for carbon fixation, carbohydrate production, plant functioning, and eventual death. A sufficiently developed alternative mechanism, including one involving reactive oxygen species and cellular damage, could also meet the complete criterion.

A partial mechanistic explanation contained at least one valid intermediate causal relationship while leaving the pathway to plant death insufficiently developed. A nonmechanistic explanation restated the disruption or outcome without explaining an intermediate mechanism. For example, naming impaired photosynthesis and plant death alone did not establish the missing causal connections. These are summaries of the actual coding rules, not examples presented as student quotations.

I recorded critical scientific inaccuracies on a separate dimension. Statements that the blockade strengthened the proton gradient, increased ATP or NADPH, or accelerated the Calvin cycle conflicted with the required mechanism. Keeping this accuracy code separate helped distinguish an absent step from a contradictory one.

Complete mechanistic

A coherent, sufficiently developed causal pathway from inhibition through intermediate processes to impaired plant function and death, including a defensible alternative mechanism when explained fully.

Partial mechanistic

At least one valid intermediate causal connection, with a necessary later relationship still missing or incomplete.

Nonmechanistic

Components, disruption, or outcomes named without an explicit intermediate biological mechanism.

The manuscript documents these criteria alongside the assessment sequence. They describe how responses were interpreted within this research question and should not be mistaken for a validated general-purpose biology rubric. Individual student records are not reproduced here.

The proposed sequence would need to account for students' existing understanding of photosynthesis, available class time, and access to learning materials. I would also want students to examine the limitations of the diagrams they use, particularly where an idealized thylakoid representation could make the physical arrangements or causal relationships appear simpler than they are.

A change of context or representation could offer another opportunity to examine whether students can use the mechanism beyond the original question. I would be interested in which connections they retain and which need further explanation, since a complete answer to the corrected bentazon task alone provides limited evidence about reasoning across cellular energetics.

Independent mechanism explanation task

Newly authored teaching material, developed from the documented assessment problem. This task and its criteria have not been tested as a separate classroom instrument.

Student-facing extension · Independent work, without a supplied pathway

In a plant exposed to an inhibitor that prevents electron transfer from photosystem II to plastoquinone, explain how the disruption could affect ATP formation, NADPH formation, and carbon fixation. Your response may use an annotated diagram and accompanying sentences. Indicate which connections are direct consequences of reduced electron transport and which depend on continuing exposure or other conditions.

Criteria for evaluating causal explanations

A strong response identifies the blocked electron-transfer step and then develops both branches of the mechanism. It connects reduced electron flow with a diminished contribution to the thylakoid proton gradient and ATP synthesis. It also accounts for restricted downstream reduction of NADP+ to NADPH. Finally, it explains how less ATP and NADPH can constrain carbon fixation. A statement about plant death needs an appropriate qualification about exposure, duration, and physiological conditions.

I would assess accuracy and causal completeness separately, as I did with the original explanations. Omitting the proton gradient leaves a different question to pursue than claiming that the gradient increases when electron transport is inhibited. The extension also permits scientifically defensible alternative pathways and questions about the adequacy of the model.
