AP Biology · Documented classroom inquiry and proposed instructional follow-up

Photosynthetic electron transport and plant function

What happens when an inhibitor disrupts the transfer of electrons through photosystem II? The consequences extend beyond the light reactions, but explaining how they could affect a plant requires tracing several intermediate processes and considering other routes to tissue injury.

This mechanism appeared in an AP Biology assessment. Comparing students' original explanations with independent reconstructions after correction gave me more specific questions for future teaching, though it did not establish why their responses differed. This page distinguishes the documented assessment from a proposed sequence developed during retrospective analysis. The action research account presents the student records, classifications, and limitations.

Original assessment question

Unit 3 assessment · Original two-point free-response prompt

Discuss how Bentazon binding at the plastoquinone-binding site of photosystem II inhibits electron transport and can lead to plant death.

Reproduced from the student-facing question documented in my classroom inquiry. The prompt was selected before student responses were examined.

The question supplied the inhibitory site and asked students to work forward from it. A response needed to connect electron transport with the processes supporting carbon fixation, then explain how sustained impairment could affect plant function. During analysis, I attended to whether a relationship was absent or expressed incorrectly. These patterns called for different follow-up questions, although a brief answer could not always reveal why a student left something unstated.

Thylakoid structure and the inhibition pathway

What the pathway leaves open

Bentazon is a photosystem II inhibitor, but inhibitory strength varies with the compound and experimental conditions. Work examining several herbicides in isolated pea thylakoids found relatively weak inhibition and less specific interaction with the QB site for bentazon than for several other compounds (Battaglino et al., 2021). The original question assumes that electron transfer is inhibited; it does not establish how strongly a particular plant was exposed.

Injury can also involve reactive oxygen species and damage to cell membranes following disrupted photosynthetic electron transfer. Plants may show tissue injury before any prolonged shortage of carbohydrates would explain their condition. The extent of injury depends on exposure and plant susceptibility (University of Minnesota Extension, n.d.). These possibilities matter when interpreting the question's final reference to plant death.

When a consequence is named without its cause

A student might predict less ATP without explaining proton movement across the thylakoid membrane. I would ask them to trace what sustains the proton gradient and how ATP synthase depends on it. That response would help me distinguish a missing written step from a relationship the student still needs to develop.

When the proposed relationship is contradictory

A claim that inhibition strengthens the gradient raises a different question. I would ask what processes the student believes add protons to the thylakoid lumen and how an inhibitor affects them. In the inquiry, I recorded scientifically incorrect relationships separately from whether an explanation included enough intermediate steps.

In examining these responses, I drew on scholarship that treats mechanistic reasoning as the explanation of entities, their activities, and the relationships producing an outcome (Russ et al., 2008; Shiroda et al., 2024). I developed criteria for the relationships needed in this particular photosynthesis question rather than assuming that a general framework could classify each answer on its own.

Correction and independent reconstruction

In the earlier cycle of the wider inquiry, students reflected on their Unit 2 responses and subsequently reported how they prepared for Unit 3. The sequence below concerns the later Unit 3 assessment and reconstruction. Keeping these activities distinct is important when interpreting which records informed each decision.

01

Original Unit 3 assessment

All 28 students responded to the two-point question about bentazon and photosynthetic electron transport. Their original written explanations provided a record to retain for later comparison.

02

Feedback and correction

Students reviewed their Unit 3 responses and received feedback about missing or inaccurate biological relationships. They corrected their answers with access to that feedback, without being supplied a complete model pathway.

03

Independent reconstruction

Two school days after feedback-assisted correction, all 28 students completed an individual, ungraded 15-minute reconstruction of the same mechanism without earlier answers, feedback, notes, or reference materials. The prompt requested intermediate causal connections more explicitly and permitted words, equations, or a labelled diagram.

04

Retrospective comparison

After grades were finalized, I compared the ten original Unit 3 answers that had not met the completeness criteria with their corresponding reconstructions. I traced which relationships were newly expressed, omitted, or contradictory, while accounting for the revised prompt and intervening feedback.

What the comparison changed for teaching

Reading the later answer alone could make an omitted connection look like an explanation that had never included it. Comparing responses let me identify where to ask next, especially when a student no longer expressed a relationship that had appeared in the original answer. The comparison did not establish whether that relationship was forgotten, misunderstood, or simply left unstated.

If a pathway is extended

Testing the additional connection

Can the student explain the newly expressed NADPH connection in a different context without being shown a prepared model? What evidence would distinguish a reusable explanation from one tied closely to the reconstruction prompt?

If an earlier contradiction is not repeated

Examining competing claims

Which of the student's original claims about the proton gradient is scientifically defensible, and why? A later answer without the contradiction does not show whether the student can evaluate the earlier alternatives.

If an earlier link is omitted

Returning to the earlier explanation

Can the student explain the connection they previously made between the proton gradient and ATP production? I would ask about that relationship directly before inferring that they no longer understood it.

I developed these follow-up questions during retrospective analysis after grading. They were not administered during the reported inquiry, and their use in a subsequent lesson has not been evaluated.

Classification of mechanistic explanations

These research classifications were distinct from the two-point scores students received on the original assessment.

The analysis distinguished explanations that developed a coherent causal pathway from those that supplied only some intermediate relationships or named an outcome without explaining how it arose. A sufficiently developed alternative mechanism, such as reactive oxygen species contributing to cellular injury, could also meet the complete criterion. I considered scientific inaccuracies separately, since a missing relationship and an explicitly incorrect relationship could require different follow-up questions. The criteria below summarize the categories applied to the Unit 3 records.

Complete mechanistic

A coherent, sufficiently developed causal pathway from inhibition through intermediate processes to impaired plant function and death, including a defensible alternative mechanism when explained fully.

Partial mechanistic

At least one valid intermediate causal connection, with a necessary later relationship still missing or incomplete.

Nonmechanistic

Components, disruption, or outcomes named without an intermediate causal explanation. For example, naming impaired photosynthesis and plant death alone does not explain how one could lead to the other.

The research account explains how these classifications were used across the assessment sequence and describes selected de-identified records.

The proposed sequence would begin by examining the relationships students already understand and the representations they use to explain photosynthesis. An idealized thylakoid diagram could help organize the causal pathway, but its simplified spatial arrangement would also give students something to examine as they developed their explanations.

For the final meeting, I would use the constructed oxygen-evolution comparison below. It gives students a measured outcome to interpret while requiring them to infer the unmeasured consequences for ATP and NADPH. Comparing that response with their earlier explanations could help me identify which relationships merit further attention. Since the tasks differ, the comparison would still need to be interpreted cautiously.

Examining evidence and reconstructing a mechanism

Newly authored teaching material using constructed chloroplast-assay data. The task and its criteria have not been tested with students.

Student-facing extension · Independent analysis

In a hypothetical experiment, isolated chloroplast preparations are illuminated under matched conditions. A compound that inhibits electron transfer from photosystem II to plastoquinone is added to the treatment group. The control group has a mean net oxygen-evolution rate of 8.0 nmol O2 per minute per milligram of chlorophyll, compared with 2.0 in the treated group. Each mean represents three independently prepared samples, but the individual measurements are unavailable. ATP, NADPH, and carbon fixation were not measured.

  1. Explain what the difference in oxygen evolution supports. Identify a conclusion the two means cannot establish without further information.
  2. Develop separate causal pathways predicting how inhibited electron transfer could affect ATP production and NADPH production. Explain how the two pathways relate to carbon fixation, identifying relationships inferred from the mechanism.
  3. Propose a measurement that could examine a relationship in each pathway. Describe what the measurements would add to the oxygen-evolution result.
  4. Consider whether the experiment establishes that a treated plant would die. Explain what additional evidence would be needed for that claim.

Interpreting the response

The lower mean oxygen-evolution rate is consistent with inhibition of photosynthetic water oxidation under the constructed conditions. Oxygen evolution is associated with photosystem II, but these measurements alone do not quantify the proton gradient, ATP synthesis, NADPH production, or carbon fixation. Students would need to distinguish those unmeasured processes from the experimental observation, then propose measurements that could test their predictions.

Without the individual observations, students cannot examine variation within either group or assess the uncertainty of the difference between the means. Replication does not by itself establish statistical significance. Nor would an isolated-chloroplast assay reveal whether an exposed whole plant survives. That question would require evidence about plant condition, exposure, and the extent of tissue damage.
