Research Methods Studio

A research question can seem workable until a student begins looking for measurements, sources, or participants. At that point, the methods may call for the question to change. I developed Research Methods Studio to support these decisions across eighteen connected stages, from an initial interest and literature work to design, analysis, and writing. The stages give students places to return as they learn more about what their evidence can support.

Experimental design example

The experimental-study guidance follows a hypothetical investigation of seedling growth under assigned salinity conditions. This example introduces a decision that affects the entire analysis. Whether treatment is assigned to each plant or to a whole tray determines which observations can count as independent replications. Students also need to define growth and establish what the comparison could reasonably show.

Question

How does assigned salinity concentration affect mean seedling growth under standardized laboratory conditions?

Assignment

Decide whether the salinity treatment is assigned to individual plants or to entire trays. If a whole tray receives one condition, several plants inside it do not automatically become independent treatment replicates.

Measurement

Define growth, the observation period, and the number of independent units in each condition. Record starting size and keep relevant growing conditions consistent.

Comparison

Plan to inspect group differences alongside variation. The analysis must respect the experimental unit and the way conditions were assigned.

Claim

Explain what the comparison supports within the tested species, conditions, and time period. An observed difference alone would not establish every biological mechanism responsible.

Treatment assignment and experimental units

Suppose each salinity treatment is assigned to one tray containing several seedlings. The treatment then varies between trays, while plants in the same tray share the assigned condition. Treating each seedling as an independent treatment replicate would overstate the amount of independent evidence. The Methods Lab asks students to identify that dependency early enough to reconsider how they assign treatments or interpret their results.

Methods across research designs

The seedling example concerns an experiment, but similar questions about what evidence can establish arise in other forms of research. An observational study must account for selection and potential confounding without researcher-assigned treatments. Interviews require decisions about recruitment, consent, questions, and interpretation. A literature review depends on a traceable account of how publications were located, selected, and examined.

The eighteen-stage pathway connects these decisions to more specialized workspaces. Students can return from an analysis question to reconsider how data were collected, or from a draft claim to examine whether their literature search supports it. The comparison below identifies some of the choices that require attention within each approach.

ApproachDecision that changes the evidenceQuestion to ask before analysis
ExperimentAssignment of treatments to independent units, including shared trays or classroomsDoes the analysis respect the level of assignment and replication?
Observational studySampling, measurement, and plausible confounding variablesCould an observed association arise from differences that were not measured or controlled?
Interview-based inquiryWho participates, how questions are asked, and how interpretations are documentedWhat supports the interpretation, and how are participant privacy and agency protected?
Evidence reviewSearch sources, eligibility criteria, screening decisions, and record managementCan another reader understand which evidence entered the synthesis and why?

In the seedling experiment, the assignment procedure determines the evidence available for comparing treatments. In a review, the corresponding record is the search and selection process that determines which publications are examined. The workspace encourages students to document those choices, while responsibility for the final methodological justification remains with the researcher.

Guidance delivery and research records

The workspace also keeps instructional guidance separate from students' working notebooks. An authenticated Content Studio can publish a validated release of guidance for the eighteen stages. The loader checks six defined fields per stage and rejects incomplete or invalid releases; when an update cannot be loaded, the bundled curriculum remains available. Updating published guidance does not alter the application version or overwrite a student's locally stored project.

Student work follows a different path from published guidance. The notebook normally stays in browser storage, while optional Research Chat sends a reduced selection of project context to a server. The release documentation describes checks in both the browser and server and reports no collection of research-study data or operational telemetry. I have not independently audited those claims for security or privacy.

An earlier guidance release can be restored if published instructions need correcting, without altering students' working notebooks. That separation also raises a question for evaluation. I would want to examine whether students notice changes to the guidance and how they use it when reconsidering a methodological decision.

Architecture and release details are taken from the application's repository documentation. No private administration features, network responses, or user records were accessed in this review.

Research stages

Stages 1–4

Research topic and question

Explore interests and develop a question that can be investigated.

Stages 5–8

Literature and evidence

Search and compare related studies, keeping track of where a claim comes from.

Stages 9–12

Study design

Examine design choices, sampling, measurements, procedures, and ethics.

Stages 13–15

Data analysis

Consider data quality and choose analyses that fit the design and question.

Stages 16–18

Research writing and review

Report the study, trace claims to evidence, and examine what needs revision.

Methods and writing tools

Literature Workspace

Record and compare sources, including agreements, differences, and questions that remain.

Methods Lab

Review the match between the research question, proposed measurements, and study design.

Data and Statistics Lab

Work with a local data file, inspect its structure, and consider what analysis is justified.

Scientific Writing Lab

Revisit how methods, results, interpretations, and citations fit together.

Exports, backups, and privacy

Students can export a Word document for reading or sharing and make a JSON backup of their project. Because the working notebook is normally kept in browser storage, an external backup is valuable for work they need to retain. Optional Research Chat sends selected project context across a network boundary. These features can support the writing process, while students still need to check sources and explain their methodological choices.
