Research Methods Studio

A research question can change as someone discovers what can actually be measured, observed, or supported by the available evidence. Research Methods Studio gives students room to revisit those decisions as their work develops. Its eighteen stages connect an initial interest with the question, evidence, design, analysis, and written account.

Experimental design example

The experimental-study guidance includes a question about assigned salinity concentrations and seedling growth. A learner could write a plausible question quickly, yet the study would still depend on decisions about treatment assignment, independent units, measurement, and the scope of the eventual conclusion.

Question

How does assigned salinity concentration affect mean seedling growth under standardized laboratory conditions?

Assignment

Decide whether the salinity treatment is assigned to individual plants or to entire trays. If a whole tray receives one condition, several plants inside it do not automatically become independent treatment replicates.

Measurement

Define growth, the observation period, and the number of independent units in each condition. Record starting size and keep relevant growing conditions consistent.

Comparison

Plan to inspect group differences alongside variation. The analysis must respect the experimental unit and the way conditions were assigned.

Claim

Explain what the comparison supports within the tested species, conditions, and time period. An observed difference alone would not establish every biological mechanism responsible.

Treatment assignment and experimental units

Suppose each salinity condition is delivered to one tray holding several seedlings. The treatment has been assigned to trays, so the plants sharing a tray do not provide independent replications of that treatment assignment. Treating every plant as independent would make the evidence seem more extensive than the design supports. The Methods Lab asks for the experimental unit and assignment structure so students can examine that issue before collecting data.

Methods across research designs

The seedling example concerns a decision specific to experimental research, where treatment assignment determines the independent unit. Other designs bring different methodological questions. In an observational study, selection and measurement affect the claims that can be supported. An interview-based study requires careful decisions about recruitment, consent, and interpretation. A literature review depends on a traceable process for searching, selecting, and examining published work.

The workspace combines its staged pathway with specialised labs. The comparison below helps explain how the research decisions differ across approaches; it does not describe an automated validation of every choice.

ApproachDecision that changes the evidenceQuestion to ask before analysis
ExperimentAssignment of treatments to independent units, including shared trays or classroomsDoes the analysis respect the level of assignment and replication?
Observational studySampling, measurement, and plausible confounding variablesCould an observed association arise from differences that were not measured or controlled?
Interview-based inquiryWho participates, how questions are asked, and how interpretations are documentedWhat supports the interpretation, and how are participant privacy and agency protected?
Evidence reviewSearch sources, eligibility criteria, screening decisions, and record managementCan another reader understand which evidence entered the synthesis and why?

For an experiment, assignment defines the unit that can support a treatment comparison. In a review, the analogous obligation is a traceable inclusion decision. The workspace can prompt these questions, but a student still has to justify the methodological choice and check the evidence on which it rests.

Guidance delivery and research records

The version 3 release separates instructional guidance from students’ active research notebooks. An authenticated Content Studio can publish a complete, validated set of guidance for all eighteen stages. The public loader accepts updates to six defined fields per stage, including the stage title, purpose, and guidance. It rejects incomplete or invalid releases, and the bundled curriculum remains available if the published content cannot be loaded promptly. Publishing guidance does not change the application version or replace a student’s locally stored research project.

The application also separates public guidance delivery from optional Research Chat. According to the release documentation, project context sent to Research Chat is minimized in the browser and checked again by the server, while the notebook itself normally remains in browser storage. Students can export a Word document or make a JSON backup. The current release does not enable research-study data collection or operational telemetry. These are documented implementation boundaries, not the results of an independent security or privacy audit.

The content-publication design includes revision history and rollback through a new published release. That offers a way to correct instructional guidance without silently rewriting the student application. A separate evaluation would still be needed to determine whether students understand the guidance, recognize methodological problems, or make better research decisions while using the tool.

Architecture and release details are taken from the application's repository documentation. No private administration features, network responses, or user records were accessed in this review.

Research stages

Stages 1–4

Research topic and question

Explore interests and develop a question that can be investigated.

Stages 5–8

Literature and evidence

Search and compare related studies, keeping track of where a claim comes from.

Stages 9–12

Study design

Examine design choices, sampling, measurements, procedures, and ethics.

Stages 13–15

Data analysis

Consider data quality and choose analyses that fit the design and question.

Stages 16–18

Research writing and review

Report the study, trace claims to evidence, and examine what needs revision.

Methods and writing tools

Literature Workspace

Record and compare sources, including agreements, differences, and questions that remain.

Methods Lab

Review the match between the research question, proposed measurements, and study design.

Data and Statistics Lab

Work with a local data file, inspect its structure, and consider what analysis is justified.

Scientific Writing Lab

Revisit how methods, results, interpretations, and citations fit together.

Exports, backups, and privacy

The Word export is useful for reading or sharing a written account. A JSON backup serves a different purpose by preserving project information for recovery. Because the working notebook remains in browser storage, students should keep a backup outside the browser for work they need to retain. Research Chat is optional, and choosing to share project context crosses a separate network boundary. Neither export nor chat resolves the researcher's responsibility to verify evidence and explain methodological choices.
