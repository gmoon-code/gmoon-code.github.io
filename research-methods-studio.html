Research Methods Studio

This workspace helps students keep the decisions in a research project connected. Its eighteen stages move from an initial interest through the question, evidence base, design, analysis, and written account. Students can return to earlier stages when a later decision changes what the study can support.

A decision worth working through

The experimental-study guidance includes a question about assigned salinity concentrations and seedling growth. A learner could write a plausible question quickly, yet the study would still depend on decisions about treatment assignment, independent units, measurement, and the scope of the eventual conclusion.

Question

How does assigned salinity concentration affect mean seedling growth under standardized laboratory conditions?

Assignment

Decide whether the salinity treatment is assigned to individual plants or to entire trays. If a whole tray receives one condition, several plants inside it do not automatically become independent treatment replicates.

Measurement

Define growth, the observation period, and the number of independent units in each condition. Record starting size and keep relevant growing conditions consistent.

Comparison

Plan to inspect group differences alongside variation. The analysis must respect the experimental unit and the way conditions were assigned.

Claim

Explain what the comparison supports within the tested species, conditions, and time period. An observed difference alone would not establish every biological mechanism responsible.

Why the experimental unit changes the answer

Suppose each salinity condition is delivered to one tray holding several seedlings. The treatment has been assigned to trays, so the plants sharing a tray do not provide independent replications of that treatment assignment. Treating every plant as independent would make the evidence seem more extensive than the design supports. The Methods Lab asks for the experimental unit and assignment structure so students can examine that issue before collecting data.

This is a hypothetical planning example grounded in the workspace's experimental-study pathway and Methods Lab checks. It is neither a student submission nor an experiment carried out for this website.

When the research pathway must branch

The seedling example shows why research guidance cannot rely on a single checklist. An experiment involves assignment to conditions and the definition of an independent unit. An observational study asks how measurements and selection processes relate to a claim without researcher-assigned treatment. Interview-based qualitative inquiry requires a defensible account of recruitment, consent, interpretation, and the relationship between what participants say and what the researcher concludes. A literature review requires a traceable approach to searching, selecting, and interpreting published records.

The workspace offers staged guidance and specialised labs. The table below is an analytical comparison of decisions that its users may need to examine. It should not be read as a claim that every branch or automated safeguard is currently implemented in precisely this form.

ApproachDecision that changes the evidenceQuestion to ask before analysis
ExperimentAssignment of treatments to independent units, including shared trays or classroomsDoes the analysis respect the level of assignment and replication?
Observational studySampling, measurement, and plausible confounding variablesCould an observed association arise from differences that were not measured or controlled?
Interview-based inquiryWho participates, how questions are asked, and how interpretations are documentedWhat supports the interpretation, and how are participant privacy and agency protected?
Evidence reviewSearch sources, eligibility criteria, screening decisions, and record managementCan another reader understand which evidence entered the synthesis and why?

I would use these distinctions to evaluate whether the guidance asks students to justify a decision before choosing an analysis. In the salinity example, an apparent sample of many plants might represent very few assigned treatment units. That issue has to be resolved at the design stage. No later statistical option can supply independent treatment replication that the experiment did not include.

Because the notebook is stored locally and Research Chat is optional, data handling is also a design decision. Students need to know when work is stored in their browser, what a backup contains, and which material a networked feature may receive. These controls merit direct testing in the application; a statement on this page is not a privacy or security audit.

How the released system handles guidance and student work

The version 3 release documentation describes a local-first research notebook, eighteen stages, and optional Research Chat. The application can retrieve validated published guidance for the stages, while retaining a bundled curriculum as a fallback if the remote guidance is unavailable or invalid. This separates the stage guidance that can be updated from the student's own project work.

Research Chat introduces a different data boundary. According to the repository documentation, the browser minimizes project context when it is enabled for chat, with additional server-side minimization. The application does not enable research-study data collection or operational telemetry in this release. These are documented architectural claims whose deployment and security would require separate independent testing.

A meaningful design evaluation would ask whether students understand these boundaries before using the workspace. It would also examine whether the guidance helps them notice an inappropriate experimental unit, unsupported causal inference, or mismatch between a question and an analysis. The existence of a warning or method check cannot establish that learners acted on it.

Architecture and release details are taken from the application's repository documentation. No private administration features, network responses, or user records were accessed in this review.

Where the work goes

Stages 1–4

Find a direction

Explore interests and develop a question that can be investigated.

Stages 5–8

Read the evidence

Search and compare related studies, keeping track of where a claim comes from.

Stages 9–12

Plan the study

Examine design choices, sampling, measurements, procedures, and ethics.

Stages 13–15

Work with data

Consider data quality and choose analyses that fit the design and question.

Stages 16–18

Write and check

Report the study, trace claims to evidence, and examine what needs revision.

Tools alongside the stages

Literature Workspace

Record and compare sources, including agreements, differences, and questions that remain.

Methods Lab

Review the match between the research question, proposed measurements, and study design.

Data and Statistics Lab

Work with a local data file, inspect its structure, and consider what analysis is justified.

Scientific Writing Lab

Revisit how methods, results, interpretations, and citations fit together.

Saving and sharing work

The research notebook is kept locally in the student's browser. Backup and recovery options are available, and Research Chat is optional. A learner choosing to use a networked chat feature should review what project context they share and avoid entering identifiable or sensitive records.

The workspace offers questions, examples, and checks. Students still make the methodological decisions, verify their sources, and examine the limits of their own results. I designed the stages to keep those decisions visible while a project is still taking shape.
