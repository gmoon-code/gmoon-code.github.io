Research Methods Studio

A research question can seem workable until a student begins deciding what to measure, where to find evidence, or whom to involve. Research Methods Studio is a workspace I developed to help students revisit those decisions across eighteen connected stages. The synthetic radish-salinity example below follows one such decision from treatment assignment to analysis. It asks what counts as an independent observation when five seeds share the same assigned treatment in a dish.

A synthetic experiment from design to analysis

The application's repository includes a worked radish-salinity exemplar in which eighteen Petri dishes are assigned to 0, 50, or 100 mM sodium chloride. Each dish contains five seeds. The experimental procedure, measurements, and findings are constructed for teaching, allowing students to inspect the path from research question to analysis without implying that an experiment was conducted.

Question

How does assigned salinity concentration affect mean radicle length after 72 hours?

Assignment

Six independent dishes are assigned to each concentration, with five seed subsamples inside every dish.

Measurement

Record germination for all ninety seeds and radicle length for those that germinate. Calculate dish-level mean length and germination proportion.

Analysis

Compare eighteen dish-level observations. Ninety seed rows do not represent ninety independent assignments of the treatment.

Claim

Interpret any difference within the experimental conditions and distinguish the measured outcomes from an explanation of the biological mechanism.

Two questions hidden in the same dataset

Treatment varies between dishes, while seeds within each dish share its assigned condition and environment. Treating the ninety seed-level rows as independent treatment replicates would exaggerate replication. With only one dish per condition, treatment would be inseparable from differences among those individual dishes. The exemplar therefore uses the eighteen independently assigned dishes for its main comparison.

The two reported outcomes also involve different sets of seeds. Germination proportion includes all five seeds in each dish, whereas mean radicle length is calculated only among those that germinated. As germination proportions change across the constructed conditions, the mean length needs to be read with that selection in mind. The example makes this choice visible before students interpret differences or make claims about why growth may have changed.

The synthetic data document how the design, analysis table, and conclusion can remain connected. They provide no empirical evidence about radish salinity tolerance or students' learning outcomes.

Methods across research designs

The radish example concerns treatment assignment in an experiment, yet the underlying question about what a record can establish extends to other research designs. Without assigned treatments, an observational study must consider selection and potential confounding. Interviews call for decisions about recruitment and consent alongside the interpretation of participants' accounts, while a literature review requires a traceable account of how publications were located and selected.

The comparison below follows that concern across research approaches. A question encountered during analysis may send a student back to the design, just as a draft claim may reveal that the available sources or records cannot support the intended interpretation. The workspace preserves those connections across stages of the project.

ApproachDecision that changes the evidenceQuestion to ask before analysis
ExperimentAssignment of treatments to independent units, including shared trays or classroomsDoes the analysis respect the level of assignment and replication?
Observational studySampling, measurement, and plausible confounding variablesCould an observed association arise from differences that were not measured or controlled?
Interview-based inquiryWho participates, how questions are asked, and how interpretations are documentedWhat supports the interpretation, and how are participant privacy and agency protected?
Evidence reviewSearch sources, eligibility criteria, screening decisions, and record managementCan another reader understand which evidence entered the synthesis and why?

Each approach requires records suited to its claims. In the radish exemplar, the treatment assignments and linked seed-level and dish-level files establish how the analysis units were formed. An interview inquiry would require documentation of recruitment, consent, and the grounds for interpreting participants' accounts. An evidence review would need its search and selection record. The workspace allows students to revisit such choices when writing their conclusions, while leaving them responsible for justifying what the evidence supports.

The eighteen-stage research pathway

Questions about sources, measurements, and analysis arise at different points in a project. The pathway keeps the stages connected so that a decision made during data collection or writing can lead students back to reconsider an earlier choice.

Stages 1–4

Research topic and question

Explore interests and develop a question that can be investigated.

Stages 5–8

Literature and evidence

Search and compare related studies, keeping track of where a claim comes from.

Stages 9–12

Study design

Examine design choices, sampling, measurements, procedures, and ethics.

Stages 13–15

Data analysis

Consider data quality and choose analyses that fit the design and question.

Stages 16–18

Research writing and review

Report the study, trace claims to evidence, and examine what needs revision.

Workspaces for methodological decisions

Within the pathway, separate tools support source comparison, research design, data inspection, and scientific writing. Their prompts invite students to examine their evidence and revise a decision when needed.

Literature Workspace

Record and compare sources, including agreements, differences, and questions that remain.

Methods Lab

Review the match between the research question, proposed measurements, and study design.

Data and Statistics Lab

Work with a local data file, inspect its structure, and consider what analysis is justified.

Scientific Writing Lab

Revisit how methods, results, interpretations, and citations fit together.

Exports, backups, and privacy

Saving an independent copy is useful since students' working notebooks normally remain in browser storage. The application offers a Word-compatible .doc export for reading or sharing and a JSON backup for preserving project data across browsers or devices. Optional Research Chat introduces a different boundary by sending selected project context to a server. Students need to understand what remains in the browser and what they choose to send, while retaining responsibility for checking sources and explaining their methodological decisions.

How the workspace handles guidance and records

Research guidance and students' working notebooks are maintained separately. The public application documentation describes a release process that can update instructional guidance without overwriting a locally stored project. It also describes technical checks and the optional network features discussed above. Those documented safeguards have not been independently audited for privacy or security.

Guidance releases, validation, and recovery

An authenticated Content Studio can publish a validated release of guidance for the eighteen stages. The loader checks six defined fields per stage and rejects incomplete or invalid releases. If updated guidance cannot be loaded, the bundled curriculum remains available, and a prior guidance release can be restored without changing the application version or a student's working notebook.

The documentation describes checks in the browser and server, and reports no collection of research-study data or operational telemetry. The privacy implications of these arrangements depend on their implementation and deployment, alongside the network boundary introduced by optional Research Chat.

A subsequent evaluation could examine whether students notice revisions to guidance and how those changes affect the methodological decisions they revisit.
