Haemoglobin S, malaria, and natural selection

A proposed sequence connecting the biology of haemoglobin with malaria outcomes and population genetics.

Haemoglobin S offers a way to examine how a single genetic variant can have different consequences according to genotype and environment. In the proposed sequence below, students follow its effects from β-globin interactions and red-cell function to malaria outcomes and population-level selection. The work also asks them to consider the evidence and classifications used when discussing human variation.

Molecular and clinical relationships

Haemoglobin transports oxygen in red blood cells. A variant of the β-globin gene, HBB, produces haemoglobin S (HbS). The classic HbS allele involves a single nucleotide substitution that changes glutamic acid to valine at position six of the mature β-globin chain. When oxygen levels are low, deoxygenated HbS can polymerize. Red blood cells may then become less deformable, contributing to haemolysis and obstruction of small blood vessels. The severity and clinical manifestations of sickle cell disease vary and can be influenced by other genetic and physiological factors.

A person with two copies of the HbS allele (HbSS) is at risk of sickle cell disease. People with one HbS and one usual adult β-globin allele (HbAS) generally have sickle cell trait and are often asymptomatic, although trait is not entirely without health risks. In settings where Plasmodium falciparum malaria has been prevalent, HbAS has been associated with substantial protection against severe malaria. That conditional advantage can help maintain an allele whose homozygous state can cause serious illness. This is a classic example of balancing selection through heterozygote advantage.

These differences connect the molecular explanation with natural selection. The consequences of an allele depend on the genotype in which it occurs and the environment in which people live, including exposure to malaria. When those conditions affect survival and reproduction, allele frequencies may change over generations. The HbS allele itself did not arise in anticipation of the disease.

Evidence for genetic and evolutionary claims

Molecular mechanism

Students compare the molecular properties of oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin and explain why a substitution can alter protein interactions. A sequence difference alone does not establish every clinical outcome.

Population-level change

Students distinguish genotype-specific survival from the frequency of an allele in a population. They consider how selection, migration, drift, and changing malaria exposure could affect the distribution.

Geographic patterns and inference

A geographic correlation between historical malaria and an allele's distribution can motivate a hypothesis. It cannot by itself prove the selective mechanism or identify the genotype of a particular person.

To examine these relationships, students would need sources that distinguish molecular mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and population-level claims. An existing HHMI BioInteractive AP Biology storyline offers an instructional example. My proposed sequence draws on the same scientific phenomenon while developing its own tasks for reading evidence and examining the limits of evolutionary explanations.

Malaria outcomes in a cohort study

Published empirical research, distinct from the illustrative fitness model on this page.

Protection associated with HbAS differs according to the malaria outcome being measured. In two cohorts of children on the Kenyan coast, the estimated protection was approximately 50% against mild clinical malaria, 75% against hospital admission for malaria, and almost 90% against severe or complicated malaria (Williams et al., 2005). The investigators reported no reduction in symptomless parasitaemia. These distinctions are essential when interpreting the study, since protection against clinical illness does not establish that infection is prevented or that people with HbAS are immune. As observational comparisons, the estimates also cannot serve directly as selection coefficients.

I would ask students to examine the study record and prepare a short table showing the outcomes that were measured. They could then revise a claim that HbAS confers immunity to malaria, using the study population and comparisons to explain what the evidence supports. This reading would also prepare them for the later selection model. Translating observed illness outcomes into reproductive fitness would require additional information about the population, environmental conditions, and contributions to the next generation.

Source-reading task · Published study

Identify the study population and compare two of its reported malaria outcomes. Explain why protection from clinical illness and the prevalence of symptomless parasitaemia are different endpoints. What would you need to know before using the findings to estimate allele-frequency change across generations? Write a claim supported by this study and a separate sentence specifying what the study alone cannot establish.

The percentages above summarize findings reported in the paper. They are observational findings from the specified cohorts, not universal genotype-specific fitness coefficients or outcomes from my classroom.

Illustrative population-genetics task

Newly written, illustrative assessment. No student responses or outcomes are claimed.

Student-facing free-response extension

In a hypothetical population exposed to falciparum malaria, let the HbA allele frequency be 0.8 and HbS be 0.2. For the initial Hardy–Weinberg calculation, assume random mating and that genotype frequencies have not yet been altered by selection in this model. (a) Determine expected HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotype frequencies before selection. (b) Explain why a selective advantage for HbAS could sustain HbS across generations even when HbSS has serious health consequences. (c) Identify two assumptions you would need to check before using these calculations to explain allele frequencies in a real population. (d) Explain why this model cannot determine the genotype or health status of a person from their racial identity.

Worked solution and interpretation

With p = 0.8 and q = 0.2, p² = 0.64, 2pq = 0.32, and q² = 0.04. Those are expected frequencies at the specified starting point under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions, not measured values and not a prediction of genotype frequencies after selection. A plausible selection explanation must connect differences in survival and reproduction across genotypes to changes in allele frequencies across generations. The strength and direction of selection depend on the environment; the example does not provide fitness coefficients from which to calculate the next generation.

I would read the numerical result alongside the student's biological explanation. The calculation needs to be connected to genotype-dependent survival and reproduction, and the starting Hardy–Weinberg expectations must be distinguished from the later selection step. A mathematically correct table would still leave an important problem unresolved if the explanation treated populations as genetically homogeneous or used race to infer a particular genotype.

Selection across one generation

Constructed fitness values for teaching the calculation. They are not measured survival rates in any human population.

The preceding calculation gives the genotype frequencies expected at the starting point. To examine how selection could change the HbS allele frequency, students need a further assumption about each genotype's contribution to the next generation. For a separate hypothetical exercise, suppose the relative contributions of HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotypes to the breeding population are 0.70, 1.00, and 0.20, respectively. Selection calculation

Normalize each weighted contribution by the total, 0.776. The expected HbS allele frequency among the contributors to the next generation is q′ = [0.320/2 + 0.008]/0.776 = 0.168/0.776 ≈ 0.2165. It increases from 0.20 in this single modeled generation. Assuming random union of gametes, the next zygote genotype frequencies can then be computed from p′ = 1 − q′ and q′. Those zygote frequencies describe a later stage than the weighted genotype contributions shown in the table.

Before interpreting the calculated change in allele frequency, students would need to explain each term in the calculation. The heterozygote contribution is divided by two because only half of its alleles are HbS. They would also need to explain the assumptions underlying the next generation's Hardy–Weinberg proportions and consider how different fitness values could change the direction of selection. Since the numbers are stipulated for this exercise, the result offers neither an estimate of selection strength nor an equilibrium frequency for a real population.

Teacher check. This extension supplies the quantitative selection step promised in meeting three. It can be worked independently before students inspect the calculation and its assumptions.

Human variation and genetic interpretation

Alleles associated with malaria selection occur across populations with different histories, including populations in parts of Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia. Their geographical distributions reflect population histories and environments. Broad racial labels are inadequate substitutes for genetic testing, and allele frequencies describe populations under specified sampling conditions. Teaching this example also requires examining the unsupported assumption that people grouped under one racial category share the same genetic characteristics.

The way human variation is represented in genetics lessons can either reinforce or challenge the assumption that racial groups have uniform biological characteristics (Donovan, 2022). With that concern in mind, I would ask students to examine the scientific claims and categories used in a source while considering how the data were collected. Discussions of disease and ancestry also require attention to privacy. Students could question the classifications without being asked to disclose their identity, genetic status, or family medical history.

The assessment could extend that discussion by asking whether a study's categories are appropriate to its biological claim. Students would use sampling information and the stated evidence to justify their interpretation, preparing them to examine scientific classification through the different response formats described below.

Participation and assessment design

For the molecular task, students could use an annotated model, an explanation in sentences, or a combination of the two. Whichever form they choose, the work would need to connect the nucleotide substitution with altered β-globin interactions and the effects on red-cell properties. This keeps the scientific demand consistent while allowing students to develop the explanation through different representations.

After discussing their initial explanations, students could annotate revisions privately before completing an individual calculation and interpretation. Comparing those responses would allow me to examine which causal relationships they express across the different tasks. Language clarification could support their reading of the instructions while retaining the requirement to justify the biological mechanism.

The discussion of human variation would use published or wholly hypothetical population descriptions. Students would not be asked to connect the example to their own ancestry, health, or family histories. A question challenging the categories in a source would be considered on its scientific merits, with no expectation of personal disclosure.

These are proposed participation and assessment arrangements. They are not observations about how a class responded.
