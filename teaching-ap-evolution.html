AP Biology · Unit 7 natural selection, with Units 5 and 6 connections · Developed curriculum proposal

How does a genetic variant persist?

Haemoglobin, malaria, and the care needed when interpreting human variation.

One allele can have different consequences across genotypes and environments. I would use this problem to connect molecular function, inheritance, natural selection, and the interpretation of evidence. It also requires attention to the categories through which people are represented in science. This is a developed curriculum proposal for my website, informed by established biology and science education scholarship. I have not presented it as an implemented or evaluated classroom unit.

Begin with a biological explanation

Haemoglobin transports oxygen in red blood cells. A variant of the β-globin gene, HBB, produces haemoglobin S (HbS). The classic HbS allele involves a single nucleotide substitution that changes glutamic acid to valine at position six of the mature β-globin chain. When oxygen levels are low, deoxygenated HbS can polymerize. Red blood cells may then become less deformable, contributing to haemolysis and obstruction of small blood vessels. The severity and clinical manifestations of sickle cell disease vary and can be influenced by other genetic and physiological factors.

A person with two copies of the HbS allele (HbSS) is at risk of sickle cell disease. People with one HbS and one usual adult β-globin allele (HbAS) generally have sickle cell trait and are often asymptomatic, although trait is not entirely without health risks. In settings where Plasmodium falciparum malaria has been prevalent, HbAS has been associated with substantial protection against severe malaria. That conditional advantage can help maintain an allele whose homozygous state can cause serious illness. This is a classic example of balancing selection through heterozygote advantage.

The explanation runs across levels. A DNA change affects a protein's interactions; protein behaviour influences red-cell properties; genotypes have different consequences under different environmental conditions; and differential survival and reproduction can change allele frequencies over generations. The environment, relevant pathogen, and genotype all belong in an explanation of selection. The allele did not arise in anticipation of malaria.

What would count as evidence?

Molecular account

Students compare the molecular properties of oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin and explain why a substitution can alter protein interactions. A sequence difference alone does not establish every clinical outcome.

Population account

Students distinguish genotype-specific survival from the frequency of an allele in a population. They consider how selection, migration, drift, and changing malaria exposure could affect the distribution.

Limits of a map

A geographic correlation between historical malaria and an allele's distribution can motivate a hypothesis. It cannot by itself prove the selective mechanism or identify the genotype of a particular person.

To investigate this phenomenon, I would draw on accessible primary or carefully curated scientific sources, compare what each measures, and ask what alternative interpretations remain. HHMI BioInteractive has published an AP Biology storyline on sickle cell and natural selection; it is an existing educator resource, and I would credit it explicitly when adapting a lesson idea. The sequence below is my own proposed organization around scientific explanation and evidence evaluation. Read the published educator example.

A task that asks students to reason

Newly written, illustrative assessment. No student responses or outcomes are claimed.

Student-facing free-response extension

In a hypothetical population exposed to falciparum malaria, let the HbA allele frequency be 0.8 and HbS be 0.2. Assume random mating for the initial Hardy–Weinberg calculation. (a) Determine expected HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotype frequencies before selection. (b) Explain why a selective advantage for HbAS could sustain HbS across generations even when HbSS has serious health consequences. (c) Identify two assumptions you would need to check before using these calculations to explain allele frequencies in a real population. (d) Explain why this model cannot determine the genotype or health status of a person from their racial identity.

Worked interpretation

With p = 0.8 and q = 0.2, p² = 0.64, 2pq = 0.32, and q² = 0.04. Those are expected frequencies at the specified starting point under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions, not measured values and not a prediction of genotype frequencies after selection. A plausible selection explanation must connect differences in survival and reproduction across genotypes to changes in allele frequencies across generations. The strength and direction of selection depend on the environment; the example does not provide fitness coefficients from which to calculate the next generation.

I would assess the numerical result, the genotype-dependent biological mechanism, the distinction between starting expectations and evolutionary change, and the limits placed on claims about identity. A mathematically correct table would remain incomplete if the biological explanation treated populations as homogeneous or presented race as a diagnostic genotype category.

From genotype frequencies to selection

Constructed fitness values for teaching the calculation. They are not measured survival rates in any human population.

The Hardy–Weinberg calculation above supplies a starting genotype distribution. It does not itself model selection. For a separate hypothetical exercise, suppose the relative contributions of HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotypes to the breeding population are 0.70, 1.00, and 0.20, respectively. Work through the next step

Normalize each weighted contribution by the total, 0.776. The expected HbS allele frequency among the contributors to the next generation is q′ = [0.320/2 + 0.008]/0.776 = 0.168/0.776 ≈ 0.2165. It increases from 0.20 in this single modeled generation. Assuming random union of gametes, the next zygote genotype frequencies can then be computed from p′ = 1 − q′ and q′. Those zygote frequencies describe a later stage than the weighted genotype contributions shown in the table.

I would ask students to account for every factor in the calculation before interpreting the result. Why is the heterozygote contribution divided by two when finding the HbS allele frequency? Which assumption makes the next generation's Hardy–Weinberg proportions appropriate? What would have to change in the fitness values for the direction of allele-frequency change to differ? The numbers show how a mechanism could work under stipulated conditions. They provide no estimate of an equilibrium allele frequency or of selection strength in a real population.

Teacher check. This extension supplies the quantitative selection step promised in meeting three. It can be worked independently before students inspect the calculation and its assumptions.

Scientific precision and responsibility

Alleles associated with malaria selection occur across populations with different histories, including populations in parts of Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia. Their geographical distributions reflect population histories and environments. Broad racial labels are inadequate substitutes for genetic testing, and allele frequencies describe populations under specified sampling conditions. Students deserve an account precise enough to resist a familiar error, the assumption that human groups possess uniform biological essences.

Donovan (2022) reviews how genetics instruction can reinforce or challenge genetic essentialism depending on the explanatory material and framing. I would use that work to examine my own representations and the claims permitted by the task. Discussions of disease and ancestry also require care for privacy. No student should be asked to disclose family medical histories, identity, or genetic status. A student may critique the categories in a dataset without being asked to make their life an example.

One question I would keep open for students is whether the categories used in a published study fit its biological claim. That question can lead back into sampling, data provenance, and the scientific limits of the evidence. The class would still need to determine which claims can be supported and which remain uncertain.
