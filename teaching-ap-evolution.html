Haemoglobin S, malaria, and natural selection

The HbS allele can cause serious disease in one genotype while offering protection against severe malaria in another. Following these different outcomes gives students a way to examine how a molecular change can contribute to selection across generations, without treating an allele as uniformly harmful or beneficial.

I developed this proposed sequence to connect β-globin interactions and red-cell function with the evidence used to explain HbS frequencies in populations. The questions change as students move from protein behaviour to clinical observations and then to population models. Each move calls for different evidence, particularly where the available study records do not directly measure reproductive fitness. The lessons and student tasks below are prospective designs, not reports of an implemented intervention.

Molecular and clinical relationships

Haemoglobin transports oxygen in red blood cells. A variant of the β-globin gene, HBB, produces haemoglobin S (HbS). The classic HbS allele involves a single nucleotide substitution that changes glutamic acid to valine at position six of the mature β-globin chain. When oxygen levels are low, deoxygenated HbS can polymerize. Red blood cells may then become less deformable, contributing to haemolysis and obstruction of small blood vessels. The severity and clinical manifestations of sickle cell disease vary and can be influenced by other genetic and physiological factors.

People with two copies of the HbS allele (HbSS) have sickle cell anaemia, a form of sickle cell disease (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). People with one HbS and one usual adult β-globin allele (HbAS) generally have sickle cell trait and are often asymptomatic, although trait is not entirely without health risks. In settings where Plasmodium falciparum malaria has been prevalent, HbAS has been associated with substantial protection against severe malaria. That conditional advantage can help maintain an allele whose homozygous state can cause serious illness, an example of balancing selection through heterozygote advantage.

These genotype-specific outcomes provide the connection to natural selection. Where malaria exposure influences survival and reproduction, the relative contributions of different genotypes can alter allele frequencies over generations. The HbS allele did not arise in response to a population's need for protection, and its consequences cannot be explained without considering the environment in which they occur.

Evidence for genetic and evolutionary claims

Molecular mechanism

Students compare the molecular properties of oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin and explain why a substitution can alter protein interactions. A sequence difference alone does not establish every clinical outcome.

Population-level change

Students distinguish genotype-specific survival from the frequency of an allele in a population. They consider how selection, migration, drift, and changing malaria exposure could affect the distribution.

Geographic patterns and inference

A geographic correlation between historical malaria and an allele's distribution can motivate a hypothesis. It cannot by itself prove the selective mechanism or identify the genotype of a particular person.

An HHMI BioInteractive storyline (opens in a new tab) also connects sickle cell disease with genetics and evolution (van Hoeck, 2025). Here, I focus more narrowly on how students justify the move from observed malaria outcomes to claims about selection, and on which assumptions need to be supplied before an allele-frequency prediction is possible.

Malaria outcomes in a cohort study

Published empirical research, distinct from the illustrative fitness model on this page.

Protection associated with HbAS differs according to the malaria outcome being measured. In two cohorts of children on the Kenyan coast, the estimated protection was approximately 50% against mild clinical malaria, 75% against hospital admission for malaria, and almost 90% against severe or complicated malaria (Williams et al., 2005). The investigators reported no reduction in symptomless parasitaemia. These distinctions are essential when interpreting the study, since protection against clinical illness does not establish that infection is prevented or that people with HbAS are immune. As observational comparisons, the estimates also cannot serve directly as selection coefficients.

The cohort results give students several outcomes to compare, each with a different clinical meaning. They do not report a complete set of genotype-specific survival and reproductive contributions for the population in the later teaching model. To predict an allele-frequency change, students would therefore need further evidence or explicitly stipulated fitness values. Keeping those requirements visible helps separate an observed protective association from the assumptions of an evolutionary calculation.

Source-reading task · Published study

  1. Identify the study population and compare two reported malaria outcomes. Explain why reduced clinical illness and unchanged symptomless parasitaemia describe different endpoints.
  2. Specify what additional information would be needed to estimate a change in HbS allele frequency across generations.
  3. Write one claim supported by this study, followed by a separate statement of what it cannot establish.

The percentages above summarize findings reported in the paper. They are observational findings from the specified cohorts, not universal genotype-specific fitness coefficients or outcomes from my classroom.

Illustrative population-genetics task

Newly written, illustrative assessment. No student responses or outcomes are claimed.

Student-facing free-response extension

In a hypothetical population where falciparum malaria occurs, suppose the starting HbA and HbS allele frequencies are 0.8 and 0.2, respectively. For this calculation, assume that the genotype frequencies initially follow Hardy–Weinberg proportions, before selection.

  1. Determine the expected HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotype frequencies before selection.
  2. Explain why an advantage for HbAS could maintain HbS across generations even though HbSS can cause serious illness.
  3. Identify two assumptions you would need to examine before applying the calculation to a real population.
  4. A student claims that p² = 0.64 means 64% of the population carries at least one HbA allele. Evaluate this interpretation using the calculated genotype frequencies.

Worked solution and interpretation

With p = 0.8 and q = 0.2, p² = 0.64, 2pq = 0.32, and q² = 0.04. These are expected starting frequencies under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions, not observed genotype counts or frequencies after selection. The 0.64 value refers only to HbAA homozygotes. In this model, both HbAA and HbAS carry at least one HbA allele, giving a combined expected frequency of 0.96. Explaining a subsequent change in HbS frequency would require information about genotype-specific survival and reproductive contribution.

I would read students' calculations alongside their explanations of what each frequency represents. A correct answer could still conceal confusion between an allele frequency, a homozygous genotype, and all carriers of an allele. Their answers to the final question would help me locate that difficulty before introducing the separate fitness model.

Selection across one generation

Constructed fitness values for teaching the calculation. They are not measured survival rates in any human population.

The preceding calculation gives the genotype frequencies expected at the starting point. To examine how selection could change the HbS allele frequency, students need a further assumption about each genotype's contribution to the next generation. For a separate hypothetical exercise, suppose the relative contributions of HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotypes to the breeding population are 0.70, 1.00, and 0.20, respectively. Selection calculation

Normalize the weighted contributions by their total of 0.776. The expected HbS allele frequency among gametes contributed to the next generation is q′ = [0.320/2 + 0.008]/0.776 = 0.168/0.776 ≈ 0.2165. It has increased from 0.20 under these model assumptions. With random union of gametes, p′ ≈ 0.7835, giving expected next-zygote frequencies of p′² ≈ 0.6139 (HbAA), 2p′q′ ≈ 0.3392 (HbAS), and q′² ≈ 0.0469 (HbSS). These zygote proportions refer to a later stage than the weighted contributions in the table, which describe the selection step before mating.

The numerator counts HbS alleles in proportion to each genotype's expected reproductive contribution. Half of the contribution from HbAS is assigned to HbS, while all of the HbSS contribution is assigned to it. The numerical increase therefore follows from the invented fitness values and the assumed mating pattern; it is not a prediction for a real population. Different environments and genotype-specific contributions could give a different result.

Human variation and genetic interpretation

Historical surveys and modelling have documented HbS in parts of Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia (Piel et al., 2010). The geographic patterns reflect population histories and past malaria exposure, while the available estimates do not necessarily describe present-day frequencies. Students would therefore need to examine whom a study sampled, when the evidence was collected, and which biological features were measured, particularly when a source also uses racial categories.

Treating racial categories as substitutes for measured genetic variation can reinforce assumptions of biological uniformity within those groups (Donovan, 2022). When evaluating a published comparison, I would ask what each category describes, which genetic variants were actually measured, and whether the sampling supports the conclusion. Students could challenge these classifications through the available evidence without being asked to disclose their own ancestry, health, genotype, or family histories.

Participation and decisions about evidence

Ways to express scientific reasoning and a prospective opportunity to shape subsequent inquiry.

To examine students' scientific reasoning across forms of expression, the molecular task could allow an annotated model, sentences, or both. Whatever the form, their explanation would need to connect the nucleotide substitution to β-globin interactions and changes in red-cell properties. Providing these options would allow students to work through the mechanism in different ways while keeping the scientific relationships under examination consistent.

Students could discuss the source material and annotate revisions before completing an individual calculation and interpretation. Where useful, I would allow clarification of terminology in students' more familiar languages, then examine how they make the required scientific relationships intelligible. Comparing the discussion notes with the individual response would give me a more situated view of what each form of work shows.

Choosing a question for further inquiry

These arrangements would allow students to examine the scientific claims while retaining control over what they share. I also want to consider whether their questions could influence what the class investigates next. After the four proposed meetings, students could compare the published malaria outcomes with the hypothetical selection model, identify a biological relationship that remains uncertain, and propose what evidence might help resolve it. Questions about HbS distribution would call for genetic measurements and information about population histories, while questions about protection would require clearly defined clinical outcomes.

Prospective extension · Selecting evidence for further inquiry

What biological question remains unresolved after examining the published malaria outcomes and the hypothetical selection model? Propose a way to investigate it using published, non-identifying evidence. Explain which populations, variables, or measurements would be relevant and why the categories used in the proposed comparison would be scientifically appropriate. Discuss the different proposals and identify a feasible question for the class to pursue, recording the evidence and reasons behind the decision.

I would use the question students selected to guide a subsequent source comparison or analysis where feasible. Keeping a record of the proposals, reasons for choosing among them, and any changes to the subsequent task would help establish what influence students actually had over the inquiry. Where course requirements or available evidence prevented a proposal from being pursued, those constraints would need to be discussed as part of the decision.
