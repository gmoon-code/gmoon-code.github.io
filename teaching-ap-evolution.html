Haemoglobin S, malaria, and natural selection

An allele associated with a serious inherited disease can persist when people who carry one copy experience an advantage in particular environments. Haemoglobin S offers students a way to follow that relationship from a change in protein structure to selection across generations.

The proposed sequence begins with β-globin interactions and red-cell function, then turns to malaria outcomes and the persistence of HbS in populations. At each stage, students would consider what the evidence can establish. Molecular evidence helps explain protein behaviour, while clinical and population records offer different information about illness and change across generations.

Molecular and clinical relationships

Haemoglobin transports oxygen in red blood cells. A variant of the β-globin gene, HBB, produces haemoglobin S (HbS). The classic HbS allele involves a single nucleotide substitution that changes glutamic acid to valine at position six of the mature β-globin chain. When oxygen levels are low, deoxygenated HbS can polymerize. Red blood cells may then become less deformable, contributing to haemolysis and obstruction of small blood vessels. The severity and clinical manifestations of sickle cell disease vary and can be influenced by other genetic and physiological factors.

People with two copies of the HbS allele (HbSS) have sickle cell anaemia, a form of sickle cell disease (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). People with one HbS and one usual adult β-globin allele (HbAS) generally have sickle cell trait and are often asymptomatic, although trait is not entirely without health risks. In settings where Plasmodium falciparum malaria has been prevalent, HbAS has been associated with substantial protection against severe malaria. That conditional advantage can help maintain an allele whose homozygous state can cause serious illness, an example of balancing selection through heterozygote advantage.

These genotype-specific outcomes provide the connection to natural selection. Where malaria exposure influences survival and reproduction, the relative contributions of different genotypes can alter allele frequencies over generations. The HbS allele did not arise in response to a population's need for protection, and its consequences cannot be explained without considering the environment in which they occur.

Evidence for genetic and evolutionary claims

Molecular mechanism

Students compare the molecular properties of oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin and explain why a substitution can alter protein interactions. A sequence difference alone does not establish every clinical outcome.

Population-level change

Students distinguish genotype-specific survival from the frequency of an allele in a population. They consider how selection, migration, drift, and changing malaria exposure could affect the distribution.

Geographic patterns and inference

A geographic correlation between historical malaria and an allele's distribution can motivate a hypothesis. It cannot by itself prove the selective mechanism or identify the genotype of a particular person.

An HHMI BioInteractive (opens in a new tab) AP Biology storyline also uses the HbS phenomenon. My proposed sequence places particular emphasis on the assumptions involved in moving between forms of evidence, including which clinical outcomes can inform an evolutionary explanation and what additional information that explanation requires.

Malaria outcomes in a cohort study

Published empirical research, distinct from the illustrative fitness model on this page.

Protection associated with HbAS differs according to the malaria outcome being measured. In two cohorts of children on the Kenyan coast, the estimated protection was approximately 50% against mild clinical malaria, 75% against hospital admission for malaria, and almost 90% against severe or complicated malaria (Williams et al., 2005). The investigators reported no reduction in symptomless parasitaemia. These distinctions are essential when interpreting the study, since protection against clinical illness does not establish that infection is prevented or that people with HbAS are immune. As observational comparisons, the estimates also cannot serve directly as selection coefficients.

Comparing the cohort's different malaria outcomes would help students examine why protection against clinical illness does not mean complete immunity. That distinction also affects the evolutionary claim they can make from these findings. To model how the frequency of HbS changes across generations, students would need estimates of genotype-specific contributions through survival and reproduction in a defined environment. The cohort's clinical outcomes offer evidence relevant to that question, while leaving those reproductive fitness values undetermined.

Source-reading task · Published study

Identify the study population and compare two of its reported malaria outcomes. Explain why protection from clinical illness and the prevalence of symptomless parasitaemia are different endpoints. What would you need to know before using the findings to estimate allele-frequency change across generations? Write a claim supported by this study and a separate sentence specifying what the study alone cannot establish.

Illustrative population-genetics task

Newly written, illustrative assessment. No student responses or outcomes are claimed.

Student-facing free-response extension

In a hypothetical population exposed to falciparum malaria, let the HbA allele frequency be 0.8 and HbS be 0.2. For the initial Hardy–Weinberg calculation, assume random mating and that genotype frequencies have not yet been altered by selection in this model. (a) Determine expected HbAA, HbAS, and HbSS genotype frequencies before selection. (b) Explain why a selective advantage for HbAS could sustain HbS across generations even when HbSS has serious health consequences. (c) Identify two assumptions you would need to check before using these calculations to explain allele frequencies in a real population. (d) Explain why this model cannot determine the genotype or health status of a person from their racial identity.

Worked solution and interpretation

With p = 0.8 and q = 0.2, p² = 0.64, 2pq = 0.32, and q² = 0.04. Those are expected frequencies at the specified starting point under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions, not measured values and not a prediction of genotype frequencies after selection. A plausible selection explanation must connect differences in survival and reproduction across genotypes to changes in allele frequencies across generations. The strength and direction of selection depend on the environment; the example does not provide fitness coefficients from which to calculate the next generation.

I would examine the numerical result alongside each student's biological explanation. The starting Hardy–Weinberg expectations must remain separate from the later selection step, which depends on genotype-specific contributions to the next generation. A correct calculation would leave the explanation incomplete if it treated a population as genetically uniform or assumed that a racial label identified an individual's genotype.

Selection calculation

Normalize each weighted contribution by the total, 0.776. The expected HbS allele frequency among the contributors to the next generation is q′ = [0.320/2 + 0.008]/0.776 = 0.168/0.776 ≈ 0.2165. It increases from 0.20 in this single modeled generation. Assuming random union of gametes, the next zygote genotype frequencies can then be computed from p′ = 1 − q′ and q′. Those zygote frequencies describe a later stage than the weighted genotype contributions shown in the table.

Interpreting the change in allele frequency requires students to explain the contributions used in the calculation. The heterozygote term is divided by two because half of an HbAS individual's alleles are HbS. The next generation's expected genotype proportions require an additional assumption of random mating, and different fitness values could lead to different changes in allele frequency. The stipulated values are useful for practising that reasoning, although they cannot estimate selection in a real population.

Human variation and genetic interpretation

HbS and other alleles associated with malaria selection occur in populations with different histories, including parts of Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia. Their distributions reflect population history and environmental conditions. That variation provides a reason for students to examine whom a study sampled and which biological features it actually measured, especially when a source also uses racial categories.

Treating racial categories as substitutes for measured genetic variation can reinforce assumptions of biological uniformity within those groups (Donovan, 2022). I would therefore ask students what each category describes and whether the sampling and measurements justify its use. Because those questions concern ancestry and disease, the discussion also requires care with privacy. Students should be able to challenge a classification through scientific evidence while keeping their own and their families' medical histories private.

An assessment could ask students to evaluate whether a study's categories suit its stated biological question. I would look for an argument grounded in sampling and evidence and invite students to express it through the different formats described below.

Participation and decisions about evidence

Ways to express scientific reasoning and a prospective opportunity to shape subsequent inquiry.

To examine students' scientific reasoning across forms of expression, the molecular task could allow an annotated model, sentences, or both. Whatever the form, their explanation would need to connect the nucleotide substitution to β-globin interactions and changes in red-cell properties. Providing these options would allow students to work through the mechanism in different ways while keeping the scientific relationships under examination consistent.

Students could first discuss and privately annotate revisions, then complete an individual calculation and interpretation. Comparing those responses would help me examine which causal connections they articulate across tasks. Language clarification could support their understanding of the instructions without removing the requirement to explain the science.

For the discussion of human variation, I would use published or wholly hypothetical population descriptions. Students would never be expected to connect the example to their own ancestry, genotype, health, or family history. I would consider questions about the categories on their scientific merits, leaving personal disclosure entirely in students' hands.

Choosing a question for further inquiry

These arrangements would allow students to examine the scientific claims while retaining control over what they share. I also want to consider whether their questions could influence what the class investigates next. After the four proposed meetings, students could compare the published malaria outcomes with the hypothetical selection model, identify a biological relationship that remains uncertain, and propose what evidence might help resolve it. Questions about HbS distribution would call for genetic measurements and information about population histories, while questions about protection would require clearly defined clinical outcomes.

Prospective extension · Selecting evidence for further inquiry

What biological question remains unresolved after examining the published malaria outcomes and the hypothetical selection model? Propose a way to investigate it using published, non-identifying evidence. Explain which populations, variables, or measurements would be relevant and why the categories used in the proposed comparison would be scientifically appropriate. Discuss the different proposals and identify a feasible question for the class to pursue, recording the evidence and reasons behind the decision.

I would use the question students selected to guide a subsequent source comparison or analysis where feasible. Keeping a record of the proposals, reasons for choosing among them, and any changes to the subsequent task would help establish what influence students actually had over the inquiry. Where course requirements or available evidence prevented a proposal from being pursued, those constraints would need to be discussed as part of the decision.
