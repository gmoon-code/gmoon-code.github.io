Evidence Routes and Validity Risk in Claims About Multilingual Learners' Science Understanding in Secondary Science

A preregistered scoping review of how researchers interpret evidence of scientific understanding when students work across languages and forms of expression.

Scoping review · 2026

Why I began this review

In an English-medium science classroom, an explanation can depend on more than whether a student understands the scientific relationships. The question may require particular English terms, a familiar way of reading a graph, or a certain format for showing reasoning. I wanted to understand how studies of multilingual learners account for these conditions when interpreting student responses.

My guiding question concerned the route from evidence to claim. What did students actually produce, what aspect of science were they asked to explain, and what did researchers use to decide what that response demonstrated?

What I examined

Scope

Empirical studies of multilingual learners in secondary science settings, with attention to how scientific understanding was inferred from student work.

Search and selection

I searched ERIC on April 17, 2026. The search retrieved 216 peer-reviewed journal records, of which 20 empirical studies met the final eligibility criteria.

Evidence forms

Written tasks, classroom talk, models, drawings, and other representations of scientific ideas.

Charting

I examined each study's setting, science content, evidence form, judgment procedure, and description of learners' language backgrounds.

Protocol

The review protocol was preregistered with the Open Science Framework.

Why the form of evidence matters

A short written explanation might reveal whether a student can name a scientific process. A diagram may show how that student connects parts of a system. Conversation can make reasoning visible as ideas are developed with other people. Each form also brings its own demands, so researchers need to explain how they move from the response to a claim about understanding.

The studies in this review led me to pay closer attention to those inferential steps, including the scientific relationships being assessed and the language or representational conditions under which students worked.

What I am taking back to teaching

I am more careful now about what a single response can tell me. When I can, I look for opportunities for students to explain, draw, revise, or discuss the same scientific relationship. I still need to judge each response in the context of the task, and I cannot assume the forms of evidence will agree.
