Evidence of multilingual students' science understanding

I examined 20 empirical studies of multilingual learners in secondary science, asking how researchers connected a student's written response, classroom exchange, drawing, or model with a claim about scientific understanding.

Study title · Evidence Routes and Validity Risk in Claims About Multilingual Learners' Science Understanding in Secondary Science: An ERIC-Bounded Scoping Review

On this page Evidence routes A real study Review method

Focus of the review

The studies drew on written answers, classroom conversations, drawings, and models. Each form of work made particular aspects of scientific reasoning available, so I examined the tasks and interpretive criteria used to connect that work with researchers' conclusions.

A drawing, for example, might identify the organisms in an ecological system while a conversation shows how students explain their relationships. Reading the representation alongside the interaction can change what an analyst is able to say. I therefore charted both the scientific idea attributed to a contribution and the evidence used to justify that interpretation.

Forms of evidence in the included studies

The counts below describe the dominant form of evidence in each included study. Some studies used more than one form, which I recorded separately during charting.

8 of 20 studies

Written assessment

Written responses placed the interpretive burden on task design, testing language, and scoring. A limited answer could reflect the target science reasoning, the conditions for expressing it, or the rules used to recognize it.

Worth checking · Did the task give students a reasonable way to express the target concept?

7 of 20 studies

Classroom talk

Interactional studies drew evidence from sequences of explanation, clarification, and uptake. The judgment depended on who could speak, which language resources were available, and whether others recognized and developed the scientific contribution.

Worth checking · Who had an opportunity to contribute, and how did the group respond?

5 of 20 studies

Models and drawings

Drawings and models supported interpretations when their represented entities and relationships could be connected to the scientific construct under examination. The task and criteria still had to establish what features of the artifact counted as disciplinary evidence.

Worth checking · Which parts of the representation count as evidence, and why?

An example from the review

Included study · Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021)

Ecological modelling and language use

In an English-dominant sixth-grade ecology classroom, students developed biosphere models using drawings, physical materials, writing, and discussion. Their work unfolded across activities, allowing the researchers to examine how ecological explanations developed and what role different language resources played (Pierson et al., 2021).

What students expressed

Luis used Spanish and English in a model description that distinguished plants and algae as food for the fish from the oxygen they used to breathe. Carlos contributed drawings and a system of stickers and labels to organize the biosphere.

What the translation changed

When Luis translated the description into English for his peers, his shorter sentence brought algae, energy, and oxygen together without clearly separating eating from breathing. That distinction had been more explicit in his earlier writing.

What happened in the group

Sean said he could not read Spanish and asked his group to use English. The authors discuss this tension alongside the ways students used models, pictures, and talk to explain their ideas to one another.

Luis's original description, its English translation, and the subsequent group conversation made different parts of his ecological explanation available. Examining the sequence helps explain how a shortened translation could leave a previously explicit distinction difficult for peers to see. Read the published study.

Reporting learner and classroom characteristics

These comparisons also depend on what researchers tell readers about the conditions under which student work was produced. Every study in the corpus identified a multilingual population or language arrangement, yet details of the language options and schooling contexts surrounding particular tasks were less consistently reported. That missing context makes it harder to judge whether two contributions were produced under comparable conditions.

I searched ERIC on April 17, 2026, using its peer-reviewed journal and article filters. Eligible reports were empirical studies published from 2010 onward that directly examined multilingual learners' scientific understanding in formal secondary science settings. One of the 79 reports selected for full-text assessment could not be retrieved. I assessed the remaining 78 and included 20 studies.

For each report, I charted the science topic, form of student work, grounds for the interpretation, and language or participation conditions described by the researchers. A second reader independently reviewed a subset of eligibility decisions and codes. I retained source-level decisions and rationales so that the descriptive counts could be checked against the included studies.

Interpretation and limitations

Across the corpus, it was easier to examine an interpretation when the study identified the scientific relationship at issue, described how students could express it, and explained how the response supported the claim. Validity arguments ask for that connection between evidence and interpretation (Kane, 2013). In these studies, the issue became particularly visible when the response format or scoring criteria made only some aspects of a student's reasoning available.

The review was bounded by its ERIC search and its final corpus of 20 studies, most conducted in the United States. I conducted the initial screening and coding, with an independent audit of a smaller subset. These findings describe how the included studies reported and justified their interpretations; they do not estimate the effect of an instructional approach or represent every study of multilingual science education.
