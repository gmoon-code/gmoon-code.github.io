Evidence of multilingual students' science understanding

I examined 20 empirical studies of multilingual learners in secondary science to understand how researchers moved from students' work to claims about what they understood. Written answers, classroom conversations, and models offered different kinds of evidence. Reading the studies together raised a further question about what each form of evidence allowed a researcher to conclude.

Focus of the review

I began with the particular scientific relationship a study claimed its students could explain. I then examined the work used to support that interpretation and the steps connecting the two. With a written response, the task and criteria could determine which relationships students were invited to make explicit. In classroom talk, an explanation might develop across several speakers and through drawings or gestures that a transcript alone could obscure. I charted the language arrangements and participation conditions reported alongside these records, attending to what a reader would need in order to evaluate the interpretation.

Forms of evidence in the included studies

Each of the 20 studies appears once below, according to the form of student work most central to its analysis. I also recorded other evidence forms used within the same study.

8 of 20 studies

Written and constructed responses

Assessment questions and scoring criteria shaped which scientific relationships a student could be expected to express. When an answer was brief, the report needed to show how the task's language demands and judgment rules supported the interpretation being made.

Worth checking · Did the task give students a reasonable way to express the target concept?

7 of 20 studies

Classroom talk

Researchers followed ideas as students proposed, clarified, or revised them in conversation. Whether an explanation became part of the group's work also depended on how peers and teachers responded, and on the linguistic resources students could use.

Worth checking · Who had an opportunity to contribute, and how did the group respond?

5 of 20 studies

Models and drawings

Models could make relations visible through the position, movement, or transformation of represented entities. Researchers needed to explain how those features expressed the science under investigation, rather than assume that producing a model demonstrated understanding.

Worth checking · Which parts of the representation count as evidence, and why?

The categories represent ways of supporting research claims, not separate learner populations or estimates of instructional effectiveness. I also charted the inferential pathways through which researchers interpreted task responses, followed the uptake or revision of ideas in interaction, or assessed relationships expressed through representations.

An example from the review

Included study · Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021)

Ecological modelling and language use

Pierson and colleagues studied a nine-week ecology unit in an English-dominant sixth-grade STEM classroom. In one group, Luis, Carlos, and Sean used written descriptions, drawings, physical materials, and conversation to develop a biosphere model. Following these contributions across activities allowed the researchers to examine how scientific relationships became visible through different linguistic and representational resources (Pierson et al., 2021).

Earlier accounts and models

The records show how these forms of expression contributed different information. Luis's mixed Spanish-English description distinguished plants and algae as food for the fish from oxygen used in breathing, while Carlos organized the biosphere through drawings, stickers, and labels. The group’s original biosphere plan (Figure 3 in the published article) (opens in a new tab) shows how these resources were arranged together.

Interpretive significance · The earlier description made two ecological relationships explicit, and the model offered another form in which students could organize their ideas.

An English rendering

Luis subsequently translated his description into a shorter English sentence that joined algae, energy, and oxygen without clearly distinguishing food from breathing.

Interpretive significance · This version expressed less of the ecological relationship. Reading it without the earlier bilingual account could make an omission appear to be a limit of the student's understanding.

Negotiating language use

Sean said he could not read the Spanish text and asked his peers to use English. The researchers also note that the teachers did not notice this language policing as it occurred.

Interpretive significance · Whether an idea could enter the group's discussion was shaped by how peers responded to its language and representation. The exchange reveals a participation condition without establishing which ecological relationships the group ultimately adopted.

The distinction between what Luis expressed in his bilingual description and what appeared in the English version becomes visible only when the records are read together. The group exchange adds another consideration about whose contributions could enter discussion. These are my interpretations of the published study (opens in a new tab), paraphrased rather than presented as verbatim participant accounts.

Reporting language and schooling conditions

The example draws attention to information that was not consistently available across the wider corpus. All 20 reports identified a multilingual population or language arrangement, but only 14 made the language condition of the focal task at least partly explicit. Fewer described programme placement or newcomer status. These omissions limited what I could establish about the circumstances under which students were asked to demonstrate scientific understanding.

Of the 216 records screened, 137 were excluded by title and abstract. One of the remaining 79 reports could not be retrieved in full text; 58 of the 78 assessed reports were excluded after eligibility review. The final 20 met the inclusion criteria. No duplicate records were identified within the ERIC search results.

I searched ERIC on April 17, 2026, with the peer-reviewed and journal-article filters applied. The publication-year boundary of 2010 onward was enforced during eligibility screening rather than as a search filter. I included empirical studies of formal secondary science education that directly examined multilingual learners' scientific understanding, including claims made through explanation, modelling, argumentation, or assessment. The initial ERIC corpus was not intended to be a comprehensive search across every education database.

For each included report, I charted the science domain, primary evidence form, inferential pathway, and point at which missing or ambiguous evidence posed the greatest risk to the interpretation. I also recorded the language and participation conditions described by the researchers. A second reader independently audited a subset of eligibility decisions and coding. The source-level ledger preserved decisions and rationales, including secondary routes where an article relied on more than one form of evidence.

Interpretation and limitations

Validity risk enters when a claim about scientific understanding depends on relationships the available record does not establish. A brief response, for example, might be taken to show an absence of understanding when the prompt never required the missing relationship. In an exchange, the proposal of an explanation does not necessarily show that other participants understood or used it. Kane's argument-based account of validity helped me examine the assumptions connecting evidence to interpretations (Kane, 2013). Applied here, that perspective calls for attention to the task, the scientific construct, and the conditions under which students contributed.

Fourteen of the 20 studies took place in the United States, and the review drew on one database. The remaining studies covered relatively few national and schooling contexts, limiting meaningful comparisons across settings. I conducted the primary screening and charting, with a second reader auditing a subset. These results map the evidentiary practices of the included studies. They cannot establish how frequently the same practices occur across the wider literature or whether any particular instructional approach improves learning.
