Evidence of multilingual students' science understanding

I examined 20 empirical studies of multilingual learners in secondary science. The question was how researchers decided that a written answer, classroom exchange, drawing, or model demonstrated a particular scientific idea.

Study title · Evidence Routes and Validity Risk in Claims About Multilingual Learners' Science Understanding in Secondary Science: An ERIC-Bounded Scoping Review

On this page Evidence routes A real study Review method

Focus of the review

The included studies used written responses, classroom interactions, drawings, and models to examine scientific understanding. I considered what each task made available for interpretation and how the researchers justified the scientific relationships they attributed to students' work.

For each study, I examined the student work, the scientific relationships identified by the researchers, and the reasoning connecting that evidence to their interpretation.

Forms of evidence in the included studies

The counts below describe the dominant form of evidence in each included study. Some studies used more than one form, which I recorded separately during charting.

8 of 20 studies

Written assessment

Written responses placed the interpretive burden on task design, testing language, and scoring. A limited answer could reflect the target science reasoning, the conditions for expressing it, or the rules used to recognise it.

Worth checking · Did the task give students a reasonable way to express the target concept?

7 of 20 studies

Classroom talk

Interactional studies drew evidence from sequences of explanation, clarification, and uptake. The judgment depended on who could speak, which language resources were available, and whether others recognised and developed the scientific contribution.

Worth checking · Who had an opportunity to contribute, and how did the group respond?

5 of 20 studies

Models and drawings

Drawings and models supported interpretations when their represented entities and relationships could be connected to the scientific construct under examination. The task and criteria still had to establish what features of the artifact counted as disciplinary evidence.

Worth checking · Which parts of the representation count as evidence, and why?

An example from the review

Included study · Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021)

Ecological modelling and language use

In an English-dominant sixth-grade ecology classroom, students created biosphere models using drawings, physical objects, writing, and talk. Pierson and colleagues followed how these representations and students' language choices shaped the scientific meaning available to their group.

What students expressed

Luis used Spanish and English in a model description that distinguished plants and algae as food for the fish from the oxygen they used to breathe. Carlos contributed drawings and a system of stickers and labels to organize the biosphere.

What the translation changed

When Luis translated the description into English for his peers, his shorter sentence brought algae, energy, and oxygen together without clearly separating eating from breathing. That distinction had been more explicit in his earlier writing.

What happened in the group

Sean said he could not read Spanish and asked his group to use English. The authors discuss this tension alongside the ways students used models, pictures, and talk to explain their ideas to one another.

The original description, its translation, and the group's exchanges offered different evidence to interpret. Attending to all three helped the authors examine how scientific meaning was expressed and recognized in this particular classroom. Read Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021) ↗

Reporting learner and classroom characteristics

The biosphere example also raises a question about what researchers know of the students whose work they interpret. All 20 studies named a multilingual learner population or classroom language arrangement, but their descriptions often gave too little detail to compare students' experiences of a particular task. Fewer reports specified the circumstances that could help a reader understand the performance.

Search and charting methods

I searched ERIC on April 17, 2026, using peer-reviewed journal and article filters, and retained empirical studies published from 2010 onward that directly examined multilingual learners' science understanding in formal secondary science settings. One report could not be retrieved at the full-text stage. The remaining 78 were assessed, and 20 met the final criteria.

I charted the science topic, the form of student work, the reasoning connecting that work to an interpretation, and the language or participation conditions described in each report. An independent reader reviewed a subset of inclusion decisions and codes. I kept the source-level decisions and their rationales together so the descriptive counts could be checked against the studies.

Interpretation and limitations

Across the included studies, the evidence was easier to interpret when researchers named the science relationship being assessed, described how students could express it, and explained the judgments connecting the work to a claim. These conditions are part of examining the validity of an interpretation, as discussed by Kane (2013).

The search was limited to ERIC and the final corpus contained 20 studies, most from the United States. A single reviewer conducted the primary screening and coding, with a smaller independent audit. The synthesis does not estimate the effect of a teaching intervention or describe every study of multilingual science learning.
