ERIC-bounded scoping review · 2026

What makes student work evidence of science understanding?

I examined 20 empirical studies of multilingual learners in secondary science. The question was how researchers decided that a written answer, classroom exchange, drawing, or model demonstrated a particular scientific idea.

Study title · Evidence Routes and Validity Risk in Claims About Multilingual Learners' Science Understanding in Secondary Science: An ERIC-Bounded Scoping Review

On this page Evidence routes A real study Review method

What I was looking for

A student may use English terms in a written answer, build an explanation while talking with classmates, or show the relationship between processes in a drawing. Each response offers something a researcher can examine. The interpretation still depends on knowing what the student was asked to do and how the response was judged.

I read the studies with three linked questions in mind. What was the student work? Which scientific relationship did the researchers say it revealed? What connected the two?

Three routes from student work to a claim

The counts below describe the dominant form of evidence in each included study. Some studies used more than one form, which I recorded separately during charting.

8 of 20 studies

Written assessment

Students responded to prompts or assessment items. Researchers had to connect the question's scientific demand, permitted language, response format, and scoring decisions to the claim.

Worth checking · Did the task give students a reasonable way to express the target concept?

7 of 20 studies

Classroom talk

Scientific meaning developed during interaction, often through questions, clarification, and revisions. The sequence and other people's responses affected what became available to interpret.

Worth checking · Who had an opportunity to contribute, and how did the group respond?

5 of 20 studies

Models and drawings

Researchers examined features of visual representations. The important judgment concerned whether those features carried the entities, processes, or relationships in the scientific claim.

Worth checking · Which parts of the representation count as evidence, and why?

One study from the review

Included study · Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021)

Reading an ecological model across languages

In an English-dominant sixth-grade ecology classroom, students created biosphere models using drawings, physical objects, writing, and talk. Pierson and colleagues followed how these representations and students' language choices shaped the scientific meaning available to their group.

What students made

In one group, students drew and labeled parts of an ecosystem. One student's combined Spanish and English description distinguished what fish ate from the oxygen they used to breathe.

What changed in translation

A shorter English rendering brought food, energy, algae, and oxygen together without preserving the distinction between eating and breathing as clearly. Judging only that rendering could miss scientific detail in the earlier work.

What else affected the interpretation

The models developed through shared activity. Participation was uneven at times, and a peer sometimes felt excluded during Spanish-language discussion. The group interaction also mattered to the evidence.

The study does not establish that every multilingual response is more scientifically developed. It gives a specific instance in which the language and form of a response affected what a researcher could recognize. Read the study by Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021) ↗

A second difficulty, describing the learners

All 20 studies named a multilingual learner population or classroom language arrangement. Their descriptions were often too broad to compare students' experiences of a particular task. Fewer reports specified the circumstances that could help a reader understand the performance.

I searched ERIC on April 17, 2026, using peer-reviewed journal and article filters, and retained empirical studies published from 2010 onward that directly examined multilingual learners' science understanding in formal secondary science settings. One report could not be retrieved at the full-text stage. The remaining 78 were assessed, and 20 met the final criteria.

I charted the science topic, the form of student work, the reasoning connecting that work to an interpretation, and the language or participation conditions described in each report. An independent reader reviewed a subset of inclusion decisions and codes. I kept the source-level decisions and their rationales together so the descriptive counts could be checked against the studies.

What this review can tell us

Across the included studies, the evidence was easier to interpret when researchers named the science relationship being assessed, described how students could express it, and explained the judgments connecting the work to a claim. These conditions are part of examining the validity of an interpretation, as discussed by Kane (2013).

The search was limited to ERIC and the final corpus contained 20 studies, most from the United States. A single reviewer conducted the primary screening and coding, with a smaller independent audit. The synthesis does not estimate the effect of a teaching intervention or describe every study of multilingual science learning.
