Evidence of multilingual students' science understanding

I examined 20 empirical studies of multilingual learners in secondary science, asking how researchers connected a student's written response, classroom exchange, drawing, or model with a claim about scientific understanding.

Study title · Evidence Routes and Validity Risk in Claims About Multilingual Learners' Science Understanding in Secondary Science: An ERIC-Bounded Scoping Review

On this page Evidence routes A real study Review method

Focus of the review

For each study, I traced the scientific relationships researchers attributed to students and the records used to justify those interpretations. The form of evidence shaped what each interpretation could support. For a written response, the question and scoring criteria helped establish which scientific relationships the student had been asked to express. An account of classroom interaction required following how an explanation developed across speakers, drawings, and other representations. I also examined the language and participation conditions reported for each task, since those details affect how readers can assess the claim.

Forms of evidence in the included studies

The counts below describe the dominant form of evidence in each included study. Some studies used more than one form, which I recorded separately during charting.

8 of 20 studies

Written assessment

When researchers interpret written responses, the question, language demands, and scoring criteria help determine what the answer can establish. A limited response may leave uncertainty about the student's reasoning, the opportunity to express it, or the rules used to recognize it.

Worth checking · Did the task give students a reasonable way to express the target concept?

7 of 20 studies

Classroom talk

In interactional studies, an explanation often developed through successive contributions and requests for clarification. Interpreting that development required attention to who could speak, the language resources available, and how others responded to the scientific idea.

Worth checking · Who had an opportunity to contribute, and how did the group respond?

5 of 20 studies

Models and drawings

Drawings and models supported interpretations when their represented entities and relationships could be connected to the scientific construct under examination. The task and criteria still had to establish what features of the artifact counted as disciplinary evidence.

Worth checking · Which parts of the representation count as evidence, and why?

An example from the review

Included study · Pierson, Clark, and Brady (2021)

Ecological modelling and language use

In an English-dominant sixth-grade ecology classroom, students developed biosphere models using drawings, physical materials, writing, and discussion. Their work unfolded across activities, allowing the researchers to examine how ecological explanations developed and what role different language resources played (Pierson et al., 2021).

Earlier accounts and models

The records show how these forms of expression contributed different information. Luis's Spanish and English description distinguished plants and algae as food for the fish from the oxygen used in breathing, while Carlos organized the biosphere through drawings, stickers, and labels.

Interpretive significance · The earlier description made two ecological relationships explicit, and the model offered another form in which students could organize their ideas.

The subsequent translation

Luis's shorter English sentence brought algae, energy, and oxygen together without clearly separating eating from breathing.

Interpretive significance · The scientific distinction was less explicit in this version. The translation alone cannot establish whether Luis no longer understood the relationship he had expressed earlier.

Language choices in the group

Sean said he could not read Spanish and asked the group to use English. The researchers discuss that exchange alongside students' use of drawings, models, and talk.

Interpretive significance · Reading the exchange with the earlier records helps distinguish a contribution from the conditions under which peers could access it. This episode alone does not establish which relationships the group ultimately adopted.

Reading the earlier bilingual description alongside the English sentence and the group exchange changes the basis for interpreting Luis's contribution. Each record reveals something about his scientific explanation and the conditions under which classmates could use it. Reporting learner and classroom characteristics

The differences among Luis's contributions also show why the conditions surrounding a task are relevant to interpreting the resulting evidence. All 20 reports identified a multilingual population or language arrangement, although they varied in how fully they described the language options and schooling contexts surrounding particular tasks. Where these details were missing, readers had less basis for examining the opportunities students had to express and develop their scientific ideas.

I searched ERIC on April 17, 2026, using its peer-reviewed journal and article filters. Eligible reports were empirical studies published from 2010 onward that directly examined multilingual learners' scientific understanding in formal secondary science settings. One of the 79 reports selected for full-text assessment could not be retrieved. I assessed the remaining 78 and included 20 studies.

For each report, I charted the science topic, form of student work, grounds for the interpretation, and language or participation conditions described by the researchers. In the Pierson example above, this meant attending separately to the relationships expressed in the earlier description, the shorter translation, and the subsequent group exchange. The same charting questions guided the reading of other included reports, even when their available evidence took a different form. A second reader independently reviewed a subset of eligibility decisions and codes. I retained source-level decisions and rationales so that the descriptive counts could be checked against the included studies.

Interpretation and limitations

A validity risk arises when an interpretation of students' scientific understanding extends beyond what the reported records can support. For instance, a brief answer may be treated as evidence that a student lacks an entire scientific relationship, even though the prompt or scoring rules made only part of that relationship available. A classroom exchange may show that an explanation was proposed without establishing how others interpreted or used it. The included reports varied in how clearly they connected claims with tasks, student contributions, and language conditions. Examining those connections draws on the justification of interpretations central to validity arguments (Kane, 2013).

The review was bounded by its ERIC search and its final corpus of 20 studies, most conducted in the United States. I conducted the initial screening and coding, with an independent audit of a smaller subset. These findings describe how the included studies reported and justified their interpretations; they do not estimate the effect of an instructional approach or represent every study of multilingual science education.
