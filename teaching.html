Teaching science

I teach subjects where a short answer often rests on a long chain of reasoning. A student can know the names of the molecules in a process or complete a calculation and still need time to work out how the parts connect.

I am interested in what our explanations, diagrams, laboratory work, and assessment questions ask students to make visible. I also care about whose questions we make room for, which scientific assumptions a class can examine, and how students participate in deciding what deserves further investigation.

I've developed two curriculum studies here. The Biology example connects human genetic variation with evolution and careful interpretation of evidence. The Chemistry example examines equilibrium and environmental questions. Both are prospective teaching designs. A separate account documents what I actually examined in classroom assessment.

Planning across a unit

I usually begin by identifying the relationships that a student must be able to explain at the end of a sequence. In the haemoglobin study, this means moving from a specific protein variant through genotype-dependent consequences to population change. In equilibrium, it means understanding why a mixture changes composition when its reaction quotient differs from its equilibrium constant.

The next question is what evidence would make those relationships visible. A labelled diagram could reveal the location of a process, while a written explanation can show how a student connects one step to the next. A numerical result can be correct even when the explanation of what the number means is incomplete. Each representation offers a different opportunity to examine the science.

Time is part of the design. A laboratory investigation requires preparation, safe procedures, data collection, and time to examine the results. An assessment correction needs space to consider feedback and try a related explanation again. I try to reserve opportunities to revisit a question after its first appearance, while recognizing that AP coursework also places demands on pacing and examination preparation.

Questions I check before a sequence

  1. Which relationships must be explained, beyond recalling terms?
  2. What prior knowledge and representations will students need?
  3. What would an incomplete explanation look like?
  4. What evidence can I collect before the next major assessment?
  5. Where can students question the task or the model itself?

The examples on the two case pages show specific schedules. Both flagship sequences are proposed designs, with their evidence and assumptions stated. My separate AP Biology assessment inquiry documents an actual classroom correction and reconstruction procedure.

What I learned from examining assessment records

My classroom inquiry led me to look at different kinds of post-assessment evidence separately. With feedback in front of them, students can often identify a missing term or causal step. Their preparation records describe what they say they did afterward. A reconstruction completed without those resources offers another view of the mechanism they can articulate under that task's conditions. A subsequent unit assessment asks for reasoning about a different biological system.

In my AP Biology study, I added a closed-resource reconstruction after reviewing what the original correction routine allowed me to infer. I compared it with the earlier answer to the same bentazon question. Some students extended pathways, while others omitted relationships they had written before. Those differences helped me develop targeted follow-up questions. The use and effects of those subsequent questions remain to be examined.

Black and Wiliam (2009) develop an account of formative assessment in which evidence becomes useful through the decisions and actions that follow it. That raises a practical test for my own feedback. Does it help identify a relationship worth discussing, another question worth asking, or an aspect of the prompt that needs revision? The documented teaching inquiry explains the scientific question, original coding criteria, and subsequent assessment decisions. My research page provides the linked records and their limitations.

Who helps decide where an inquiry goes?

Teaching advanced science involves substantial disciplinary responsibility. I need to teach concepts accurately, assess claims against evidence, and keep the course coherent. I am also learning to examine the ways teachers, textbooks, and examination structures determine which examples appear and which questions are taken seriously.

For a chemistry unit about nitrogen compounds, a discussion might develop toward environmental monitoring or agricultural use. The scientific work would require careful measurement and credible sources. Students could also ask which places to investigate and which affected communities should have a voice in defining the problem. Morales-Doyle (2017) offers an account of justice-centred advanced chemistry teaching that takes both scientific learning and community concerns seriously. I am interested in learning from this scholarship as I consider my own curricular decisions.

That interest also informs how I approach biological categories and examples. A representation of sex determination or human variation can be scientifically useful while leaving important complexity unresolved. Student questions about those boundaries deserve scientific attention and care for privacy. I cannot infer anyone's identity from what they ask. The related questions about knowledge, authority, and justice are developed further on my Research page.
