Teaching science

In biology and chemistry, I pay close attention to the relationships students include in their explanations. A response may contain the correct terms or numerical answer while leaving part of the reasoning unclear. Reading those responses carefully helps me decide what to ask next and which connections may need further attention.

Students often develop their thinking across diagrams, calculations, discussion, and writing. Each offers different evidence of what they understand, so I try to consider the task and the form of the response before drawing a conclusion. Their questions can also bring me back to explanations I have taught many times, especially when they ask about a relationship I had passed over too quickly. I want to preserve the scientific demands of the lesson while giving those questions room to develop.

The pages below include an AP Biology assessment inquiry that changed the questions I asked after correction, two curriculum sequences I have designed, and examples of how I support students as they develop their own research.

Classroom action research and assessment

Documented AP Biology assessment inquiry · 28 students

My AP Biology action research grew from examining the relationships students expressed in assessments and subsequent corrections. Their feedback-assisted reflections, later explanations, and independent reconstructions sometimes contained different causal connections, which made the conditions of each task relevant to my interpretation. In particular, I needed to distinguish what students could explain while consulting their earlier work from what they could reconstruct without it.

That uncertainty led me to add an individual reconstruction after students corrected the Unit 3 photosynthesis question. Some of the ten paired records contained additional relationships, while others omitted connections present in the original responses. Comparing the two answers helped me identify more specific questions for students, although the effects of those follow-ups remain unexamined. The teaching case develops the biological mechanism and instructional decisions, while the action research account documents the linked evidence and its limitations.

This experience has also made me more attentive to the decisions that follow an assessment. Formative assessment involves interpreting evidence and using it to inform subsequent teaching and learning (Black & Wiliam, 2009). When a student leaves a causal relationship unstated, the written response alone may not establish whether the connection is unavailable to them or was simply omitted. I may therefore ask them to explain that step in words or through a diagram, using the additional response to decide what kind of feedback or instruction would be useful.

Curriculum and assessment planning

When planning a unit, I begin with the explanation, calculation, or investigation students will eventually undertake. I then consider the knowledge and intermediate relationships they will need to work toward it, including places where an otherwise plausible inference may remain incomplete. This helps me choose the questions, representations, and activities that prepare students for the scientific work.

The evidence I gather during those activities needs the same care. A correct numerical answer may accompany an incorrect interpretation, while a diagram may locate structures without explaining how they interact. Looking at what each task permits me to infer helps me decide whether students need another opportunity to explain, a different representation, or further instruction.

Those decisions also take place within the practical limits of a school timetable. Laboratory activities require preparation, time for measurements, and opportunities to examine uncertainty. Students also need an occasion to use feedback in subsequent work. I consider these requirements alongside the pace of the Advanced Placement curriculum and its assessment schedule.

Questions I check before a sequence

  1. Which relationships must be explained, beyond recalling terms?
  2. What prior knowledge and representations will students need?
  3. What would an incomplete explanation look like?
  4. What evidence can I collect before the next major assessment?
  5. Where can students question the task or the model itself?

Mentoring independent student research

Alongside my science courses, I supervise secondary students completing capstone and independent research and review proposals and presentations as a faculty member. Their projects involve decisions about the question, methods, evidence, and conclusions that are often predetermined in a classroom investigation. I work with students to examine those decisions while keeping the scope of a study realistic for the time and resources available.

Our discussions commonly begin with what a student hopes to investigate and whether their proposed observations or measurements can answer that question. From there, we consider relevant literature, research design, analysis, and the claims the eventual report could support. I want students to explain and defend their own choices, including the limitations they recognize as their work develops.

The Research Methods Studio provides a structured place to revisit similar decisions while working on a project. It complements the more specific conversations that arise in supervision, where each investigation brings its own questions about evidence and feasibility.

Additional teaching materials

Air quality and school decision-making

Cross-disciplinary lesson design · Developed for graduate coursework · Not evaluated in a classroom

I designed a lesson in which students would use local PM2.5 measurements from AirKorea to consider what a school should do on high-pollution days. The planned task connects the chemistry of airborne particle mixtures and filtration with respiratory biology. Students would compare response options using the available evidence, feasibility, and uncertainty, then prepare a short school action brief. The task ends with students explaining which school actions their evidence supports and where additional measurements would be needed.

Osmosis and mass-change data

The osmosis activity uses hypothetical mass-change data for practice with percentage calculations and explanations of water movement. Students also consider which aspects of membrane transport the measurements can support and which require further evidence.
