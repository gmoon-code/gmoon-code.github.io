Teaching science

I teach Advanced Placement Biology, Advanced Placement Chemistry, and life science in an English-medium international school in South Korea. My students bring different experiences with science, English, and the ways school asks them to explain what they know. I am still learning how to make my lessons responsive to those differences.

In my classroom

Students work with experiments, graphs, diagrams, models, scientific reading, and written explanations. I try to give them opportunities to revisit an idea after feedback and to explain how the parts of a process fit together.

What a lesson can look like

We often begin by returning to an earlier idea through a short knowledge check. Reading a science text together gives us a chance to talk through unfamiliar language. Students then use the remaining time to investigate a question, revise a model, analyze data, or continue a project. I provide guided notes and other resources so students can prepare before class and return to difficult material afterward.

In biology and chemistry, I try to ask students to explain relationships. Naming a process is useful, and I also want to know whether students can trace what happens before and after it. Their diagrams, explanations, and questions help me see where an additional example or another kind of representation may be needed.

Teaching across languages

One conversation about axolotls has stayed with me. A student moved between English and the Korean words 우파루파 and 재생 while discussing regeneration. The exchange made me pay closer attention to the ideas students may be working with as they search for words. I try to make room for clarification and for different ways of representing a scientific relationship, while helping students develop the English they need for their courses.

I cannot always tell from a short response whether the difficulty comes from the science, the language of the task, or the format of the explanation. I find it useful to ask another question, look at a model, or invite a student to explain their thinking again before making a judgment.

Curriculum and assessment

I develop and adapt materials using the Advanced Placement Course and Exam Descriptions and the Next Generation Science Standards. Students encounter laboratory work, data interpretation, diagrams, simulations, and writing tasks. When assessments reveal recurring gaps in causal explanations, I revisit the sequence of ideas and look for ways students can practice explaining the connections between them.

My classroom action research grew from these decisions. Following students' reflections and later explanations across assessments helped me examine what a correction could show and what I still needed to ask.

Working with student researchers

I also support secondary students as they develop their own research projects. We work through finding a manageable question, reading related studies, choosing methods, thinking about ethics and measurements, and explaining results carefully. I encourage students to ask what their data can answer and where their evidence stops.

When a student has an ambitious idea, I enjoy helping them find a smaller question they can investigate well. Their choices and interpretations remain their own, and I am grateful for the questions they bring that I had not considered.
