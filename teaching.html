Teaching science

A student can arrive at the expected answer while leaving an important scientific relationship unexplained. In my biology and chemistry classes, I attend to how students connect processes, interpret evidence, and return to an explanation after feedback. I also need to consider what a single response cannot tell me, especially when later work differs from an earlier answer.

This page brings together a documented classroom inquiry, curriculum designs developed for this website, and my approach to supervising independent research. Across these settings, I consider what students can reasonably conclude from their work and how their questions can influence what happens next.

Classroom action research and assessment

My AP Biology action research examined responses to a question about bentazon inhibiting photosynthetic electron transport. After feedback-assisted correction, all 28 students independently reconstructed the mechanism without access to their earlier answers or notes. I later compared ten reconstructions with the corresponding original explanations. Five extended an earlier causal pathway, two did not repeat an earlier contradiction, two omitted a relationship previously expressed, and one remained underdeveloped in both responses.

The differences gave me more specific questions to ask, but they did not demonstrate a change in understanding. The reconstruction prompt requested intermediate steps more explicitly than the original, and the comparison could not isolate the effect of feedback. I therefore retained the earlier explanations when interpreting what the later responses might mean. The action research account describes the analysis and its limitations; the AP Biology teaching case examines the mechanism and develops follow-up questions that were proposed after the inquiry, rather than administered or evaluated within it.

Curriculum proposals

The following sequences are newly designed teaching examples, not reports of implemented lessons. Each sets out a disciplinary problem, the evidence students would examine, and decisions I would make in response to their explanations.

Haemoglobin variation, malaria, and natural selection

This case follows the HbS allele through changes in haemoglobin function, clinical outcomes, and selection across generations. Students compare findings from a published malaria study with a deliberately hypothetical population-genetics model. They also examine why racial categories cannot identify an individual's genotype, then consider which unanswered question the class might investigate further.

Protein→Genotype and environment→Population change

Four illustrative 50-minute meetings, with a source-reading task, worked allele-frequency calculations, and a student-proposed question for further inquiry.

Connecting reaction quotient, equilibrium, and evidence

A worked ammonia-equilibrium problem begins with a reaction quotient below the equilibrium constant. Students then connect the predicted direction of change with continuing forward and reverse reactions. A compression problem asks them to distinguish the immediate change in partial pressures from the mixture's subsequent adjustment.

Reaction rates↔Q and K↔Predicted change

Curriculum and assessment planning

When planning an assessment, I first consider what a response will allow me to infer. A calculation may show whether a student can use an equation while revealing little about how they interpret its result. An annotated model might make a relationship visible that remains difficult to express in prose, although the model alone may leave the student's reasoning ambiguous. Those differences affect which response formats I offer and what I need to ask afterward.

Lesson planning involves working backward from the explanation or investigation students will eventually undertake. I identify prerequisite relationships, decide which representations or observations might help students examine them, and anticipate where an incomplete explanation could arise. For dynamic equilibrium, for example, students need to distinguish reaction rates from the quantities present in a mixture before they can explain why equilibrium remains dynamic. A later question can then examine whether they carry that distinction into a different situation.

Time and available evidence limit what a single lesson can establish. A laboratory investigation may need another meeting for checking measurements or considering uncertainty, while an assessment response may call for a shorter follow-up question rather than reteaching an entire mechanism. I try to use those decisions to inform subsequent instruction, consistent with the attention to evidence and responsive teaching developed by Black and Wiliam (2009).

Questions I use when planning

  1. What should students be able to explain, beyond naming the right result?
  2. Which prerequisite relationships might remain uncertain?
  3. What could a correct answer conceal?
  4. What evidence would help me decide on the next question or lesson?
  5. Where could a student's question change the direction of the inquiry?

Mentoring independent student research

Alongside my AP courses, I supervise secondary students' capstone and independent research projects. Much of this work begins with the scope of a question. An observation may be intriguing but difficult to measure reliably; a comparison may be feasible while remaining unable to support the conclusion a student hopes to draw. I help students consider those distinctions while they retain responsibility for their own investigation.

Our early conversations often concern what a variable or outcome would mean in practice, which sources or measurements are available, and what can realistically be completed. As students begin collecting and interpreting evidence, the questions change. We may need to reconsider a procedure, account for missing data, or narrow a claim. I ask students to keep track of those decisions so that the eventual report can explain how the investigation developed.

I developed Research Methods Studio to help students organize the work between those conversations. The tool offers a place to develop questions and methods, record decisions, and return to earlier plans. That record gives us something specific to examine together, rather than treating the final proposal or presentation as the only evidence of their thinking.
