Teaching science

In biology and chemistry, students sometimes write answers containing the expected terms or calculations while leaving a causal relationship unexplained. An AP Biology response may name ATP and NADPH without explaining their production, while an equilibrium calculation can be correct even when its interpretation is not. These differences guide the questions I ask about a student's understanding of the mechanism or model.

Interpreting those answers becomes more complicated when a diagram or conversation reveals reasoning left implicit in writing. The sections below consider related decisions in assessment, curriculum design, and mentorship of independent student research.

Classroom action research and assessment

Documented AP Biology assessment inquiry · 28 students

In an AP Biology assessment on the effects of bentazon, some explanations identified outcomes without developing the pathway through photosynthetic electron transport, ATP, NADPH, and carbon fixation. The difference between an omitted connection and an incorrect one affected the question I needed to ask next. If a student left out the proton gradient, I needed to examine whether they could explain its role in ATP production. A claim that the gradient became stronger under inhibition required examining the proposed direction of change.

After feedback and correction, I compared students' original answers with independent reconstructions of the same mechanism. The AP Biology teaching case develops the scientific pathway, a proposed lesson sequence, and an explanation task informed by these difficulties. The action research account examines the linked student records, their classifications, and the limits of the conclusions. The proposed follow-up lessons and questions have not been evaluated.

Curriculum and assessment planning

When planning a unit, I begin with the explanation, calculation, or investigation I hope students will be able to undertake. Working backward from that task helps me identify the ideas they will need to connect and the assumptions they may need to examine. I can then plan activities that make those relationships available for discussion before students are asked to explain them independently.

To examine whether those connections are developing, I plan questions that ask students to explain what their answers mean. A correct reaction-quotient calculation, for instance, may accompany an incorrect account of equilibrium, just as a labelled photosynthesis diagram may identify ATP and NADPH without connecting their production to electron transport. In each case, the follow-up would depend on the relationship the student had expressed or left unclear.

I try to make room in the timetable for students to explain their reasoning and use feedback in later work. Laboratory investigations also need time for preparation, measurement, and consideration of uncertainty. Balancing these activities within an Advanced Placement course is part of my planning.

Questions I check before a sequence

  1. Which relationships must be explained, beyond recalling terms?
  2. What prior knowledge and representations will students need?
  3. What would an incomplete explanation look like?
  4. What evidence can I collect before the next major assessment?
  5. Where can students question the task or the model itself?

Mentoring independent student research

Alongside my science courses, I supervise secondary students completing capstone and independent research, and I review proposals and presentations as a faculty member. These projects give students responsibility for choosing questions, methods, and the claims they can defend. I support them as they work through those decisions, including how to keep an investigation feasible with the time and resources available.

I usually begin by asking what a student hopes to understand and what the proposed observations or measurements could establish. That conversation leads into literature, design, analysis, and the claims they may eventually be able to defend. As the project develops, I want students to make their reasoning visible, including when they recognize a limitation and decide to change course.

Some of these decisions also appear in Research Methods Studio, which gives students a structured way to return to a question as their evidence and methods develop. In supervision, we can then spend more time on the particular difficulties that arise from the student's own investigation.
