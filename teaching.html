Teaching science

I teach Advanced Placement Biology, Advanced Placement Chemistry, and life science at an English-medium school in South Korea. A good part of my preparation goes into deciding what students will have in front of them when they meet a difficult idea, and what they can return to afterward.

Here are some of the ordinary parts of that work, including a sample activity you can read, use, or adapt.

One life science lesson

In a 50-minute life science class, I often use the following sequence. The short reading gives us time to work through scientific vocabulary together. Students have guided notes and a time-lapse explanation to use while preparing at home or reviewing afterward. This is one classroom routine, and it does not describe every AP lesson or laboratory period.

  1. 15 min
    Knowledge check

    Return to earlier concepts through a few questions. Responses help me decide what to clarify before we continue.

  2. 15 min
    Read and talk through a science text

    Read aloud together, pause for unfamiliar terms, and connect the language to diagrams or examples.

  3. 5 min
    Break

    Make a little room to reset before the last activity.

  4. 15 min
    Project or investigation

    Use the remaining time to examine evidence, improve a model, or continue a question from an earlier lesson.

A sample activity you can use

I've prepared a short osmosis activity for this site. It begins with a small table of potato-mass measurements, then asks students to describe the pattern, propose a mechanism, and consider what the measurements leave uncertain.

The numbers are illustrative practice data. This is a shareable activity, with teacher notes and a print-friendly version, and it is not presented as a record of a particular class.

A question to ask after looking at the table

What would need to happen at a cell membrane for the measured mass to change?

The question moves from describing the numbers toward an explanation students can examine.

Questions to ask after an explanation

In the osmosis activity, a student might identify which potato sample gained mass without explaining how water moved through the tissue. I could ask the student to mark the direction of water movement on a drawing and explain what makes that direction reasonable.

If a student explains the movement convincingly, I could ask what the mass measurements actually show and which parts of the mechanism we are inferring. Those follow-up questions help separate an observed pattern from an explanation of it. The original prompt and diagram still need checking, especially if their wording makes the expected response difficult to express.

Supporting student investigations

When students design their own research, we spend time checking whether a question is manageable with the materials, time, and permissions available. We also look at what a proposed measurement can tell us. Sometimes the most useful next step is to narrow a question or make one comparison more precise.

I have built a separate Research Methods Studio for students who want more structured help with those decisions. My classroom inquiry documents a different part of my teaching, following assessment corrections and scientific explanations across time.
