Teaching science

I teach subjects where a short answer often rests on a long chain of reasoning. A student can know the names of the molecules in a process or complete a calculation and still need time to work out how the parts connect.

I am interested in what our explanations, diagrams, laboratory work, and assessment questions ask students to make visible. I also care about whose questions we make room for, which scientific assumptions a class can examine, and how students participate in deciding what deserves further investigation.

I've developed two curriculum studies here. The Biology example connects human genetic variation with evolution and careful interpretation of evidence. The Chemistry example examines equilibrium and environmental questions. Both are prospective teaching designs. A separate account documents what I actually examined in classroom assessment.

Planning across a unit

I usually begin by identifying the relationships that a student must be able to explain at the end of a sequence. In the haemoglobin study, this means moving from a specific protein variant through genotype-dependent consequences to population change. In equilibrium, it means understanding why a mixture changes composition when its reaction quotient differs from its equilibrium constant.

The next question is what evidence would make those relationships visible. A labelled diagram could reveal the location of a process, while a written explanation can show how a student connects one step to the next. A numerical result can be correct even when the explanation of what the number means is incomplete. Each representation offers a different opportunity to examine the science.

Time is part of the design. A laboratory investigation requires preparation, safe procedures, data collection, and time to examine the results. An assessment correction needs space to consider feedback and try a related explanation again. I try to reserve opportunities to revisit a question after its first appearance, while recognizing that AP coursework also places demands on pacing and examination preparation.

Questions I check before a sequence

  1. Which relationships must be explained, beyond recalling terms?
  2. What prior knowledge and representations will students need?
  3. What would an incomplete explanation look like?
  4. What evidence can I collect before the next major assessment?
  5. Where can students question the task or the model itself?

The two curriculum sequences are proposed designs. The classroom assessment case documents work I carried out and the questions that arose from it.

What a student's response allows me to infer

In my AP Biology action research, I examined what students could articulate under different assessment conditions. Their initial answers, feedback-assisted reflections, independent reconstructions, and later explanations did not always tell the same story. That made the wording of a task and the resources available during it central to how I interpreted the response.

After reviewing the earlier records, I added an individual reconstruction of the photosynthetic mechanism following correction. Comparing it with the original answer revealed additional relationships in some cases and omissions in others. The comparison suggested more specific questions I could ask, while leaving the effects of those follow-ups untested. The teaching case examines the actual task and coding criteria. The action research account examines the linked evidence and its limitations.

Black and Wiliam (2009) connect formative assessment with the decisions teachers and learners make from evidence. I am still working out how to make those decisions proportionate to what a particular response can support. A missing step on paper can warrant another question without establishing everything the student understands.

What questions can change the lesson?

In an advanced course, students need access to disciplinary explanations and opportunities to test their limits. The haemoglobin curriculum study asks what a population model can establish and where genetic claims become overgeneralizations. The chemistry study asks what additional measurements would be needed before attributing nitrogen concentrations to a particular source. I would want a question raised by a student to make a difference to the next source, model, or measurement the class considers.

I am learning from justice-centred science education scholarship about the responsibilities involved in such work. Morales-Doyle (2017) documents advanced chemistry teaching connected to a community environmental concern. My proposed materials have not established that kind of collaboration. The case studies offer a way to examine scientific and curricular decisions carefully while keeping that distinction visible.
