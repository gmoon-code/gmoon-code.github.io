Teaching science

In biology and chemistry, I pay close attention to how students connect scientific ideas. A response can include the correct terms or numerical answer while leaving the process behind it difficult to follow. I read for those connections because they help me decide what I understand about the student's reasoning and what I still need to ask.

Students work through scientific questions in diagrams, calculations, discussions, and writing, and a relationship that is clear in one form may remain unstated in another. I try to examine what each task has allowed students to express before deciding what their responses mean. Their questions also bring me back to explanations I have taught many times. When a student notices a difficulty I have overlooked, we have a reason to look more closely at the science together.

I have included an AP Biology assessment inquiry, two proposed curriculum sequences, and examples from my work with independent student researchers. The detailed pages make the original questions and teaching decisions available for closer examination.

Classroom action research and assessment

Documented AP Biology assessment inquiry · 28 students

My AP Biology action research began with a difficulty I encountered while reading students' corrections. A revised answer could contain a sound explanation, yet the correction alone did not tell me which causal relationships the student could explain without looking back at feedback. I followed their assessment answers, reflections, and later work to examine what each record made visible about their reasoning.

After the Unit 3 photosynthesis assessment, I added an individual reconstruction of the same mechanism, completed without reference materials. Among ten paired records, some reconstructions developed connections absent from the original answer, while others omitted relationships that had appeared earlier. Those differences helped me formulate more specific questions for students. The prompts differed, and the reconstruction followed feedback, so the comparison could guide my next questions without establishing how students' understanding had changed. The teaching case develops the biological pathway and proposed instruction, while the action research account examines the records in greater detail.

The paired responses also led me to reconsider how I respond when a student leaves out a causal step. An incomplete explanation does not establish whether the student is uncertain about the relationship or has simply left it unstated. Asking them to explain the connection aloud or through a diagram can help me distinguish those possibilities and decide what feedback would be useful. In this way, evidence from an assessment can guide the next teaching decision, a central concern of formative assessment (Black & Wiliam, 2009).

Curriculum and assessment planning

When planning a unit, I begin with the explanation, calculation, or investigation I hope students will be able to undertake. Working backward from that task helps me identify the ideas they will need to connect and the assumptions they may need to examine. I can then plan activities that make those relationships available for discussion before students are asked to explain them independently.

As students work through the sequence, I look for the different kinds of evidence their responses provide. A correct calculation may accompany a mistaken interpretation, while a labelled diagram may locate structures without showing how they interact. These distinctions help me decide whether to ask another question, invite a different representation, or return to the underlying science.

Planning also has to work within a school timetable. A laboratory investigation needs enough time for preparation, measurement, and consideration of uncertainty, and students need a chance to use feedback in later work. I try to protect room for those activities while meeting the demands of the Advanced Placement course.

Questions I check before a sequence

  1. Which relationships must be explained, beyond recalling terms?
  2. What prior knowledge and representations will students need?
  3. What would an incomplete explanation look like?
  4. What evidence can I collect before the next major assessment?
  5. Where can students question the task or the model itself?

Mentoring independent student research

Alongside my science courses, I supervise secondary students completing capstone and independent research, and I review proposals and presentations as a faculty member. These projects give students responsibility for choosing questions, methods, and the claims they can defend. I support them as they work through those decisions, including how to keep an investigation feasible with the time and resources available.

I usually begin by asking what a student hopes to understand and what the proposed observations or measurements could establish. That conversation leads into literature, design, analysis, and the claims they may eventually be able to defend. As the project develops, I want students to make their reasoning visible, including when they recognize a limitation and decide to change course.

Some of these decisions also appear in Research Methods Studio, which gives students a structured way to return to a question as their evidence and methods develop. In supervision, we can then spend more time on the particular difficulties that arise from the student's own investigation.

Additional teaching materials

Air quality and school decision-making

Cross-disciplinary lesson design · Developed for graduate coursework · Not evaluated in a classroom

I designed a lesson in which students would examine local PM2.5 measurements from AirKorea and consider how a school might respond on days with high pollution. The proposed task brings the chemistry of particulate mixtures and filtration into conversation with respiratory biology. Students would compare possible responses while attending to uncertainty, feasibility, and the limits of the available measurements. They would then prepare an action brief explaining what the evidence supports and what the school would still need to know.

Osmosis and mass-change data

The osmosis activity uses hypothetical mass-change data for practice with percentage calculations and explanations of water movement. Students also consider which aspects of membrane transport the measurements can support and which require further evidence.
