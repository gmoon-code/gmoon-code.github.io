Teaching science

In biology and chemistry, I often look closely at the relationships students include in their explanations. A response may use the correct scientific terms or reach the correct numerical answer while leaving part of the reasoning unclear. I try to understand what the student has expressed before deciding which question to ask next.

My students use diagrams, calculations, discussion, and writing to develop scientific ideas. These forms of expression offer different evidence, and I have become more careful about the conclusions I draw from any one response. Their questions sometimes return me to biological and chemical explanations I thought I knew well. I want to maintain the scientific demands of the lesson while considering how students can develop and communicate their reasoning.

The pages below include an AP Biology assessment inquiry that changed the questions I asked after correction, two curriculum sequences I have designed, and examples of how I support students as they develop their own research.

Classroom action research and assessment

Documented AP Biology assessment inquiry · 28 students

In my AP Biology action research, I examined what students could articulate under different assessment conditions. Their initial answers, feedback-assisted reflections, independent reconstructions, and later explanations did not always tell the same story. That made the wording of a task and the resources available during it central to how I interpreted the response.

After reviewing the earlier records, I added an individual reconstruction of the photosynthetic mechanism following correction. Comparing it with the original answer revealed additional relationships in some cases and omissions in others. The comparison suggested more specific questions I could ask, while leaving the effects of those follow-ups untested. The teaching case examines the actual task and coding criteria. The action research account examines the linked evidence and its limitations.

Black and Wiliam (2009) connect formative assessment with the decisions teachers and learners make from evidence. I try to keep my response proportionate to the evidence in front of me. When a student leaves out a causal step, I can ask them to explain it again or in another form before deciding what help would be useful.

Curriculum and assessment planning

When planning a unit, I consider the explanation, calculation, or investigation students will eventually complete. I work back through the knowledge and relationships they will need, including points where an inference might be plausible but incomplete. This helps me decide which questions, representations, and activities to prepare.

I also consider what the task allows me to infer. A correct numerical answer may accompany incorrect reasoning, and a diagram may show the position of structures while leaving their relationships unexplained. I use that information to decide whether students need another question, a different representation, or further instruction.

Practical constraints also shape the sequence. Laboratory work requires safe preparation, adequate time for measurements and analysis, and opportunities to consider uncertainty. Revision after feedback requires a new occasion to use the idea. In Advanced Placement courses, I weigh these needs against the pacing of the curriculum and assessment schedule.

Questions I check before a sequence

  1. Which relationships must be explained, beyond recalling terms?
  2. What prior knowledge and representations will students need?
  3. What would an incomplete explanation look like?
  4. What evidence can I collect before the next major assessment?
  5. Where can students question the task or the model itself?

Mentoring independent student research

Alongside my science courses, I supervise secondary students undertaking capstone and independent research and serve as a faculty reviewer of research proposals and presentations. Students need to make decisions that a conventional classroom investigation may already have made for them. Their questions must be narrow enough to investigate, their methods must produce interpretable evidence, and their conclusions must remain within what the work can establish.

My supervision includes research-question development, literature review, experimental design, data analysis, scientific writing, and presentation. I want students to make and defend these decisions themselves, so our conversations often begin with what they hope to learn and what their proposed measurements would actually tell them. We examine the limitations of the design and how they could explain their choices to another reader. A promising topic can still require substantial revision before it becomes a workable study.

I also built Research Methods Studio to give students a way to revisit earlier decisions as their research develops. In person, those decisions lead to different conversations for different projects.

Additional teaching materials

Air quality and school decision-making

Cross-disciplinary lesson design · Developed for graduate coursework · Not evaluated in a classroom

I designed a lesson in which students would use local PM2.5 measurements from AirKorea to consider what a school should do on high-pollution days. The planned task connects the chemistry of airborne particle mixtures and filtration with respiratory biology. Students would compare response options using the available evidence, feasibility, and uncertainty, then prepare a short school action brief. The task ends with students explaining which school actions their evidence supports and where additional measurements would be needed.

Osmosis and mass-change data

A separate biology osmosis exercise uses an illustrative mass-change table to practise interpreting measurements, explaining water movement, and identifying what the data cannot establish. The example data are illustrative, giving students a chance to distinguish what a measured change establishes from what remains uncertain.
