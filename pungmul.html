Music, movement, and community

Pungmul (풍물)

I joined a queer pungmul ensemble to learn a musical tradition I had long wanted to play. What has stayed with me is how much a performance asks of everyone involved. The sound changes as we listen to one another, respond to cues, and move together. Playing has also made me curious about the many places this music has occupied in Korean life, from village gatherings to contemporary performances and public demonstrations.

Performances with our ensemble

We have played at a formal concert, Seoul Pride, and smaller events supporting queer communities. These settings have different audiences and surroundings, so each performance feels a little different even when some of the rhythms are familiar. I especially enjoy the moments when the music brings performers and listeners into the same shared space.

Listening to the ensemble

Pungmul brings percussion, dance, and movement together. The related term nongak (농악) is widely used in heritage descriptions, although local traditions have their own names and practices. Depending on the setting, a performance may include procession, dramatic characters, ritual elements, or elaborate formations. The National Institute of Korean History's introduction to nongak provides a useful account of these elements.

For someone watching pungmul for the first time, following the instruments offers a way into the performance. The small gong, kkwaenggwari (꽹과리), often carries leading cues, while the large jing (징) sounds across longer intervals. The hourglass-shaped janggu (장구) and barrel-shaped buk (북) develop the rhythms between them. Smaller drums, wind instruments, dancers, and other performers may contribute as well. As musicians change positions or move into another passage, the sound becomes inseparable from what happens across the group.

A tradition with many histories

Accounts of nongak describe music associated with agricultural work, village rites, seasonal celebrations, and gatherings that raised funds for communal needs. These occasions involved participation by neighbours as well as performers. There is considerable variation across regions and villages, including differences in rhythms, instruments, formations, and costumes. UNESCO's description of nongak, which was inscribed on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2014, documents this variety.

The settings have continued to change. Village performance traditions have developed alongside travelling troupes and staged productions. In the late 1970s, samulnori brought four percussion instruments into a concert-centred format that drew on nongak rhythms. These developments offer different ways of hearing and presenting related musical material, and they have not replaced the regional practices from which much of the music draws. The National Institute of Korean History traces several of these changes.

Playing in public

Pungmul also has a history within South Korea's struggles for democracy. During the 1970s and 1980s, student and cultural groups used pungmul and other forms of communal performance in activities connected to labour and farmers' movements. Heung-Ju Park's study of Seoul's pungmul movement between 1980 and 1987 examines how performers drew on village ritual and collective participation while responding to contemporary political concerns. The article also considers tensions between creating political performances and sustaining an understanding of the traditions as lived communal practices.

Our ensemble's performances at Pride and other public gatherings belong to a different moment. Learning about the earlier movement has helped me appreciate that pungmul has been played for many reasons by people in very different circumstances. For me, performing with other queer musicians is both a chance to continue learning the music and a way to stand alongside people with whom I feel at home. I am grateful for the space we make together, and for the chance to share some of that music with people who stop to listen.
