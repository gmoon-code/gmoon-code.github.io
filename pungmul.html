Music, movement, and community

Pungmul (풍물)

I play pungmul with a queer ensemble in South Korea. Learning the music has also made me curious about its history and the different places it has been performed. Here I bring together photographs of our ensemble with a closer look at the instruments, regional traditions, and changing roles of pungmul in Korean public life.

Listening to the ensemble

Pungmul brings percussion, dance, and movement together. The related term nongak (농악) is widely used in heritage descriptions, while local traditions have their own names and practices. Depending on the setting, a performance may include processions, dramatic characters, ritual elements, or elaborate formations (National Institute of Korean History, n.d.).

The instruments have different roles within the ensemble. The small gong, kkwaenggwari (꽹과리), often carries leading cues, while the large jing (징) sounds across longer intervals. The hourglass-shaped janggu (장구) and barrel-shaped buk (북) support interlocking rhythmic patterns. Depending on the tradition and performance, other instruments and dancers may join the group, with the music accompanying its movements and changing formations.

A tradition with many histories

Accounts of nongak describe music associated with agricultural work, village rites, seasonal celebrations, and gatherings that raised funds for communal needs. These occasions involved participation by neighbours as well as performers. Regional traditions differ in their rhythms, instruments, formations, and costumes, as described in UNESCO's 2014 heritage record for nongak (UNESCO, 2014).

The settings have continued to change. Village performance traditions have developed alongside travelling troupes and staged productions. In 1978, samulnori emerged as a staged performance of rearranged nongak rhythms played with four percussion instruments. Its early form differed from the standing ensembles and processions often associated with nongak (National Folk Museum of Korea, n.d.). The development opened another way of presenting related music, while regional practices continued to be performed and taught (National Institute of Korean History, n.d.).

Pungmul and social movements

Pungmul also has a history within South Korea's struggles for democracy. During the 1980s, some university performers drew on the forms of village rituals to respond to political concerns, informed by their experiences in labour and farmers' movements. Their performances brought these traditions into new settings, although questions remained about how the political work related to the everyday communal practices from which it drew (Park, 2014).
